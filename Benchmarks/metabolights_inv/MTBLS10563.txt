Study Identifier	MTBLS10563
Study Title	Multi-kingdom gut microbiota dysbiosis is associated with the development of pulmonary arterial hypertension
Study Description	<p>Gut microbiota dysbiosis has been implicated in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). However, most studies have focused on the bacterial community and the exact role and mechanisms of multi-kingdom gut microbiota, including bacteria, archaea and fungi in PAH remains largely unclear. Idiopathic PAH (IPAH) patients exhibited distinct gut microbiota profiles with altered bacterial, archaeal and fungal compositions compared to healthy controls. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from IPAH patients or monocrotaline (MCT)-induced PAH rats to antibiotic treated rats induced PAH phenotypes, including increased right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) and pulmonary vascular remodeling. Conversely, FMT from normal rats to MCT-PAH rats ameliorated PAH symptoms and reversed multi-kingdom gut microbiota dysbiosis. Metabolomics revealed significant alterations in plasma metabolites. Our findings established a causal link between multi-kingdom gut microbiota dysbiosis and PAH, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of FMT in reversing PAH phenotypes. More importantly, in addition to gut bacteria, gut archaeal and fungal communities also significantly correlate with PAH pathogenesis, highlighting their indispensable role in the gut.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The study followed ethical standards established by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University, Beijing, China (AEEI-2022-011), and adhered to current NIH guidelines for animal welfare and experimental protocols. Rats were randomly divided into two groups: the CON group (n=6) receiving a solvent injection and the MCT group (n=12) receiving an MCT injection. Subsequently, the MCT-induced PAH rats were further randomized into the MCT group and the normal-transplant microbiota (NTM) group, with the NTM group receiving fecal microbiota transplantation from the CON group (n=6). Fresh fecal samples (10 g) were collected daily from the CON group, mixed with 20 mL of sterile saline at 37 °C, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected.&nbsp;OD values of the supernatant were measured at 620 nm. Each rat in the NTM group received gastric gavage of fecal supernatant containing 2 x 10^9 fecal microbiota from the CON group for 21 consecutive days.</p>	<p>Plasma samples of rats were stored at -80 °C until analysis. Frozen cryovials were thawed, vortexed and centrifuged for 10 min at 2000 rpm, 4 °C.&nbsp;Quality control samples were prepared by pooling equal volume of aliquots from all the samples (10 μL) to a fresh Eppendorf tube and mixing with methanol (4x the volume of total sample in Eppendorf tube). For sample preparation, 100 μL of sample was added to a fresh Eppendorf tube and mixed with 400 μL methanol. Samples and quality control samples were then incubated for 1 h at -20 °C, vortexed and then centrifuged for 20 min at 14,000 rpm, 4 °C. Supernatant was collected in fresh Eppendorf tubes and vacuum dried. Dried samples were stored at -80 °C until analysis. Samples were dissolved in 100 µL of solvent containing 50% methanol and 0.1% formic acid.</p>	<p>Plasma samples were processed using methanol/acetonitrile, dried, re-dissolved and centrifuged. Separation was achieved via hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column, employing a gradient of ammonium acetate/ammonium hydroxide in water and acetonitrile.</p>	<p>The separated compounds were analyzed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography quadrupole time-of-flight (UHPLC-Q-TOF) mass spectrometry. The data acquisition followed standard mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (MS/MS) protocols, including compound detection and quantification.</p>	<p>After mass spectrometry data collection, data processing included annotation using the Collection of Algorithms for MEtabolite pRofile Annotation (CAMERA). Compound identification was performed using an in-house database for accurate metabolite profiling.</p>	<p>Metabolites identification search was performed using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB2016) within a mass error of 10 ppm. Identifications matched with fragmentation database were labeled as MSI level 2 and remaining annotations were labeled as MSI level 3</p>

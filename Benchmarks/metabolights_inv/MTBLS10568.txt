Study Title	EFFECT OF DIFFERENT POOLED QC SAMPLES ON DATA QUALITY DURING AN INTER-BATCH EXPERIMENT IN UNTARGETED UHPLC-HRMS ANALYSIS ON TWO DIFFERENT MS PLATFORMS
Study Description	Quality control (QC) samples are commonly used in metabolomics approaches for three main reasons: (i) the initial conditioning of the column; (ii) the correction of analytical drift especially between batches; and (iii) the evaluation of measurement precision. In practice there are several ways to prepare and conserve QC samples. The most common in untargeted metabolomics is to pool samples after or before extraction, in order to obtain pooled QC samples accounting respectively for analytical variance or for both analytical and sample preparation variances. In this study, focusing on untargeted analysis of tea (Camellia sinensis) leaves, we compared three ways of preparing pooled QC samples (two usual and one unusual QC sample preparations) and their efficiency to improve data quality in terms of inter-batch correction, measurement precision, and VIP candidates selection on datasets obtained using two mass spectrometry (MS) technologies (Orbitrap and time of flight (QToF)). We also investigated the effect of data processing modalities, based on the different QC preparations, on data loss and on the global structure of the datasets. Generally, our results show that usual QC sample preparation leads to comparable datasets quality in terms of precision and dispersion in both MS instruments. They also show that QC preparation is crucial for VIP selection, in fact, up to 54% of biomarkers candidates are specific of the QC preparation type used for data processing.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Commercial tea leaves (Camellia sinensis) samples were purchased from “Palais des Thés” (Paris, France). We compared three types of tea coming from China: the green tea ‘Long Jing Imperial’ (Zhejiang region), the oolong tea ‘Osmanthus d’Or Huang Jin Gui’ (Fujian region), and the black tea ‘Grand Yunnan Imperial’ (Yunnan region). Tea leaves were ground using a porcelain mortar and pestle prior storage at 4 °C. A tea mix of an equal mass of the ground three tea types was also prepared for quality control purpose and stored at 4 °C.</p>	<p>Samples were extracted using a method based on previous work (Cladière et al. 2018): 1 g of each tea sample was weighted in a 15 mL centrifuge tube and 5 mL of an ACN/MeOH (90/10 v/v) mixture acidified with 0.1% FA were added to each sample for extraction. Tubes were then agitated upside-down on an agitator plate for 1 h, and centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min. Then 2 mL of the supernatant were collected and evaporated to dryness under a gentle nitrogen stream at 30 °C. The extract was reconstituted in 0.4 mL of ACN acidified with 0.1% FA and completed by 1.6 mL of water acidified with 0.1% FA, leading to a total volume of 2 mL. The reconstituted extract was then diluted by 20 in ACN/water acidified (20/80 v/v). Then, 0.45 mL was collected and filtered at 0.2 μm using a syringeless filter vial (mini-uniprep G2, Whatman) before analysis. All extractions were performed in triplicate. A blank sample was prepared for each extraction batch going through all the extraction step but without adding tea on the extraction solvent in the first step.</p><p>Three different types of QC preparations were made with an increasing variability to compare their performances: </p><p>1.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pooled extract QC samples (QCe): made by pooling together 0.5 mL of all final extracts of the three tea types considered in both analytical sequences; one single QCe was prepared and aliquots were stored at -20 °C between the two analytical sequences.</p><p>2.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pooled leaves QC samples (QCf): made by extracting 1g of the mix of the three tea types. QCf were prepared with the extraction of the tea samples following the same protocol for each sequence on the day before LC-MS analysis. For each sequence the tea mix was extracted in duplicates, and the two extracts were pooled together before filtering in order to obtain the final QCf. This QCf type is planned to be used as Long-term QC sample, it shows both technical and sample preparation variability so it is expected to be more variable than QCe type.</p><p>3.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Batch QC samples (QCb): made by pooling together 0.5 mL of all final extracts considered in each analytical sequence; green + oolong tea extracts for QCb injected in batch 1, and oolong + black tea extracts for QCb injected in batch 2. This QC type is expected to present more variability as QCb injected in batch 1 is different from the one injected in batch 2.</p><p>Aliquots of 0.45 mL of each QC type were sampled and filtered using a syringeless filter vial.</p>	<p>Please update this protocol descriptionFor each platform, the injection volume was 5 μL and separation was achieved on a C18-PFP column (150 × 2.1 mm, 2 μm particles diameter, ACE supplied by AIT France). The same chromatographic gradients, one in positive ionisation&nbsp;mode and one in negative ionisation mode, were used on both instruments as described in previous work (Delaporte, Cladière, et Camel 2019). </p>	<p>All samples were analysed on two UHPLC-HRMS platforms. The first is a Waters® Acquity UPLC® H-Class system coupled to a Waters® high resolution Time of Flight mass spectrometer Xevo® G2-S QToF operated in centroid mode (resolution of 30,000 FWHM at m/z 200, m/z range from 60 to 800, 10 scans/sec) using an electrospray ion source (ESI). The second is a Thermo Scientific UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system coupled to a QExactiveTM Orbitrap mass spectrometer operated in centroid mode (resolution of 70,000 FWHM at m/z 200, m/z range from 60 to 800) with a heated electrospray ion source (HESI). For the Orbitrap MS method “AGC target” and “Maximum IT” parameters were set to 106 and 200 msec respectively. </p>	<p>For both instruments, data were acquired in RAW format. They were then converted to “.mzML” using the MSConvertGUI software (ProteoWizard) (Chambers et al. 2012) in order to upload and process data using Galaxy Workflow4Metabolomics platform (Giacomoni et al. 2015).</p>	<p>No identification was performed but standards were used for development so they are added to this study for comparison.</p>

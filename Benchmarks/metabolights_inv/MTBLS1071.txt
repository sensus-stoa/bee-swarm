Study Identifier	MTBLS1071
Study Title	Metabolomic fingerprinting of bull spermatozoa for identification of fertility signature metabolites
Study Description	The objective of the study was to identify the fertility-associated metabolites in bovine spermatozoa using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Six Holstein Friesian crossbred bulls (three high-fertile and three low-fertile bulls) were the experimental animals. Sperm proteins were isolated and protein-normalized samples were processed for metabolite extraction and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis. Mass spectrometry data were processed using iMETQ software and metabolites were identified using Human Metabolome DataBase while, Metaboanalyst 4.0 tool was used for statistical and pathway analysis. A total of 3,704 metabolites belonging to various chemical classes were identified in bull spermatozoa. After sorting out exogenous metabolites, 56 metabolites were observed common to both the groups while 44 and 35 metabolites were found unique to high- and low-fertile spermatozoa, respectively. Among the common metabolites, concentrations of 19 metabolites were higher in high-fertile compared to low-fertile spermatozoa (fold change > 1.00). Spermatozoa metabolites with variable importance in projections score of more than 1.5 included hypotaurine, d-cysteine, selenocystine. In addition, metabolites such as spermine and l-cysteine were identified exclusively in high-fertile spermatozoa. Collectively, the present study established the metabolic profile of bovine spermatozoa and identified the metabolomic differences between spermatozoa from high- and low-fertile bulls. Among the sperm metabolites, hypotaurine, selenocysteine, l-malic acid, d-cysteine, and chondroitin 4-sulfate hold the potential to be recognized as fertility-associated metabolites.

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Study population and classification</strong></p><p><strong>Holstein Friesian crossbred bulls </strong>(Holstein Friesian x Tharparkar) with contrasting <em>in vivo</em> fertility were selected as the experimental animals. Field CR for each bull was calculated as the proportion of pregnant animals to the total number of inseminations done per bull (up to 3rd insemination). Further, field CR were adjusted to non-genetic parameters according to the procedure described in the previously published paper<strong>[1]</strong>. The mean CR of <strong>high-fertile</strong> <strong>bulls </strong>was 42.96% while, the mean CR of <strong>low- fertile</strong> <strong>bulls </strong>was 25.39%, and the difference was significant (p &lt; .01). The Animal Ethics committee of ICAR National Dairy Research Institute approved the present study (CPCSEA/IAEC/LA/SRS-ICAR-NDRI-2017/No.07) and the study was performed as per the guidelines.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Purification of spermatozoa</strong></p><p>Spermatozoa prepared from the 3 different freezing operations, that is, 3 ejaculates per <strong>bull </strong>(<strong>high</strong>- and <strong>low</strong>-fertile each) were collected and purified as per the procedure described in the previously published paper<strong>[2]</strong>. Briefly, <strong>spermatozoa </strong>were purified using 90-45% dis-continuous Percoll gradient centrifugation by adding 0.9 mL of 45% of Percoll fractionin Sperm TALP over 0.2 mL of 90% fraction in 1.5 mL eppendorf tube (Axygen). <strong>Semen </strong>was carefully layered over the top of prepared Percoll gradient fraction. <strong>Spermatozoa </strong>were then pelleted by centrifugation at 950 x g for 15 min at room temperature and <strong>sperm </strong>pellet was washed 3 times using phosphate buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM sodium chloride; 2.7 mM potassium chloride; 10 mM sodium phosphate dibasic; 1.8 mM potassium dihydrogen phosphate; pH 7.4) at same speed and samples were stored at -80 °C until further processing.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Singh RK, Kumaresan A, Chhillar S, Rajak SK, Tripathi UK, Nayak S, Datta TK, Mohanty TK, Malhotra R. Identification of suitable combinations of in&nbsp;vitro sperm-function test for the prediction of fertility in buffalo bull. Theriogenology. 2016 Dec;86(9):2263-2271.e1. doi: 10.1016/j.theriogenology.2016.07.022. Epub 2016 Jul 26. PMID: 27555524</p><p><strong>[2] </strong>Peddinti D, Nanduri B, Kaya A, Feugang JM, Burgess SC, Memili E. Comprehensive proteomic analysis of bovine spermatozoa of varying fertility rates and identification of biomarkers associated with fertility. BMC Syst Biol. 2008 Feb 22;2:19. doi:10.1186/1752-0509-2-19. PMID:18294385</p>	<p class='ql-align-justify'>Stored&nbsp;spermatozoa&nbsp;samples from all individual animals were then thawed on ice and suspended in 500 μL of ice-cold PBS and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C (MicroCL 21R; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The supernatant was then poured off and&nbsp;sperm&nbsp;pellet was resuspended in 100 μL MilliQ water and sonicated (Qsonica Sonicator) for 2.5 min at 4 °C, repeated 5 times with 5 s intervals with 20% amplitude using 2 mm probe.&nbsp;Spermatozoa&nbsp;metabolites were extracted as described in the previously published paper with little modifications<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, 100 μg of protein equivalent sample was taken for metabolite extraction. Pooled sample was added with LC/MS grade water (Merck), methanol (Merck) and acetonitrile (JT Baker, Phillipsburg, NJ) in the proportion of 1:2:2 (i.e., v/v/v). After vertexing for (1 min), the samples were then subjected to water bath sonication for 10 min and centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 x g (at 4 °C). Supernatant was collected and aliquoted in 2 tubes and further processed for drying in speedVac for 1 h (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stored in -80 °C before proceeding for LC-MS/MS analysis.</p><p class='ql-align-justify'><br></p><p class='ql-align-justify'><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p class='ql-align-justify'><strong>[1] </strong>Zhao K, Zhang J, Xu Z, Xu Y, Xu A, Chen W, Miao C, Liu S, Wang Z, Jia R. Metabolomic Profiling of Human Spermatozoa in Idiopathic Asthenozoospermia Patients Using Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry. Biomed Res Int. 2018 Feb 28;2018:8327506. doi:10.1155/2018/8327506. PMID:29682560</p>	<p>The samples were then injected on to C18 Zorbax Rapid Resolution High Definition (RRHD) analytical columns (20 x 150 mm, 1.8 μm particle size) with the help of programmable autosampler. Analyst software version 1.6.3 with the Analyst Device Driver was used to set the parameters for the analysis. The separation of the metabolites was carried out using a 30 min LC method. Solvent A (0.1% formic acid in MilliQ water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 90% acetonitrile) were used for analysis with a standard flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Elution of metabolites was carried out using following gradient; 2% B for t=0 min; 30% B for t=10 min; 60% B for t=17 min; 95% B for t=22-26 min and 2% B for t=27.5-30 min.</p>	<p>The LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a QTRAP 6500 mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) coupled with 1290 infinity II liquid chromatography system (Agilent Technologies, USA).&nbsp;The mass spectrometry data acquisition was carried out with Information Dependent Acquisition (IDA) method in low mass mode. The IDA method was built using the Enhanced Mass Spectra (EMS) to enhanced product ion (EPI) modes. The top 5 spectra from the EMS mode were used for analysis in the EPI (MS/MS) mode; using high energy CID i.e. collisionally induced dissociation. The metabolite data was acquired in both positive and negative polarities at 4500 V and -4500 V respectively, with a probe temperature of 450 °C. The compound parameters were set at a declustering potential (DP) of 75 V, collision energy (CE) of 45 V. The data was acquired in triplicates.</p>	<p>LC-MS/MS raw data format ‘.<strong>wiff</strong>’ files from <strong>Analyst </strong>software was converted to ‘.<strong>mzML</strong>’ using <strong>MSConvert[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Chambers SM, Qi Y, Mica Y, Lee G, Zhang XJ, Niu L, Bilsland J, Cao L, Stevens E, Whiting P, Shi SH, Studer L. Combined small-molecule inhibition accelerates developmental timing and converts human pluripotent stem cells into nociceptors. Nat Biotechnol. 2012 Jul 1;30(7):715-20. doi:10.1038/nbt.2249. PMID:22750882.</p><p><br></p>	<p>Data processing was carried out using <strong>iMETQ </strong>software<strong>[1].&nbsp;</strong></p><p><br></p><p>The aligned peak list across samples, were used for metabolite identification using Human Metabolome DataBase (<strong>HMDB</strong>, <a href='http://www.hmdb.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>www.hmdb.ca</a>)<strong>[2]</strong>. Metabolite search was carried out with precursor mass error of 0.05 Da, adducts based on charge state i.e., [M+H]+, [M+2H]2+, [M+3H]3+ and [M-H]-, [M-2H]2- and [M-3H]3-, the identifications with low mass error were considered for further analysis. <strong>MetaboAnalyst </strong>tool (<a href='http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>www.metaboanalyst.ca</a>) was used for statistical and pathway analysis<strong>[3]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Chang HY, Chen CT, Lih TM, Lynn KS, Juo CG, Hsu WL, Sung TY. iMet-Q: A User-Friendly Tool for Label-Free Metabolomics Quantitation Using Dynamic Peak-Width Determination. PLoS One. 2016 Jan 19;11(1):e0146112. doi: 10.1371/journal.pone.0146112. PMID: 26784691; PMCID: PMC4718670.</p><p><strong>[2] </strong>Wishart DS, Feunang YD, Marcu A, Guo AC, Liang K, Vázquez-Fresno R, Sajed T, Johnson D, Li C, Karu N, Sayeeda Z, Lo E, Assempour N, Berjanskii M, Singhal S, Arndt D, Liang Y, Badran H, Grant J, Serra-Cayuela A, Liu Y, Mandal R, Neveu V, Pon A, Knox C, Wilson M, Manach C, Scalbert A.

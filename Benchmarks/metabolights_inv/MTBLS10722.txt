Study Title	Deep insights into microsampling technologies for global lipidomic profiling of human whole blood using high-throughput LC-MS
Study Description	<p>In recent years, blood microsampling (BμS) technologies have gained popularity, as they offer simplicity, minimal invasiveness, and suitability for remote sample collection. In this study, the aim was to explore the potential of three BμS devices for reliable untargeted lipidomic profiling using reversed-phase ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole-time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Such devices –from the traditional non-volumetric dried blood spot cards (DBS) to the recently developed quantitative DBS (qDBS), and volumetric absorptive microsampling (VAMS)– were compared to conventional plasma samples from ten healthy volunteers. In this novel study, a comprehensive lipidomic workflow was applied, combining an all-in-one extraction protocol and a single-step data processing and lipid annotation using a targeted peak extraction strategy based on a human plasma lipid database. BμS&nbsp;lipidomics&nbsp;analysis was evaluated for recovery, matrix effect and precision. The data acquired for BµS devices yielded consistent profiles, comparable to plasma, observing that the volumetric devices –qDBS and VAMS– were more similar between them compared to DBS. For BμS, more than 500 lipid species from 20 distinct lipid subclasses were annotated with high confidence level. Most deuterium-labeled lipid species studied were successfully recovered in the BµS devices with minimal ion suppression detected. Precision analysis of deuterated lipid species and the 195 endogenous lipids indicated that qDBS and VAMS had higher precision compared to DBS. Differences in lipids between each BµS device and plasma were assessed. Our work presents a robust workflow for lipid profiling using BμS, suitable for taking informed decisions in further experimental designs.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Whole blood (<strong>WB</strong>) samples, obtained by direct&nbsp;venipuncture, were collected from 10 healthy volunteers (5 males and 5 females) after overnight fasting (<strong>Fig. 1A</strong> of the paper associated to the study), at the Laboratory of Evaluation of Human Biological Performance, School of Physical Education and&nbsp;Sport Science, Aristotle University of Thessaloniki, Greece. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the same university (Prot. No. 306272/2022). All participants signed an&nbsp;informed consent&nbsp;form. From each healthy volunteer, 2 <strong>WB </strong>samples from an&nbsp;antecubital vein were collected into 6&nbsp;mL&nbsp;EDTA&nbsp;tubes. One tube was used to obtain <strong>plasma </strong>after&nbsp;centrifugation&nbsp;and was treated following a typical procedure for <strong>lipidomics </strong>already described<strong>[1]</strong>. The second <strong>WB </strong>sample was applied on <strong>3 BµS devices</strong>, (<strong>Supplemental Fig. S2</strong> of the paper associated to the study). Additionally, a <strong>pooled WB sample</strong> was prepared by mixing 0.5&nbsp;mL of all individual <strong>WB </strong>samples (<strong>Cohort </strong>study samples).</p><p><br></p><p>The <strong>BµS </strong>collection was performed as follows: exact volume of 20&nbsp;µL of <strong>WB </strong>were pipetted onto WhatmanTM&nbsp;903 filter paper card (<strong>W-DBS</strong>) using a micropipette. A drop of <strong>WB </strong>were loaded into each 10&nbsp;µL inlet spot of the CapitainerB qDBS device (<strong>C-qDBS</strong>) according to the manufacturer instructions using a Pasteur pipette. Each 20&nbsp;µL Mitra tip, based on Volumetric Absorptive Microsampling (VAMS) technology, was sampled by immersing the tip in <strong>WB </strong>to full saturation for approximately 6 s, using the Mitra Clamshell device (<strong>M-VAMS</strong>) (<strong>Fig. 1A</strong> of the paper associated to the study). Samples were left to dry at&nbsp;room temperature&nbsp;for 3&nbsp;h, frozen at -80&nbsp;°C, and subsequently shipped on dry ice to the Center of&nbsp;Metabolomics&nbsp;and&nbsp;Bioanalysis&nbsp;(CEMBIO), Madrid, Spain. Upon arrival, samples were immediately stored at -80&nbsp;°C until further analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Gonzalez-Riano, C., Gradillas, A. and Barbas, C., 2021. Exploiting the formation of adducts in mobile phases with ammonium fluoride for the enhancement of annotation in liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry based lipidomics.&nbsp;Journal of Chromatography Open,&nbsp;1, p.100018. doi:10.1016/j.jcoa.2021.100018.</p>	<p>Lipid extraction from <strong>WB</strong>, <strong>WB loaded on the BµS devices</strong> and <strong>plasma </strong>samples was performed using a single-phase extraction protocol (monophasic system) with a binary solvent (MeOH/MTBE (1:1, v/v)), that allowed&nbsp;protein precipitation&nbsp;and lipid extraction in 1 step<strong>[1]</strong>(<strong>Fig. 1B</strong> of the paper associated to the study).</p><p><br></p><p>The <strong>WB microsamples </strong>were taken based on the type of device: <strong>(i) </strong>1 punch of 6&nbsp;mm (approximate volume of 8&nbsp;µL of <strong>WB</strong>) from<strong> W-DBS</strong>; <strong>(ii)</strong> 2 spots (with an exact volume of 10&nbsp;µL of <strong>WB </strong>each) from <strong>C-qDBS</strong>; <strong>(iii)</strong> 1 tip (with an exact volume of 20&nbsp;µL of <strong>WB</strong>) from <strong>M-VAMS</strong>. Samples were thawed on ice and transferred into 2&nbsp;mL Eppendorf tubes using punches, spots or tips as appropriate. Firstly, to hydrate the samples, 50&nbsp;μL of ultra-pure H2O was added, followed by vortexing for 2&nbsp;min. For lipid extraction, 175&nbsp;μL of MeOH, spiked with the <strong>ISTD</strong>, 3&nbsp;ppm of C17-sphinganine, 6&nbsp;ppm of deuterated&nbsp;palmitic acid&nbsp;(d31) and 5&nbsp;μL of <strong>SPLASH LIPIDOMIX</strong>, were added to the samples and vortexed for 3&nbsp;min. Then, 175&nbsp;µL of MTBE were added to the Eppendorf tube at room temperature. The resulting mixture was vortexed for 3&nbsp;min followed by&nbsp;sonication&nbsp;for 15&nbsp;min at 15&nbsp;°C. Afterwards, samples were centrifuged at 16,100&nbsp;x&nbsp;g&nbsp;for 15&nbsp;min at 15&nbsp;°C. Finally, 120&nbsp;μL of the resulting supernatant were transferred to 2 separate Chromacol LC-MS vials with insert (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madrid, Spain) for each MS ionization mode.</p><p><br></p><p>For <strong>WB </strong>and <strong>plasma </strong>samples, lipid extraction was carried out by pipetting 20&nbsp;µL of <strong>WB </strong>or <strong>plasma </strong>into a 2&nbsp;mL Eppendorf tube, followed by the addition of 175&nbsp;μL of MeOH (spiked with the <strong>ISTD</strong> as described above). Then, 175&nbsp;µL of MTBE were added to the Eppendorf tube at room temperature. The resulting mixture was vortexed for 30&nbsp;min at room temperature followed by centrifugation at 16,100&nbsp;x&nbsp;g&nbsp;for 15&nbsp;min at 15&nbsp;°C. Finally, 120&nbsp;µL of the resulting supernatant were transferred to Chromacol LC-MS vials with insert (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madrid, Spain) for each MS ionization mode.</p><p><br></p><p>In parallel to the preparation of the samples of interest,&nbsp;quality control&nbsp;(<strong>QC</strong>) samples were prepared. For the <strong>QC </strong>sample, the <strong>WB </strong>pooled sample was applied in an additional spot or tip of each of the <strong>3 BµS </strong>and followed the same procedure as the samples. Once lipids were extracted, the supernatants from the 3 devices were mixed. In addition, standard solutions were prepared on MeOH/MTBE (1:1, v/v) and contained <strong>SPLASH LIPIDOMIX</strong>, following the same procedure as the samples.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Villasenor, A., Garcia-Perez, I., Garcia, A., Posma, J.M., Fernandez-Lopez, M., Nicholas, A.J., Modi, N., Holmes, E. and Barbas, C., 2014. Breast milk metabolome characterization in a single-phase extraction, multiplatform analytical approach.&nbsp;Analytical chemistry,&nbsp;86(16), pp.8245-8252. doi:10.1021/ac501853d. PMID:&nbsp;25058331.</p>	<p>Samples were injected in <strong>positive </strong>and <strong>negative </strong>ionization modes with volumes of <strong>2 μL</strong> and <strong>5 μL</strong> respectively, using an <strong>Agilent 1290 Infinity II Multisampler system</strong> kept at 15 °C to maintain lipid stability and prevent precipitation and an <strong>Agilent 1260 Infinity II LC System</strong>. An <strong>Agilent InfinityLab Poroshell 120 EC-C18 (100 mm × 3 mm x 2.7 μm) reversed-phase column </strong>and an <strong>Agilent InfinityLab Poroshell 120 EC-C18, 3.0 × 5 mm, 2.7 μm guard column</strong> were used for lipid separation, with temperature maintained at 50 °C. The mobile phases used for both ionization modes were (A) 10 mM CH3COONH4 and 0.2 mM NH4F in H2O/MeOH (9:1, v/v) and (B) 10 mM CH3COONH4 and 0.2 mM NH4F in ACN/MeOH/IPA (2:3:5, v/v/v). The flow rate was set at 0.6 mL/min. The gradient during the chromatographic analysis was as follows: 0-1 min, 30% A; 3.5-10 min, 14% A; 11-17.1 min, 0% A; 17.1-19 min, 30% A. The strategy used for multiwash involved a mixture of MeOH/IPA (1:1, v/v) and a wash time of 15 s, followed by a mixture of A/B (30:70, v/v) to assist in reaching the initial conditions.</p>	<p>For the mass spectrometer, the <strong>Agilent 6545 QTOF-MS </strong>was set with the following parameters: 175 V fragmentor, 65 V skimmer, 3500 V capillary voltage, 750 V octupole radio frequency voltage, 11 L/min nebulizer gas flow, 290 °C gas temperature, 40 psi nebulizer gas pressure, 11 L/min sheath gas flow, and 370 °C sheath gas temperature. A solution consisting of 2 reference mass compounds was infused into the system throughout the whole analysis via an <strong>Agilent 1260 Iso Pump</strong> at a flow rate of 1 mL/min and with a split ratio of 1:100 for mass correction. The reference masses used for <strong>ESI(+)</strong> were m/z 121.0509 (purine, detected as [C5H4N4+H]+) and m/z 922.0098 (HP-0921 detected as [C18H18O6N3P3F24+H]+), while&nbsp;for <strong>ESI(-)</strong> the reference masses were m/z 119.0363 (purine, detected as [C5H4N4-H]-) and m/z 1033.9881 (HP-0921 detected as [C18H18O6N3P3F24+CF3COO]-).</p><p><br></p><p>The MS operated in full scan mode (<strong>MS1 </strong>level), scanning from<strong> m/z 100 – 1100</strong> at a scan rate of 1 spectra/s. Then, 10 automated <strong>MS/MS</strong> analysis were run using data-dependent acquisition (<strong>DDA</strong>) mode with an automated iterative exclusion method. The acquisition parameters were set as follows: <strong>(i)</strong> Scan rate of 3 spectra/s and 3 precursors per cycle; <strong>(ii)</strong> Threshold at 5000 counts and 0.001 % of <strong>MS/MS</strong> threshold; <strong>(iii) </strong>Mass range scan from<strong> m/z 100 – 1100</strong>;<strong> (iv)&nbsp;</strong>An exclusion list was performed to prevent references masses, noise, and artifacts (non sample related) and detected from blank samples and it was introduced in the method; <strong>(v)</strong>The collision energy of the first 5 iterative-<strong>MS/MS</strong> runs was set at 20 eV, and the remaining 5 runs were conducted at 40 eV;<strong> (vi)</strong> Isolation window for precursor selection was set as narrow width (∼ 1.3 amu); <strong>(vii)</strong> Isotope exclusion function filter was applied; <strong>(viii)</strong> Data mode in both ionization modes: <strong>centroid</strong>. Data acquisition was performed with <strong>MassHunter Data Acquisition software (B.08.02, Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany)</strong>.</p>	<p>The raw data obtained from <strong>ESI (+/-)</strong> ionization modes were initially assessed using <strong>Agilent MassHunter Qualitative software</strong> (<strong>B. 10.0, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA</strong>) to evaluate system mass accuracy of the reference masses, <strong>QC </strong>reproducibility and overall data quality through the visual analysis of the chromatograms.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Evaluation of BµS analysis</strong></p><p>The method was evaluated by assessing extraction recovery (<strong>ER</strong>), matrix effect (<strong>ME</strong>), and <strong>precision </strong>for the 3 <strong>BµS devices</strong> (W-DBS, C-qDBS and M-VAMS).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Extraction recovery</strong></p><p>The deuterium-labeled lipid species of <strong>SPLASH LIPIDOMIX</strong> were used for <strong>ER </strong>as this was the commercial mixture of deuterated standards with known concentration. Lipid extraction efficiency was determined by comparing the analyte response of the deuterated lipid species in the pooled <strong>WB </strong>sample from the 3 <strong>BµS </strong>devices, spiked <strong>before </strong>and <strong>after </strong>lipid extraction (<strong>Supplemental Fig. S2</strong>). Due to limited sample volume, we prepared 2 analytical replicates, each analyzed twice in the instrument, at <strong>2 different concentrations</strong>, by adding <strong>3 and 10&nbsp;µL</strong> of the <strong>SPLASH LIPIDOMIX</strong>. Final concentrations of each deuterium-labeled lipid standard in each case are provided in&nbsp;<strong>Supplemental Table S3</strong>. ER was calculated using&nbsp;<strong>Supplemental Eq. 2</strong>.</p><p>All <strong>ER </strong>microsamples were prepared and measured with the same protocol and analytical method described above. Peak detection, integration and manual correction of the <strong>SPLASH LIPIDOMIX </strong>mixture were performed using <strong>Agilent MassHunter&nbsp;Quantitative Analysis&nbsp;software (B. 9.0, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA)</strong>, considering RT and the selected target adduct ion for each deuterium-labeled lipid standard.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Matrix Effect</strong></p><p><strong>ME </strong>was determined by calculating the ratio of the lipid species of the <strong>SPLASH LIPIDOMIX</strong> spiked in the samples after lipid extraction to their signal in the standard solutions (containing the same concentrations). The determination of <strong>ME </strong>was calculated –at <strong>low </strong>and <strong>high </strong>concentrations– with 2 sample replicates and 2 injections per replicate. The average of the replicates and relative standard deviation (%RSD) were calculated to show the <strong>ME </strong>average and the degree of reproducibility, respectively. <strong>ME </strong>was calculated using&nbsp;<strong>Supplemental Eq. 3</strong>. Ion suppression was identified when ME &gt; 15&nbsp;%.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Precision</strong></p><p>This was conducted by spiking <strong>SPLASH LIPIDOMIX</strong> to 3 randomly selected samples from the 10 healthy volunteers and each sample was processed in triplicate. Data pre-processing was performed using the <strong>Batch TFE algorithm</strong> of <strong>Agilent MassHunter Profinder software</strong> as previously reported. The mean area values of the deuterium-labeled lipids were calculated, along with their %RSD, and the acceptance criterion for precision was defined as average RSD &lt; 25&nbsp;%.</p><p><br></p><p>In addition, precision was assessed, using the same 3 selected samples, in a robust set of 195 endogenous lipids, belonging to a reference lipid set from our SRM 1950 lipid LC-MS database<strong>[1],</strong> whose annotations achieved the highest possible confidence level, fulfilling complete agreement and consistency in the four pieces of information evaluated:&nbsp;m/z&nbsp;accuracy, <strong>MS/MS</strong> characteristic fragments, chromatographic retention behavior, and qualitative adduct and cluster profile in both <strong>ESI(+/-)</strong>. Following the same procedure as before, lipid species were normalized using the corresponding deuterium-labeled lipid species according to their lipid subclass. Then %RSD was calculated for each individual sample and the same acceptance criterion was used (RSD &lt; 25&nbsp;%). Of note, C17-sphinganine and deuterated&nbsp;palmitic acid&nbsp;(d-31) were included in these analyses as <strong>ISTD</strong> at constant concentrations to assess analytical reproducibility. Their primary role was to correct potential analytical drifts, ensuring consistent analytical conditions throughout these analyses.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Martínez, S., Fernández-García, M., Londoño-Osorio, S., Barbas, C. and Gradillas, A., 2024. Highly reliable LC-MS lipidomics database for efficient human plasma profiling based on NIST SRM 19501.&nbsp;Journal of Lipid Research,&nbsp;65 (11). doi:10.1016/j.jlr.2024.100671. PMID:&nbsp;39395790.&nbsp;PMCID:&nbsp;PMC11607663.</p>	<p>Then, data pre-processing (including lipid detection, signal integration, and lipid annotation) were performed simultaneously, using a <strong>lipid LC-MS database</strong>, based on <strong>SRM 1950 human plasma</strong>, as a&nbsp;template&nbsp;(<strong>Supplemental Table S1</strong> of the paper associated to this study)<strong>[1]</strong>. Data pre-processing was performed using the<strong> Batch&nbsp;Targeted Feature Extraction&nbsp;(TFE) algorithm</strong> of <strong>Agilent MassHunter Profinder software (B. 10.0.2, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA)</strong>. This algorithm uses a .csv input file, containing lipid ID, molecular formula,&nbsp;monoisotopic mass, and retention time (RT), to extract lipids from the MS1 raw sample data (.d files), using the computational process “<strong style='font-style: inherit;'>Find Compounds by Formula</strong>”. The input files were generated based on the ionization capacities of each lipid subclass, and their adduct profile, as previously reported<strong style='font-style: inherit;'>[1]</strong>, to minimize false-positive annotations. A detailed workflow for data pre-processing and the optimized parameters for the TFE algorithm are described in detail in a previous study<strong style='font-style: inherit;'>[1]&nbsp;</strong>and briefly summarized in&nbsp;<strong style='font-style: inherit;'>Supporting Information</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p>After running the method for the<strong> TFE algorithm</strong> the results were manually inspected, and the curated data were exported from <strong>Profinder software</strong> to a (.csv file) and saved as a (.xlsx file). <strong>ESI(+)</strong> and <strong>ESI(-) </strong>output results were combined, and duplicates were removed based on the abundance and ionization mode in which each lipid subclass better ionizes. Finally, a high-quality list comprising all matched lipid species, and their corresponding abundances, was generated.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Martínez, S., Fernández-García, M., Londoño-Osorio, S., Barbas, C. and Gradillas, A., 2024. Highly reliable LC-MS lipidomics database for efficient human plasma profiling based on NIST SRM 19501.&nbsp;<em>J</em>ournal of Lipid Research,&nbsp;65 (11). doi:10.1016/j.jlr.2024.100671. PMID:&nbsp;39395790&nbsp;PMCID:&nbsp;PMC11607663.</p>

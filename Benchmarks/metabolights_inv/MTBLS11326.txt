Study Title	Plasma metabolomic profiling during peri-parturition in healthy Thoroughbred mares
Study Description	<p>Background: Accurate prediction of the timing of parturition is crucial to ensure the health and well-being of both mares and foals. However, equine pregnancies are characterized by significant variability in gestation length, unique endocrine mechanisms, and subtle physiological changes before parturition. </p><p>Objectives: This study aimed to investigate the characteristic changes in the peripheral blood metabolome of mares before and after parturition using metabolomic approaches.</p><p>Study Design: a longitudinal study.</p><p>Methods: Peripheral blood samples were collected from nine successfully foaling Thoroughbred mares from four days before to five days after parturition, and a non-targeted metabolomic analysis was performed using GC-MS.</p><p>Results: PLS-DA and hierarchical clustering analysis demonstrated cluster separation between pre-parturition, parturition, and post-parturition. ANOVA revealed significant differences in 49 metabolites across all time points. In those metabolites, glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) levels increased three days before parturition. Four metabolites that changed significantly before parturition were enriched in six metabolic pathways. After parturition, 22 metabolites exhibited significant differences and were enriched in 20 pathways. </p><p>Main Limitations: The number of animals and samples included in this study was limited, and factors such as the age and reproductive history of the mares were not considered. In addition, this study did not conduct quantitative research to determine the specific concentrations and ranges of the key metabolites.</p><p>Conclusions: The results of this study suggest that G3P are potential biomarkers for predicting parturition and provides new insights into mare’s pre- and post-parturition blood metabolic changes.Background: Accurate prediction of the timing of parturition is crucial to ensure the health and well-being of both mares and foals. However, equine pregnancies are characterized by significant variability in gestation length, unique endocrine mechanisms, and subtle physiological changes before parturition. </p><p><br></p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>A total of 9 healthy thoroughbred mares from the Japan Racing Association (JRA) Hidaka Training and Research Center were enrolled in this study . All mares underwent health examinations before sample collection and were confirmed to be successfully convinced after mating by a veterinarian. During gestation, peripheral blood samples were collected from the jugular vein by a veterinarian, starting from the day of mating to the pre-parturition period in all pregnant mares weekly until 7 days post-parturition. When pregnancy reached one week prior to the predicted delivery date, as estimated by the veterinarian, the blood sampling frequency increased from once every two weeks to once daily. Blood samples were collected on days 1, 3 and 7 post-parturitions. Finally, blood samples collected from 4 days before delivery to 7 days after delivery were subjected to metabolomic analysis. The blood samples were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 30 min, and the separated plasma samples were stored at -80 °C for further analysis.</p>	<p>The plasma samples were filtered using a 0.2 μm,&nbsp;13 mm cellulose acetate disposable membrane filter unit (Toyo Roshi Kaisha, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Next, 50 μL of filtered plasma, 250 μL of methanol-chloroform-water solution (2.5:1:1) and 5 μL of 1 mg/mL 2-isopropylmalic acid (as internal standard) were mixed, then incubated at 1200 rpm and 37 °C for 30 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 16,000 x g at 4 °C for 5 min. Next, 225 μL of the supernatant was collected and mixed with 200 μL of distilled water. The mixtures were again centrifugated at 16,000 x g at 4 °C for 5 min, and 250 μL of the supernatant was collected. The supernatants were processed to remove some of the solvent using a centrifugal evaporator CVE-2200 (Tokyo Rikakikai Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for 25 min. Subsequently, the samples were kept frozen at -80 °C until completely solidified. The frozen samples were then freeze-dried using a vacuum freeze-drying machine FDU-1200 (Tokyo Rikakikai Co.) for 16 h.</p><p><br></p><p>After freeze-drying, the powdered samples were dissolved in 40 µL of a methoxamine hydrochloride solution (20 mg/mL in pyridine). The mixtures were incubated at 1200 rpm at 30 °C for 90 min. Subsequently, 20 μL of N-methyl-N-trimethylsilyl-trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was added, followed by incubation at 1200 rpm at 37 °C for 45 min.</p>	<p>In total 1 μL of the derivatized plasma sample was injected into a GC-MS QP2020 NX system (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) using an AOC-20i automatic injector (Shimadzu Corporation). A 1 μm DB5 column capillary column (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used for the analysis. The vaporizing chamber temperature was set to 280 °C, with a constant gas flow rate of 5 mL/min.</p><p><br></p><p>The temperature profile consisted of three stages: first, a constant temperature of 100 °C for 4 min; second, an increase of 10 °C per minute until reaching 320 °C; and finally, maintenance at 320 °C for 11 min. Metabolites were identified using a standard smart metabolite database (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan).</p>	<p>Electron ionization was performed at 70 eV. Data were recorded at 0.2-s intervals within a mass range of 45-600 m/z.</p>	<p>The original file is in qgd format, and it was converted to an abf file that can be read by MS-DIAL using the ABF Converter software.</p>	<p>Each peak was automatically identified using the MS-DIAL v4.90 software.</p>

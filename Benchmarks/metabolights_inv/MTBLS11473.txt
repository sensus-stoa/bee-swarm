Study Title	Cross-feeding and co-degradation within a bacterial consortium dominated by challenging-to-cultivate Leucobacter sp. HA-1 enhances sulfonamides degradation
Study Description	<p>Background: The use of sulfonamides (SAs) caused residual pollution in the environment. Bacteria play an important role in the degradation of sulfonamide antibiotics, and microbial consortium offers advantages over single bacterium. However, the complex degradation process and interaction mechanisms within such consortiums still poorly understood. </p><p>Results: Here, a consortium named ACJ, consisting of Leucobacter sp. HA-1, Bacillus sp. HC-1 and Gordonia sp. HAEJ-1, obtained from activated sludge of pharmaceutical plants, was identified as capable of degrading various SAs. Several papers failed to get the pure culture of Leucobacter sp. in the degradation of SAs, here we successfully obtained the Leucobacter sp. HA-1 pure culture involved in the degradation of SAs with the growth factors provided by strain HC-1 or HAEJ-1. Strain HA-1 was responsible for the initial attack of sulfonamide molecules resulting in the release of 2-aminoquinoxaline (2-AQ) and trihydroxybenzene (HHQ), which were further degraded and used for growth by strain HAEJ-1. Genomic, metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses revealed genes associated with nucleotide repair, ABC transporters, quorum sensing, TCA cycle and cell cycle in strain HA-1 were up-regulated during co-culture compared without the other two strains, which indicated that HA-1 utilized certain factors from strain HC-1 or HAEJ-1 for growth. </p><p>Conclusion: These results revealed that there was a bidirectional ecological relationship of cross-feeding and co-degradation among consortium ACJ. In summary, this study provides new insights into the mechanisms of microbial consortium interaction and co-degradation in antibiotic-contaminated environments.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The sludge water was collected from a sewage treatment plant in Jiangling County, Jingzhou, Hubei province (112°26'30'E, 30°2'13'N), China, supplemented with appropriate concentration of SQX for continuous enrichment in MSM medium. <em>Gordonia</em>&nbsp;sp. HAEJ-1 and&nbsp;<em>Bacillus</em>&nbsp;sp. HC-1 were separately cultured in 100 mL LB medium and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm. The bacteria were crushed by ultrasound for 15 min and centrifuged at high speed for 30 min. The supernatant of&nbsp;<em>Gordonia</em>&nbsp;sp. HAEJ-1 and Bacillus sp. HC-1 were collected and filtered with sterile 0.22 μm filter membrane for sterilization. Equal amount of supernatant was added to 20 mL LB medium, and inoculated with&nbsp;<em>Leucobacter</em>sp. HA-1 and cultured at 30 °C, and the non-inoculated strain HA-1 was used as CK. After 5 days of culture, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min and transferred to a new centrifuge tube. Subsequently, the supernatant was freeze-dried into a powder using a Thermo Heto PowerDry LL3000 machine, stored at -20 °C and sent to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd for metabolomics analysis.</p>	<p>The sample preparation involved collecting 500 μL of the reaction liquid and adding an equal volume of methanol to halt the reaction. Subsequently, after subjecting the mixture to high-speed agitation for 2 min, the upper liquid phase was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 1 min. The supernatant was freeze-dried into a powder using a Thermo Heto PowerDry LL3000 machine and then sent to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd for metabolomics sequencing.</p><p><br></p><p>As a part of the system conditioning and quality control process, a pooled quality control sample (QC) was prepared by mixing equal volumes of all samples. The QC samples were disposed and tested in the same manner as the analytic samples. It helped to represent the whole sample set, which would be injected at regular intervals (every 5-15 samples) in order to monitor the stability of the analysis.</p>	<p>The HPLC system utilized for this analysis was the <strong>Thermo Scientific Ultimate 3000</strong>, equipped with an <strong>ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm x 100 mm; Waters)</strong> column. Injection volume was 3 µL. Mobile phase A was 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), mobile phase B was 47.5% acetonitrile + 47.5% isopropyl alcohol + 5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid). The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min and the column temperature was 40 °C.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry data was acquired on the <strong>Thermo Scientific Q Exactive HF-X </strong>mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in positive and negative modes, and the mass scanning range is 70-1050 m/z. The flow rate of sheath gas is 50 psi, the flow rate of auxiliary gas is 13 psi, the heating temperature of auxiliary gas is 425 °C, the positive mode ion spray voltage is set to 3500 V, the negative mode ion spray voltage is set to -3500 V, the ion transport tube temperature is 325 °C, and the normalized collision energy is 20-40-60 V cyclic collision energy. The primary mass spectral resolution is 60000, the secondary mass spectral resolution is 7500, and the data are collected by DDA mode.</p>	<p>The raw data was processed and visualized using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA). After searching the database, the data matrix was uploaded to cloud.majorbio.com for analysis. Firstly, the data matrix is preprocessed as follows: The data matrix uses the 80% rule to remove the missing value, that is, to retain at least one set of samples with a non-zero value of more than 80%, and then fill the gap value (the minimum value in the original matrix to fill the gap value). In order to reduce the error caused by sample preparation and instrument instability, the response intensity of the sample essential spectrum peak is normalized by the sum normalization method, and the normalized data matrix is obtained. At the same time, variables with relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt; 30% of QC samples were deleted, and log10 logization was carried out to obtain the final data matrix for subsequent analysis. Secondly, the ropls package (v1.6.2) in R language was used to conduct principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal least partial square discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) on the pre-processed data matrix, and the stability of the model was evaluated by using 7 cycles of interactive verification. The selection of metabolites with significant differences was based on the variable weight value (VIP) obtained by OPLS-DA model and the P-value of student's t test. The metabolites with VIP &gt; 1 and p &lt; 0.05 were identified as significantly different metabolites. Differences metabolites by KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) for the metabolic pathways of annotation, differences of metabolites involved in pathways. The Python software package scipy.stats was used for pathway enrichment analysis, and the biological pathways most relevant to experimental treatment were obtained by Fisher exact test.</p>	<p>After the computer, the LC-MS raw data was imported into the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, peak alignment. The result is a data matrix of retention time, mass-charge ratio and peak intensity, And MS and MSMS HMDB mass spectrometry and metabolic information public database (http://www.hmdb.ca/) and Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and the self-built database matching, metabolites information.</p><p><br></p><p>The quantification of metabolites was achieved by constructing a standard curve through the external standard method, correlating the peak area of the sample to the curve. Metabolites were identified and classified using KEGG for metabolic pathway analysis. Significant differences in metabolites between groups were determined at p &lt; 0.05 and VIP &gt; 1.</p>

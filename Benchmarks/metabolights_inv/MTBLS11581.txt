Study Identifier	MTBLS11581
Study Title	Metabolome and comparative genome provide insights into secondary metabolites generation of a rare karst-growing Rhododendron in vitro culture
Study Description	<p>Rhododendron species have the potential to be rich in secondary metabolites with pharmaceutical or industrial value. However, there is a lack of in comprehensive metabolome studies at the genome level, particularly for unique and rare species like R. bailiense which exclusively grows in karst environments in Guizhou, southwest China. Recently, genome assembly data for this species was available. In this study, non-targeted metabolomics was employed to investigate the secondary metabolites profile of R. bailiense callus. The callus of R. bailiense was induced using 0.2 mg/L TDZ (Thidiazuron) + 0.1 mg/L IBA (3-Indole butyric acid). A comparison between light-treated calli and dark-cultured calli revealed differential accumulation of metabolites, particularly in flavonoids, terpenoids, coumarins and hydroxycinnamic acids, known for their beneficial effects such as antioxidant, anticancer and anti-inflammatory properties. Proanthocyanidins, with various health-promoting effects, were found to accumulate significantly in dark-cultured calli. Light conditions promoted diterpene and triterpene products, whereas darkness favored sesquiterpene products. Additionally, the study demonstrated the potential of utilizing Agrobacterium transformation technology on callus suspension cells to enhance secondary metabolite production. Comparison with the genome of R. molle revealed that the R. bailiense genome exhibited active 'glycosyltransferase activity', possessed a higher number of copies of monoterpene and sesquiterpene terpene synthases, and contained high copies of specific cytochrome P450 members (CYP71, CYP76, CYP79, CYP82, CYP736). This study offers valuable insights and potential strategies for the biosynthesis and production of Rhododendron secondary metabolites with pharmaceutical or industrial significance.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The pods containing the seeds of <em>Rhododendron bailiense</em>&nbsp;were collected from the Baili Rhododendron Nature Reserve in Bijie City, Guizhou Province, China (27.22° N, 105.84° E). To induce callus formation, the seeds were first washed with a 75% alcohol solution for 30 s, followed by disinfection in a 10% sodium hypochlorite solution for approximately 15 min. The seeds were then rinsed 5 times with sterile distilled water.</p><p><br></p><p>WPM medium supplemented with 1.0 mg/L&nbsp;KT (kinetin) was utilized for seed germination. The culture conditions included a photoperiod of 14 h of light and 10 h of darkness per day, with a luminous flux density provided by a white LED light source of 50 μmol/m^2/s, maintained at a room temperature of 25 °C. Young seedlings were subsequently used to induce callus in the medium consisting of WPM + TDZ (Thidiazuron) + IBA (Indole butyric acid). The calli were then transferred to media containing WPM + 0.5 mg/L&nbsp;2,4-D (2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid) + 1.0 mg/L&nbsp;NAA (Naphthalcetic acid) for proliferation under completely dark culture conditions.</p><p><br></p><p>To ensure the consistency of the initial genetic background of the cultured materials, we utilized callus somatic clones of <em>R. bailiense</em>&nbsp;for our experiments. Calli were induced from a single sterile seedling and propagated independently. Each subculture period lasted 40 days, resulting in a total of 160 days over 4 consecutive subcultures. The initial period was designated as C0, while C1 and C4 represented the first and fourth subculture periods, respectively. During the C4 subculture cycle, part of the callus material was cultured in darkness, while the remainder was subjected to light treatment. The light treatment conditions consisted of 14 h of light followed by 10 h of darkness, utilizing a white LED light source, with all other conditions remaining constant. After 35 days of treatment, a portion of each material was collected for transcriptome sequencing. Following 40 days, additional samples were taken for metabolite profiling.</p>	<p>1) Accurately weigh a sample of 50 ± 5 mg into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, and add a 6 mm diameter grinding bead. 2) Add 400 µL of extraction solution (methanol:water, 4:1, v:v), which contains 4 species of internal standards, including L-2-chlorophenylalanine at a concentration of 0.02 mg/mL. 3) Grind the mixture using a frozen tissue grinder for 6 min at -10 °C and 50 Hz. 4) Perform low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min at 5 °C and 40 kHz. 5) Allow the sample to stand at -20 °C for 30 min. 6) Centrifuge the sample for 15 min at 13,000 x g and 4 °C, and transfer the supernatant to an injection vial using an internal intubation tube for analysis. 7) Additionally, take 20 µL of supernatant from each sample and combine it to create a quality control sample.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation of metabolites was conducted using a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm x 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 µm; Waters, Milford, USA). The mobile phase A consisted of 95% water and 5% acetonitrile (with 0.1% formic acid), while mobile phase B comprised 47.5% acetonitrile, 47.5% isopropyl alcohol and 5% water (also containing 0.1% formic acid). The injection volume was set at 3 μL and the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C.</p>	<p>The mass spectrometer operated in both positive and negative modes, utilizing spray voltages of 3.5 and -3.5 kV, respectively. The sheath gas and auxiliary gas were set to 50 and 13, respectively. The capillary temperature was maintained at 325 °C. The orbital trap analyzer scanned over a mass range of 70-1050 m/z, conducting full scans at a mass resolution of 60,000. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) MS experiments employed higher energy collision-induced dissociation (HCD) scans, with a normalized collision energy of 30 eV.</p>	<p>The raw data was imported into the metabolomics processing software ProgenesisQI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction and peak alignment. This process ultimately resulted in a data matrix containing information such as retention time, mass-to-charge ratio and peak intensity. Subsequently, the software was employed to search for and identify characteristic peaks, matching MS and MS/MS mass spectrometry data with the metabolic database. The MS mass error was set to less than 10 ppm, allowing for the identification of metabolites based on the secondary mass spectrometry matching score.</p>	<p>For metabolite peak identification and matching, the raw data were loaded into Progenesis QI (version 2.3, Nonlinear Dynamics, Waters, United States). The identified compounds were then&nbsp;entered into several&nbsp;databases, including&nbsp;Majorbio Biotech’s&nbsp;self-built metabolite library,&nbsp;Metlin&nbsp;(https://metlin.scripps.edu/),&nbsp;HMDB (https://hmdb.ca/metabolites)&nbsp;and KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/)&nbsp;for annotation. Detailed information on all metabolites is provided in Data S1. These metabolites have been confirmed using MS/MS (secondary mass spectrometry). Only fragment scores&nbsp;(related to Metlin&nbsp;database), or theoretical fragment scores&nbsp;(related to HMDB&nbsp;database), exceeding 30 were&nbsp;considered credible identifications. Following the initial database-related annotation of all metabolites, a manual search was conducted to eliminate instances of false positives and false matches in the data (e.g., metabolites unique to animals), thereby refining the final set of metabolite information. For the identified metabolites classified at different levels, level B(i) (Data S1)&nbsp;relies on accurate matching with the specific MS/MS library (comprising Majorbio Biotech's self-built&nbsp;metabolite library and the Metlin database), which offers relatively higher accuracy. Conversely, level B(ii) relies on computer-simulated matching with the MS/MS theoretical library (HMDB).</p>

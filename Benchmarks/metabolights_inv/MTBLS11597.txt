Study Identifier	MTBLS11597
Study Title	Mapping of the viral shunt across widespread coccolithophore blooms using metabolic biomarkers
Study Description	<p>The viral shunt is a fundamental ecosystem process which diverts the flux of organic carbon fixed through photosynthesis during algal bloom events from heterotrophic grazers to bacteria. Through the extracellular release of metabolites, lytic viral infections supply 2-10% of photosynthetically fixed carbon in the ocean for bacterial respiration. Despite its significance for the carbon cycle, we lack tools to detect the viral shunt in the natural environment and assess its ecological impact. Here, we investigated the use of exometabolites as biomarkers for the viral shunt by applying molecular, metabolomics, and oceanographic tools to study bloom dynamics of the cosmopolitan microalga <em>Gephyrocapsa huxleyi</em> (formerly <em>Emiliania huxleyi</em>) across the Atlantic Ocean, spanning four biogeochemical provinces between Iceland and Patagonia. We mapped the distinct metabolic footprint of its viral infections using exo- and endometabolomics and detected nineteen organohalogen metabolites across the blooms, showing their global distribution. A time-resolved comparison of particulate and dissolved metabolite pools during an induced mesocosm bloom revealed that virocells – actively infected host cells – were the source of the halogenated metabolites. Three trichloro-iodo metabolites were present during the demise of all virus-infected blooms, highlighting them as suitable metabolic biomarkers for the viral shunt. The environmental stability of these halometabolites in the DOM pool over a few days can recapitulate viral infections at earlier stages of phytoplankton bloom succession. The chloro-iodo metabolites thereby expand the existing repertoire of metabolic biomarkers for viral infections at sea and may advance efforts to trace the biogeochemical impact of alga-virus interactions in the surface ocean.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Mesocosm</strong></p><p>The mesocosm experiment AQUACOSM VIMS-Ehux was carried out between 24th May (day 0) and 16th June (day 23) 2018 in Raunefjorden at the Marine Biological Station Espegrend, Norway (60°16′11N; 5°13′07E), as previously described [1]. In brief, blooms of <em>G. huxleyi</em> were induced via nutrient addition, and water samples collected daily (07:00 AM) from 4 mesocosm bags and the surrounding fjord, which served as environmental reference. For untargeted metabolite profiling, water samples were collected from approximately 1 m depth in 10-20 L carboys (rinsed with &lt;100&nbsp;kDa filtered seawater) using a peristaltic pump at ca. 5 L/min and a 200 µm nylon pre-filter. Samples were kept at 4°C until further processing.</p><p>To collect intracellular metabolites, water samples (1-6 L) were first gravity-filtered through 25&nbsp;µm pre-cleaned stainless-steel filters (47 mm, Sinun Tech, Barkan, Israel). Filtrates were then filtered with gentle vacuum (&lt;400 mbar under-pressure) through pre-combusted GF/A filters (≥5&nbsp;h at 460°C; 47 mm, GE Healthcare Whatman, Buckinghamshire, UK) and pre-cleaned 0.22 µm PVDF filters (47 mm; Durapore). Each filter was supplemented with 5&nbsp;µL internal standard solutions containing N-hexanoyl-L-homoserine lactone-d3 (0.2 µg/µL in methanol) and d2-indole-3-acetic acid (d2-IAA, 1 µg/µL in water), and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen before storage at -80°C.</p><p>To extract dissolved metabolites, 1 L aliquots of the 0.22&nbsp;µm filtrates were supplemented with internal standards and acidified to pH 2.0 as described previously [1].</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p><strong>NA-VICE</strong></p><p>Sampling of oceanic <em>G. huxleyi</em> blooms along a transect in the North Atlantic was performed on board the R/V Knorr between 13 June and 16 July 2012, as described previously [2]. In brief, two stations were sampled as depth profiles, and for one of these, a 3-day Lagrangian drift was performed using a sediment trap. Water samples (1-1.5 L) were collected with a CTD rosette sampler, pre-filtered with a 200 µm mesh and then filtered through 0.8 µm PC filters (47 mm; Millipore). Filters were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen before storage at -80°C.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p><strong>TARA Breizh</strong></p><p>Sampling of an oceanic <em>G. huxleyi</em> bloom patch in the Celtic Sea (North Atlantic) was performed on board the R/V Tara between 27 May and 2 June 2019, as described previously [3]. A Lagrangian drift was performed using an Argo float. Water samples (50 L) from the depth of the maximum <em>G. huxleyi</em> abundance were pumped through a 20 µm nylon net and then filtered sequentially through pre- combusted GF/C filters (125 mm; Whatman) and 0.45 µm PVDF filters (142 mm; Durapore) using a vacuum pump. Filters were flash-frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen and -80°C.</p><p>To extract extracellular metabolites, 1 L aliquots of the 0.45 µm filtrates were collected in glass bottles.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p><strong>TARA Gayoso</strong></p><p>Sampling of various oceanic <em>G. huxleyi</em> blooms along the Patagonian Shelf break (South Atlantic) was performed on board the R/V Tara between 4 and 27 December 2021 as part of a joint expedition with the R/V Houssay in the Mission Microbiomes AtlantEco project. The sampling in December targeted coccolithophore blooms, while the sampling in November on R/V Houssay targeted the pre-bloom conditions. Lagrangian drifts were performed using SVP drifters. Water samples (50 L) were collected from the depth of maximum <em>G. huxleyi</em> abundance using a CTD rosette sampler, pumped through a 20 µm nylon net, and then filtered sequentially on pre-combusted GF/A filters (142 mm; Whatman) and pre-cleaned 0.22 μm PVDF filters (142 mm; Millipore) using a peristaltic pump.</p><p>To extract extracellular metabolites, 1 L aliquots of the 0.22 µm filtrates were collected in glass bottles and supplemented with 5&nbsp;µL d2-IAA (1 μg/μL in water; ≥98%, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) as internal standard.</p><p><br></p><p>[1] Kuhlisch, C. <em>et al.</em>, Viral infection of algal blooms leaves a unique metabolic footprint on the dissolved organic matter in the ocean. <em>Sci. Adv.</em> <strong>7</strong> (2021).</p><p>[2] Laber, C.P. <em>et al</em>. Coccolithovirus facilitation of carbon export in the North Atlantic. <em>Nat. Microbiol.</em> <strong>3</strong>, 537-547 (2018).</p><p>[3] dos Reis, M.C. <em>et al</em>. Exploring the phycosphere of <em>Emiliania huxleyi</em>: From bloom dynamics to microbiome assembly experiments. <em>Mol. Ecol.</em> <strong>32</strong>, 6507-6522 (2023).</p>	<p><strong>Mesocosm</strong></p><p>For intracellular metabolite analysis, the frozen GF/A filters were lyophilized within 1.5 months after collection and then processed in 4 randomized batches. After thawing, filters were extracted twice by adding 3 mL pre-cooled (-20°C) methanol (HPLC grade, J.T. Baker) followed by 1 min vortexing, 30 min orbital shaking (darkness and 4°C) and 30 min sonication (100%, 37 Hz, &lt;14°C). Extracts were then centrifuged (5 min, 3200×g, 4°C) and supernatants transferred to glass vials that were kept at 4°C during the 2nd extraction of the filters. Supernatants were dried under a nitrogen flow and stored at -80°C until further processing. To remove filter debris, dried extracts were re-dissolved in 1.4 mL pre-cooled methanol, vortexed for 1 min, sonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged for 10 min at 20,800×g. Supernatants were dried under a nitrogen flow and stored at -80°C until LC-MS analysis.</p><p>A pooled quality control (QC) sample was generated by combining 50 µL aliquots of each biological sample. Two filter blanks from the 4th and 18th day of the mesocosm experiment were processed along with extraction solution blanks for each batch.</p><p>Prior to LC-MS analysis, dried extracts (n = 130) were randomized and divided into four batches with 32 or 34 samples each batch including filter and extraction blanks. Randomization was performed automatically using an in-house R script with the following constraints: the total number of biological samples per batch was 30 and included 6 samples of each bag or the fjord and at least 2 samples of each bloom phase (days 1-8, 9-16, and 17-23). Per batch, samples were thawed, re-dissolved in 200 μL methanol:water (1:1, ν:ν) containing as injection standards tryptophan-d5 (2.1 µg/mL; 98%, Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) and caffeine-d9 (1.5 µg/mL; 98%, Sigma-Aldrich), vortexed, sonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 3,200×g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were transferred to 200 μL glass inserts in autosampler vials and directly used for LC-MS analysis. For each batch, a new aliquot of the pooled QC sample was transferred into an injection vial.</p><p>For extracellular metabolite analysis, the acidified 1 L aliquots of the 0.22 µm filtrates were extracted using solid-phase extraction (SPE) as described previously [1].</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NA-VICE</strong></p><p>For intracellular metabolite analysis, the filters were extracted with water and methanol as described previously [1].</p><p><br></p><p><strong>TARA Breizh</strong></p><p>For extracellular metabolite analysis, the 1 L aliquots of the 0.45 µm filtrates were loaded with a peristaltic pump onto SPE cartridges (Oasis HLB, 500 mg; Waters) without prior sample acidification and SPE conditioning. Cartridges were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -20°C and 4°C until further processing. Seven months later, the cartridges were thawed, excess water removed by vacuum, and the cartridges washed (18 mL water; ULC/MS grade, Biolab), dried by vacuum, and eluted with 4 mL methanol (HPLC grade, J.T.Baker). Eluates were dried under a nitrogen flow and stored at -80°C until further processing.</p><p>Prior to LC-MS analysis, dried extracts were re-dissolved in 200 μL methanol:water (1:1, v/v; ULC/MS grade water, BioLab) containing as injection standard caffeine-d9 (1.5 μg/mL), vortexed, sonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 3,200×g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were transferred to 100 μL glass inserts in autosampler vials and directly used for LC-MS analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>TARA Gayoso</strong></p><p>For extracellular metabolite analysis, the 1 L aliquots of the 0.22 µm filtrates were loaded with a peristaltic pump onto SPE cartridges (Oasis HLB, 500 mg; Waters). For the conditioning and equilibration of the cartridges onboard, LC-MS grade water and methanol (Merck LiChrosolv) were used. After cartridges were washed and excess water removed, samples were stored at -20°C until further processing. Seven months later, the cartridges were thawed, washed with 18 mL water (Bio-Lab ULC-MS grade), dried, and eluted with 4 mL methanol.</p><p>Prior to LC-MS analysis, dried extracts were re-dissolved in 120 μL methanol:water (1:1, v:v) containing as injection standards caffeine-d9 (1.5 μg/mL) and tryptophan-d5 (2.1 µg/mL), vortexed, sonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 3,200×g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants (110 µL) were transferred to 100 µL glass inserts in autosampler vials and directly used for LC-MS analysis. The remaining 10 μL were combined into a pooled QC sample. A blank sample was prepared at station G4 by processing artificial seawater along with the biological samples.</p><p><br></p><p>[1] Kuhlisch, C. <em>et al. </em>Viral infection of algal blooms leaves a unique metabolic footprint on the dissolved organic matter in the ocean. <em>Sci. Adv.</em> <strong>7</strong>, eabf4680 (2021).</p>	<p>Extracts were analyzed at 1 µL injection volume (2 µL for the intracellular mesocosm samples) using a UPLC coupled to a photodiode detector (ACQUITY UPLC I-Class, Waters) and a quadrupole time-of-flight (QToF) mass spectrometer (SYNAPT G2 HDMS, Waters), as described previously [1]. Briefly, metabolites were separated using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters) attached to a VanGuard Pre-column (5 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters) with a column oven temperature of 35°C and a gradient of 5-100% acetonitrile at a flow of 0.3 mL/min leading to 40 min total run time. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in either acetonitrile:water (5:95, ν:ν, mobile phase A) or acetonitrile (mobile phase B). The chromatographic gradient was set to linear from 100% to 72% mobile phase A over 22 min and from 72% to 30% mobile phase A over 13.5 min, after which the column was first washed with 100% mobile phase B for 2 min and then returned to initial conditions (100% mobile phase A) and equilibrated for 1.5 min (40 min total run time). The PDA detector was set to 200-600 nm. A divert valve (Rheodine) excluded 0-1.2 min and 35-40 min from injection to the mass spectrometer.</p><p><br></p><p>[1] Kuhlisch, C. <em>et al. </em>Viral infection of algal blooms leaves a unique metabolic footprint on the dissolved organic matter in the ocean. <em>Sci. Adv.</em> <strong>7</strong>, eabf4680 (2021).</p>	<p>Metabolite profiling data was acquired using a SYNAPT G2 HDMS Quadrupole Time-of-Flight mass spectrometer (Waters), as described previously [1]. The ESI source was set to 140°C source and 450°C desolvation temperature, 1.5 kV capillary voltage, and 20 eV or 27 eV cone voltage (positive or negative ionization mode, respectively), using nitrogen as desolvation gas (800 L/h) and cone gas (53 L/h). The mass spectrometer was operated in separate acquisitions in full scan MSE positive and negative resolution mode (26,000 at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 556) over a mass range of 50-1600 Da alternating with 0.1 min scan time between low- (1 eV collision energy) and high-energy scan function (collision energy ramp of 10-45 eV in positive and 10-40 eV in negative ionization mode). For the intracellular mesocosm samples, LC-MS analysis was performed over 10 days with about 110 injections per batch in the positive and negative MS ionization mode including blanks, authentic standard mixes, reference material, and aliquots of the pooled QC sample.</p><p>LC-MS/MS analyses were performed using an injection volume of 4 μL for samples with high peak intensities from each sampling campaign. Data was acquired using an Orbitrap Exploris 240 (Thermo Scientific) in negative ionization mode for the deprotonated molecules. The ESI source was set to 275°C vaporizer temperature and 2.5 kV spray voltage. Full scan mode was acquired for 100-1,500 Da at 180,000 resolution and MS/MS analyses were performed at 10%, 15% and 20% HCD collision energy and 15,000 resolution.</p><p><br></p><p>[1] Kuhlisch, C. <em>et al.</em> Viral infection of algal blooms leaves a unique metabolic footprint on the dissolved organic matter in the ocean. <em>Sci. Adv.</em> <strong>7</strong>, eabf4680 (2021).</p>	<p>All Waters LC-MS files were converted from RAW binary files to open-format ‘mzXML’ files using the command line version of the ‘msconvert’ utility as part of the ProteoWizard toolkit. Conversion parameters were set as follows: 64 bit numeric accuracy, ‘zlib’ compression enabled, and ‘scanEvent’ filter set to ‘1’ corresponding with the MS1 acquisition channel.</p><p><br></p><p>All Thermo LC-MS/MS files were converted from .raw files to open-format ‘mzML’ files using MSConvert with the following parameters: 32-bit numeric accuracy, ‘zlib compression’ disabled, and 'peak picking' filter set to 'Vendor' for 'MS Levels 1-2'.</p>	<p>Metabolites were initially identified based on retention time, the presence of the precursor ion in positive and negative ionization modes, the isotope ratio of chlorine using the M, M+2, and M+4 isotopes, and the iodine fragment at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 126.905 in MSE in negative ionization mode. Metabolite identities were further confirmed using MS/MS analyses in negative ionization mode. Mass differences between the mesocosm and cruise samples were calculated for precursor and product ions, and the total number of matched peaks was enumerated.</p><p>By comparing the retention time, mass and isotope pattern of the precursor ion, and product ion spectra with the mesocosm reference sample, most chloro-iodo metabolites were identified in the environmental samples at a good confidence level. Metabolites were identified at a medium confidence, when &lt;3 product ions were present to support a match in retention time, precursor ion mass and isotope pattern.</p><p>Peak areas were integrated for the [M+H]+ or [M+H-H2O]+ adduct ions in positive ionization mode (for metabolites 3-16) and for the [M-H]- adduct ion in negative ionization mode (for metabolites 21-30) using QuanLynx. Peak areas above a certain signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio were normalized depending on the sampling campaign. The analysis of blank samples ruled out that the metabolites originated from sample collection and processing. All injection standards showed low relative standard deviations (3-7%).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Mesocosm</strong>: Peak areas above a signal-to-noise (S/N) threshold of 10 were normalized to the extraction standard (d2-IAA) and the filtration volume.</p><p><strong>NA-VICE</strong>: Peak areas above an S/N of 10 were normalized to the extraction standard (d2-IAA), as described previously [1].</p><p><strong>TARA Breizh</strong>: Peak areas above an S/N of 6 were normalized to the injection standard (positive ionization mode) or TIC (negative ionization mode).</p><p><strong>TARA Gayoso</strong>: Peak areas above an S/N of 6 were normalized to the injection standard (d5-tryptophan).</p><p><br></p><p>[1] Kuhlisch, C. <em>et al</em>.

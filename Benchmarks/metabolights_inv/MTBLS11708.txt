Study Identifier	MTBLS11708
Study Title	Gut microbiota regulates the brain metabolism of sexually mature drones
Study Description	<p>Previous studies have found that gut microbiota and metabolites may be crucial in insect sexual maturation. For example, during sexual maturity, the increased dopamine in the drone's brain can affect its reproductive behavior, such as flight, mating, etc. In addition, gut microbiota can affect brain functions such as learning and memory through metabolites. However, it is currently unclear about the changes in gut microbiota during drone sexual maturation and whether they can affect brain function through their metabolites. Thus, we analyzed gut microbiota and brain metabolome in drones during sexual maturation based on multi-omics and identified markers associated with sexual maturation. In addition, we also explored the potential correlation between differentially expressed microorganisms and metabolites before and after sexual maturation. The results showed significant changes in microbes (Lactobacillus, Chaetothyriales, Mortierella, Entrophospora), and metabolite pathways (glycerophospholipids), with substantial changes. Lactobacillus&nbsp;and Chaetothyriales&nbsp;showed the most significant changes and therefore can be used as marker bacteria in the sexual maturation of drones.&nbsp;In addition, PS (16:0/20:1(11Z)), PE (P-18:0/20:4) and LysoPC (P-18:0/0:0) showed significant differences in the variation of substances, which can be used as markers related to memory in sexually mature drones.&nbsp;This suggests that the intestinal flora of sexually mature drones undergoes substantial changes and has a stronger glycerophospholipid metabolism. Furthermore, microbial-metabolite correlation analysis revealed that Lactobacillus, Aspergillus, Mortierella and Pleosporales&nbsp;were strongly correlated with PC and PE in the glycerophospholipid metabolic pathway. Therefore, we hypothesize that these four species may affect the brain function of drones by influencing glycerophospholipid metabolism, thereby affecting their learning and memory behavior.&nbsp;These findings provide new insights into the interactions between sexual maturation, gut microbes and brain metabolism in drones.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>This experiment was conducted in June 2023 at the apiary of Kunming University of Technology. In total 6 healthy, disease-free honey bee colonies (Apis mellifera ligustica) were selected. Their colony strength, capper area and food supply were consistent among the selected colonies. The evening before the experiment commenced, a drone brood frame was introduced into each colony that had begun laying drone eggs and cleaned for 12 h. The queen bee was subsequently induced to lay eggs in the drone brood frame, and after 48&nbsp;h, the queen bee was removed, and the frame was placed in a queen-free area for further incubation and development. The frames containing drone pupae were removed from the hives 1 day before the drones emerged from the hive, and placed in a constant temperature and humidity box (with a temperature of 35 °C, relative humidity of 95%, no light). Newly fledged drones (0 days old) were painted on their thorax to determine their age, and then the marked 432 drones were introduced to 6 flat box colonies of 72 bees each, and a relay box was added to the flat box colonies to facilitate the flying and defecation of the drones. To avoid the repulsion of drones by worker bees as well as the exit of drones from the hive, the hive door of the flat box swarms was equipped with a deflowering sheet, and the swarms were also rewarded with feeding. The labeled drones&nbsp;were collected on days 3, 8 and 20, and 6 labeled drones&nbsp;were randomly collected from each box, quick-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments.</p>	<p>Briefly, 50-mg sample of drone brains was carefully weighed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube and 6-mm-diameter grinding beads were meticulously included. Extracts (400 μL; methanol:acetonitrile, 1:1, v/v) of four internal standards (L-2-chlorophenylalanine, etc) were added. The extracts underwent grinding with a cryogenic tissue grinder at -10 °C and 50 Hz for 6 min, followed by sonication extraction at 5 °C and 40 kHz for 30 min. After being stored at -20 °C for 30 min, the samples underwent centrifugation at 13,000 x g and 4 °C for 15 min, followed by transfer of the supernatant to a syringe vial equipped with an internal cannula for subsequent analysis. Furthermore, 20 μL of supernatant was extracted from each sample, combined, and utilized as a quality control sample as described in reference.</p>	<p>In total 3 μL of the sample was separated on an HSS T3 column (100 mm x 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 µm) and passed into mass spectrometry. Mobile phase A was 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), and mobile phase B was 47.5% acetonitrile + 47.5% isopropanol + 5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid). The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min and the column temperature was 40 °C.</p>	<p>The mass spectrometer used was a Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). Samples were subjected to electrospray ionization, and mass spectra were acquired in positive (+) and negative (-) ion scanning modes. The scan range was 70-1050 m/z, the sheath gas flow rate was 50 arb, the auxiliary gas flow rate was 13 arb, the heating temperature was 425 °C, the capillary temperature was 325 °C and the S-lens voltage was 50. The spray voltage was 3500 V in the positive mode (+) and -3500 V in the negative mode (-). The normalized collision energy was 20-40-60 V cyclic collision energy. The resolution of the primary mass spectrum was 60,000, the resolution of the secondary mass spectrum was 7500, and the data were collected in DDA mode.</p>	<p>The raw data were imported into the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction and peak alignment / pairing to obtain a data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio and peak intensity information.</p>	<p>The MS and MS/MS mass spectra were matched to public databases, such as&nbsp;METLIN, the human metabolome database (HMDB) and&nbsp;LIPID MAPS, with the MS mass error set to less than 10 ppm, and the metabolites being identified based on the secondary mass spectra matching score.</p>

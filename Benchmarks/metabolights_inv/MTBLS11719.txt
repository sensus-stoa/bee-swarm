Study Title	Impact of shear stress and moderate electric field on the global metabolic profile of a blended fruit and vegetable juice
Study Description	This study investigated the impact of moderate electric field (MEF) and shear stress (SS) on the chemical profile of a blended fruit and vegetable juice using untargeted metabolomics. Samples underwent mild thermal treatment, MEF, and a combination of MEF and SS, with subsequent chemical changes analyzed through liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Principal component analysis revealed distinct clustering based on treatment type, indicating that processing with MEF and MEF+SS altered the juice metabolite composition compared to both fresh juice and sample subjected to mild thermal treatment. Notably, anthocyanins underwent degradation, with MEF and MEF+SS treatments resulting in increased rates of compound breakdown and/or oxidation. Furthermore, MEF and MEF+SS treated samples had higher levels of some phenolic acids, such as gallic acid and caffeic acid, suggesting enhanced bioaccessibility and bioavailability. These findings suggest that MEF, both with and without SS, induces significant non-thermal effects, contributing to the degradation or release of key metabolites. This work underscores the potential of MEF and MEF+SS technologies to modify food composition during processing, offering new opportunities for optimizing food quality.

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Sample preparation</strong></p><p>A blended juice, composed of apples (58%), leaves of red kale (9%), blackberries (11%), lime juice (7%), ginger (1%) and water (13%), was prepared using a domestic juice extractor (Hamilton Beach Juicer Machine, 800W). All fruits and vegetables were purchased in a local market (Columbus, OH, US). Samples were homogenized, packaged in 50 mL aliquots in falcon tubes, and stored at - 18°C. For the experiments, samples were thawed in a water bath, homogenized, and maintained in an ice bath until processing. All experiments were conducted with 5 replicates.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Sample processing</strong></p><p>Samples were treated by 3 different technologies: mild thermal treatment (MT -see details below), moderate electric field (MEF) and moderate electric field associated with shear stress (MEF+SS).&nbsp;The objective was to evaluate the separate contributions of individual process parameters (temperature, MEF, and shear stress) and their effects on product quality. Fresh (untreated but frozen) juice (FJ) serves as a control. Figure 1 summarizes all treatments evaluated and the processing conditions applied.</p><p>&nbsp;MEF and MEF+SS technologies were applied in the apparatus described by Mok et al. (2019). The electrical setup consisted of a direct current (DC) power supply, which converted the single-phase line voltage into a regulated DC voltage. This DC voltage then powered insulated gate bipolar transistor (IGBT) switching modules, which generated alternating square waveforms ranging from 0 to 20 kHz. The blended juice was treated within the treatment chamber, which consisted of concentric electrode cylinders, made of graphite (Ohio Carbon Blank, Willoughby, OH, USA), and a cooling jacket, connected to a water bath used for temperature control.&nbsp;There was an annular gap width of 10.1 mm between the stationary outer cylinder (Ro =22.8 mm, L =90 mm) and a rotating inner cylinder (Ri = 12.7 mm, H =77 mm).&nbsp;The treatment chamber lid had a small hole at the top for sample introduction and temperature measurement during the treatment.</p><p>For MEF treatments, samples were subjected to alternating square waveform pulses at a frequency of 60 Hz for 10 min at a voltage of 80 V. [SKS1]&nbsp;For MEF+SS treatments, the same conditions were applied, along with shear rates of 19.71 s−1 (corresponding to 1172 rpm). The temperature during treatments was 40 ± 3 °C. These conditions were selected based on Mok et al. (2019, 2020, 2021), who reported synergistic antimicrobial effects of MEF and SS against Escherichia coli K12 and Listeria innocua in apple and apple-kale blend juices.</p><p>&nbsp;The mild thermal treatment was performed at 40 °C as a control, matching the temperature of both MEF and MEF+SS treatments. This setup enables the isolated evaluation of temperature effects. This treatment will be referred to as conventional treatment, as it did not use any emerging technology and was conducted solely to assess the effect of the temperature, relying solely on heat transfer to achieve the desired condition.</p><p>Samples (3 mL) were placed in an aluminum thermal death time (TDT) tube (inside diameter of 15 mm, 3 mm of thickness and 39 mm of height) (Dhakal et al., 2018) and were soaked into a water bath set at 42°C (NESLAB, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). A K-type thermocouple was inserted into the TDT for monitoring and recording sample temperature using a data logger thermometer (HH 374, Omega Engineering Inc., Stamford, Connecticut, USA).&nbsp;</p><p>&nbsp;For all treatments, the holding time of 10 min was counted after sample reached the desired target temperature of 40 °C.&nbsp;The come-up time (CUT) (time for the sample to reach the target temperature) was approximately 15 s for the mild thermal treatment and for 150 s for MEF and MEF+SS treatments. After all treatments, samples were cooled down to 10 °C using an ice bath.</p><p>&nbsp;[SKS1]Was this done without rotating the cylinder?&nbsp;In that case, the jacket temperature and/or field strength would have had to be adjusted because the heat transfer would have been slow.&nbsp;How different were these values from the MEF+SS treatment?</p>	<p><strong>Extraction of polar metabolites</strong></p><p> Samples were extracted using the protocol described by Teegarden et al. (2019). Briefly, samples were mixed with a methanol (final concentration of 3:1 methanol:water) to extract as many small molecular weight metabolites as possible. Samples were homogenized using a SPEX Geno/Grinder 2010 for 1 min at 1400 rpm (SPEX Sample Prep, Metuchen, NJ, USA), and centrifuged at 21,130 × g for 5 min at 4 °C (Thermo Scientific, Sorvall™ Legend™ Micro 21R). The extracts were diluted with water to reach 1:1 water:methanol, and transferred to HPLC vials. Pooled quality control (QC) samples were produced by combining aliquots from each individual sample. Additionally, solvent and process blanks (the latter prepared by following the same extraction procedure but replacing juice by water) were used to identify any features originating from laboratory consumables (Broadhurst et al., 2018).</p>	<p><strong>Data acquisition</strong></p><p> Untargeted full-scan data were obtained using an ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (Agilent 1290 Infinity II, UHPLC) system coupled with a quadrupole time of flight mass spectrometer (QTOF-MS) (Agilent 6546, QTOF-MS). Samples were randomized for the run order, and analytes were separated on a Waters (Milford, MA, USA) Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 μm particle size), maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of: (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient program was as follows: 0% B held for 0.5 min, a linear increase to 100% B over 8 min, 100% B held for 1 min, followed by a return to 0% B for 1 additional minute (Bilbrey et al., 2021). Samples were kept at 8 °C, and the injection volume was 10 μL. </p>	<p> The QTOF-MS (Agilent 6546, QTOF-MS) was operated in both negative and positive electrospray ionization modes, collecting data over a mass-to-charge (m/z) range of 50 to 1700 u. The gas temperature was set at 350 °C with a flow rate of 10 L min−1. The nebulizer gas pressure was maintained at 35 psi. The sheath gas was set to a temperature of 375 °C with a flow rate of 11 L min-1. QC was repeatedly injected to condition the instrument, until the base peak chromatograms stabilized. Additionally, QC samples were analyzed every six injections (i.e., once per hour) to monitor intra-run variability.</p><p> The MS/MS data for QC samples were acquired using the same MS1 settings, utilizing the iterative data-dependent acquisition mode. These experiments were conducted in positive and negative ionization modes with five injections for each collision energy (20 and 40 eV). The parameters for AutoMS2 scans were as follows: MS range from 40 to 1700 m/z, MS scan rate of 3 spectra/s, MS/MS range from 40 to 1700 m/z, MS/MS scan rate of 1 spectra/s, and an isolation width set to narrow (~1.3 amu). The decision engine was set to advanced. The precursor selection criteria included a maximum of 2 precursors per cycle, an absolute threshold of 10,000, a relative threshold of 0.100%, and enabled precursor abundance-based scan speed. The target was set to 100,000 counts per spectrum, without using an MS/MS accumulation time limit or dynamic precursor rejection. Purity stringency was set to 100%, with a purity cutoff at 30%, using a common isotope model for small molecules. Active exclusion was enabled, with precursors excluded after 2 spectra and released after 0.12 minutes, and the precursors were sorted by abundance only.</p>	<p><strong>Data pre-processing </strong></p><p>Data obtained from the UHPLC-QTOF-MS analysis, originally in Agilent files (.d), were converted to *.mzML format using ProteoWizard software (Chambers et al., 2012). The converted data were then processed using the open-source software MZMine 3.3.0 (Schmid et al., 2023) for peak deconvolution. This process deconvolutes LC-MS spectral features based on expected isotope patterns, adducts, and charge states, and aligns them across all samples. The workflow included mass detection using wavelets (Automated Data Analysis Pipeline, ADAP), encompassing feature detection, isotope grouping, alignment, gap filling, and filtering. All parameters are described in Supplementary Material, Tables S1 and S2. Finally, the feature table (*.csv) was exported for data analysis.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Data analysis</strong></p><p> Data filtering and analysis were conducted using R (version 4.4.0). First, data was normalized regarding sample mass weight (mass) and total soluble solids content (SST) (data described in Table 2). For that, the signal intensities of each metabolite was multiplied by a factor that includes the both mass of the sample from which it was derived and by sample SST measured after treatment (factor = 40/(mass x SST). Then, features that did not exhibit at least a 10-fold increase in quality control samples (QCs) compared to process blanks were excluded from the analysis. Subsequently, the analysis was limited to features present in 100% of the QCs and features with a coefficient of variation lower than 30% in the QCs. The final feature table with MS data for negative and positive modes can be found in Tables S5 and S6, respectively (Supplementary Material). Missing values were imputed with half of the lowest peak area value in the dataset. All data were log2-transformed and median-centered prior to analysis. Data was clustered with the notame R package (Klåvus et al., 2020). &nbsp;Principal components analysis (PCA) was conducted as a dimensionality reduction tool, and for observing clusters in the data, and visualized using ggplot2 (Hadley Wickham, 2016). For a feature-wise analysis, unpaired t-tests with the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate (FDR) multiple testing correction were performed using rstatix package in R (Kassambara, A., 2023).</p>	<p><strong>Annotation and molecular networking</strong></p><p> Metabolites of interest were annotated using different approaches, according to the Metabolomics Standard Initiative guidelines (Sumner et al., 2007). For level 1 identification, sample data (QC samples) were compared to an in-house compound library of standards (see 2.1), using retention time and spectra data (MS and MS/MS). </p><p> For level 2 metabolite annotation, Agilent files (.d) containing raw MS/MS data were converted to *.mzML format using ProteoWizard applications (Chambers et al., 2012). The raw spectral data were then deconvoluted using MZmine 3.3.0 (Tables S3 and S4, Supplementary Material). Negative and positive ionization modes were processed separately to generate two peak lists for metabolite identification. This was achieved through various library search algorithms available on the Global Natural Products Social (GNPS) platform (M. Wang et al., 2016). Three GNPS algorithms were employed: Classical Molecular Networking, Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN), and Library Search. To generate the molecular network, a mass tolerance of 0.02 Da for precursor ions was used. Ion fragment mass variances were set to 0.02 Da for clustering each group of acquired MS/MS spectra Nodes were connected only if the cosine score exceeded 0.7, with a minimum of 4 matching peaks in the fragmentation spectrum. The molecular network spectra were then compared to the GNPS spectral libraries, where the same data parameters were applied to the sample spectra.</p>

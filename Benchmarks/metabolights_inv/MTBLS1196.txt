Study Identifier	MTBLS1196
Study Title	New insights into the responses of soil microorganisms to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon stress by combining enzyme activity and sequencing analysis with metabolomics
Study Description	Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), some of the most widespread organic contaminants, are highly toxic to soil microorganisms. Whether long-term polluted soils can still respond to the fresh input of pollutants is unknown. In this study, the soil enzyme activity, soil microbial community structure and function and microbial metabolism pathways were examined to systematically investigate the responses of soil microorganisms to fresh PAH stress. Microbial activity as determined by soil dehydrogenase and urease activity was inhibited upon microbe exposure to PAH stress. In addition, the soil microbial community and function were obviously shifted under PAH stress. Both microbial diversity and richness were decreased by PAH stress. Rhizobacter, Sphingobium, Mycobacterium, Massilia, Bacillus and Pseudarthrobacter were significantly affected by PAH stress and can be considered important indicators of PAH contamination in agricultural soils. Moreover, the majority of microbial metabolic function predicted to respond to PAH stress were affected adversely. Finally, soil metabolomics further revealed specific inhibition of soil metabolism pathways associated with fatty acids, carbohydrates and amino acids. Therefore, the soil metabolic composition distinctively changed, reflecting a change in the soil metabolism. In summary, fresh contaminant introduction into long-term polluted soils inhibited microbial activity and metabolism, which might profoundly affect the whole soil quality.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Soil was sampled from an agricultural soil around a steel plant with long-term PAH exposure. All soil samples were air-dried and passed through a 60-mesh nylon sieve after the removal of visible plant debris for further experiments. Standard methods based on the Chinese Society of Soil Science protocols were used to determine the basic soil properties<strong>[1]</strong> as described in our previous study<strong>[2]</strong>. Specifically, the soil studied was a silty clay loam with a pH (soil:liquid (0.5 M CaCl2) = 1:2.5 (w/v)) of 6.62 and a total organic carbon (TOC) content of 18220 mg/kg. The total nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium contents of the soil were 1000 mg/kg, 620 mg/kg and 16950 mg/kg, respectively. The initial soil contained 0.19 ± 0.04 mg/kg phenanthrene, 2.8 ± 0.64 mg/kg pyrene and 1.1 ± 0.68 mg/kg benzo (a) pyrene and was designated the Control soil (CK). A fresh PAH stress treatment (ST) was prepared by spiking phenanthrene, pyrene and benzo(a)pyrene into soils. In the ST soil, the initial concentrations of phenanthrene, pyrene and benzo(a)pyrene were 47.36 ± 0.34 mg/kg, 50.13 ± 1.42 mg/kg and 9.66 ± 0.77 mg/kg, respectively. Soils in the 2 treatments were incubated under greenhouse conditions with 30% moisture content for 100 d. At the end of incubation, the soil was sampled to determine the soil enzyme activity, microbial community structure and metabolites, and all soil samples were stored at -80 °C until analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Lu R.K, 1999. Analytical methods for soils and agricultural chemistry. China Agricultural Science and Technology Press.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Li X, Song Y, Wang F, Bian Y, Jiang X. Combined effects of maize straw biochar and oxalic acid on the dissipation of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and microbial community structures in soil: A mechanistic study. J Hazard Mater. 2019 Feb 15;364:325-331. doi:10.1016/j.jhazmat.2018.10.041. PMID:30384242.</p><p><br></p>	<p>The extraction of soil metabolites was performed as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, the soil samples were mixed with extracting solution (methanol:H2O = 3:1 (v/v)) at a 1:1 (g/v) ratio in a 5 ml Eppendorf (EP) tube. Adonitol (0.5 mg/ml) was added as an internal standard and vortexed with 1 ml of ethyl acetate for 30 s. A ball mill at 45 Hz was used to homogenize samples for 4 min, followed by a 5 min ultrasound treatment (incubated in ice water). The mixed samples were centrifuged at 16000 x g and 4 °C for 15 min, and then the supernatants were transferred to a new EP tube for extraction. The ball mix extraction process was repeated 3 times, and all the supernatants of each extraction were mixed together and dried completely with a vacuum concentrator. Then, 0.03 ml of methoxylamine hydrochloride (20 mg/ml in pyridine) was added to dissolve the metabolites, and the sample was incubated at 80 °C for 30 min.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Swenson TL, Northen TR. Untargeted Soil Metabolomics Using Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry and Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry. Methods Mol Biol. 2019;1859:97-109. doi:10.1007/978-1-4939-8757-3_4. PMID:30421224.</p>	<p>The MS data were acquired using a LECO Pegasus HT time-of-flight mass spectrometer in full-scan mode with m/z ranging from 50 to 500 at a rate of 20 spectra/s and a solvent delay of 6.17 min.</p>	<p>Chroma TOF software (V4.3X, LECO Corporation) was used for raw peak extraction, data baseline filtering, calibration of the baseline, peak alignment, deconvolution analysis, peak identification and integration of the peak area based on the LECO-Fiehn Rtx5 database.</p>	<p>A gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890, Santa Clara, USA) equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column coated with 5% diphenyl cross linked with 95% dimethylpolysiloxane (30 m x 0.25 mm inner diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness, J&amp;W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA) and coupled with a Pegasus HT time-of-flight mass spectrometer (GC-TOF-MS, LECO, St. Joseph, MI) was used to determine the composition of soil metabolites. Helium was used as the carrier gas with a column flow of 1 ml/min.</p>	<p>During metabolite identification, both the MS match and retention index match were considered. The accuracy of the discriminating compound identification was ascertained by a similarity value (SV) generated by comparison with the LECO-Fiehn Metabolomics Library. Specifically, an SV &gt; 700 indicated that metabolite identification was reliable, an SV &lt; 200 meant that the identified metabolite was 'unreliable', and the annotation of a compound with an SV between 200 and 700 was considered putative. The metabolite identification and naming comply with the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) convention<strong>[1]</strong>, and detailed SV values of all identified metabolites are shown in <strong>Supplementary Information 2</strong> in the paper associated with this study.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Sumner LW, Amberg A, Barrett D, Beale MH, Beger R, Daykin CA, Fan TW, Fiehn O, Goodacre R, Griffin JL, Hankemeier T, Hardy N, Harnly J, Higashi R, Kopka J, Lane AN, Lindon JC, Marriott P, Nicholls AW, Reily MD, Thaden JJ, Viant MR. Proposed minimum reporting standards for chemical analysis Chemical Analysis Working Group (CAWG) Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI). Metabolomics. 2007 Sep;3(3):211-221. doi:10.1007/s11306-007-0082-2. PMID:24039616.</p>

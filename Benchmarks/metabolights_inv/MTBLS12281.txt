Study Identifier	MTBLS12281
Study Title	Seasonal and day/night variations of microbial metabolic functioning in simulated cloud water
Study Description	<p>Microorganisms maintain metabolic activity in clouds, with recognized impacts on chemical reactivity by contributing to the processing of small organic compounds, radicals and their precursors. However, the conditions in clouds can be highly contrasted (temperature, light, etc), and it remains unknown how these influences microbial metabolism. Here we explored the functioning of microorganisms exposed to typical cloud water conditions through transcriptomics and metabolomics. Mixed cell suspensions of representative microbial isolates from cloud water, composed of a strain of basidiomycetous fungi (Dioszegia hungarica) and three strains of bacteria (Rhodococcus enclensis, Pseudomonas syringae and P. graminis) were prepared in artificial cloud water, and exposed to combined contrasted conditions of temperature (5 vs 17 °C), oxidants (0 vs 250 µM H2O2) and light (dark vs artificial solar light) to mimic cloud conditions during winter night (WN) and summer day (SD). Differential metabolomics and transcriptomics allowed to highlight 25 differently abundant metabolites and 218 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between these conditions of incubation. In SD: the fungi overexpressed genes of the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation chain, fatty acids biosynthesis and succinate assimilation enzymes. the detection by LC-MS of 3 acylcarnitines supports the occurrence of fatty acid transports into the mitochondrion, related with energy production or possibly hydrogen peroxide scavenging. In bacteria, DEGs encoding components of ROS scavenging systems were predominant. In WN: in the yeast, DEGs encoding chaperones, proteins of the ribosome and proteasome were overexpressed, suggesting high protein turnover. For bacteria, protein rescue processes targeting oxidation-sensitive sulphur containing proteins were more expressed, and DL-methionine sulfoxide was detected. Half of the identified bacterial DEG colocalised in 17 clusters suggesting coordinated gene expression. This study demonstrates that eukaryotes and prokaryotes may engage in distinct strategies to survive in clouds depending on environmental conditions.</p>

Atmospheric Chem Phys. 2024:24(9):5181–5198. https://doi.org/10.5194/acp-24-5181-2024</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Preparation of cell suspensions</strong></p><p>Individual strains were grown from glycerol stock in liquid R2A medium at 17°C, 200rpm agitation, until mid-log phase. The cells were then harvested by centrifugation (8,000 xg, 3 min), twice rinsed and then resuspended in 5X MCW incubation medium at&nbsp;concentrations estimated a priori based on OD600 and then by flow cytometry. Desired volumes of these initial individual cell suspensions were added to 5X MCW solution to reach the target cell concentrations. The achieved concentration of each strain in the initial consortium was evaluated by plate-count of the mixed cell suspension on R2A medium and incubation for 6 days at 17°C (strains consist of different phenotypes and colours and are easily distinguishable visually from each others).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Experimental strategy</strong></p><p>The general experimental strategy consisted of assemblages of mixed microbial cell suspensions incubated in freshly prepared 5X synthetic marine cloud water (MCW) solution either at 5°C in the dark (winter night, WN condition), or at 17°C under artificial solar light irradiation and added with 250&nbsp;µM H2O2 (summer day, SD condition). The solution was distributed into 40 mL volumes in microcosms consisting of dark Erlenmeyer flasks for WN, or custom-made quartz lidded photo-bioreactors for SD [1]. Abiotic controls consisted of similar solutions incubated in parallel in the absence of cells. At dedicated times of incubation (e.g., just before incubation, and after 1h, 2h, 3h, 4h, 5h and 24h), microcosms were sacrificed for chemical analyses; the incubation was stopped when less than 20% of H2O2 remained in the SD microcosms (i.e., ~3.5h or 4h).</p><p><br></p><p>[1] Vaïtilingom M, Deguillaume L, Vinatier V, Sancelme M, Amato P, Chaumerliac N, and Delort A-M. Potential impact of microbial activity on the oxidant capacity and organic carbon budget in clouds. Proc Natl Acad Sci. 2013:110(2):559–564. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1205743110</p>	<p>For metabolomics, triplicated microcosms (i.e., 40 mL total volume per replicate) were centrifuged (10,000 x g, 4 min) to collect cells, then the pellets were rinsed with 0.8% w/v sterile NaCl, centrifuged again and stored at -80 °C until metabolome extraction.</p><p>Metabolomes were extracted with 1.2 mL of a cold solvent mixture of water/methanol/acetonitrile [1:2:2]. Samples were immersed in liquid nitrogen and thawed in ice. Then they were thoroughly vortexed and centrifuged to retrieve supernatant (10 min at 10,000 x g). The supernatant was transferred into a new tube and evaporated. Finally, the dry extracts were resuspended in 50 µL of 50/50 acetonitrile/Water (v/v) and 0.1% of formic acid. In total 5 µL of extract were injected.</p>	<p>Analyses were performed on an UHPLC Ultimate 3000 system (Thermo FisherScientific) coupled to a TimsTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker) equipped with an electrospray (ESI) source and operating in the positive ion mode. The chromatographic separation was performed using an Acquity HSST3 column (2.1 x 150 mm; 1.8&nbsp;µm-Waters) operating at 30 °C and an injection volume of 5&nbsp;µL. The flow rate was fixed at 0.4 mL/min with 0.1% of formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% of formic acid in acetonitrile (B) for mobile phases at the following gradient: initial, 0.0-2.0 min, 100% A; 2.0-15.0 min linear gradient to 0% A; 15.0-22.0 min, 0% A; 22.0-22.1 min linear gradient to 100% A; following by 4.0 min of washing and reconditioning of the column equilibration wash with 100% A. The detection was performed with full scan from 50 to 1000 m/z with capillary voltage at 4 kV and a capillary temperature set at 200 °C.</p>	<p>Analyses were performed on an UHPLC Ultimate 3000 system (Thermo FisherScientific) coupled to a TimsTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker) equipped with an electrospray (ESI) source and operating in the positive ion mode. The detection was performed with full scan from 50 to 1000 m/z with capillary voltage at 4 kV and a capillary temperature set at 200 °C.</p>	<p>Raw data were converted to network common data form (NetCDF) in order to process them using the web-based platform Galaxy Workflow4Metabolomics (W4M) framework[1]&nbsp;W4M is a collaborative online research resource for comprehensive metabolomics data analyses. XCMS software (version 3.12.0) implanted in the W4M platform was used to preprocess raw data for feature detection, alignment, and grouping and missing peak filling[2].</p><p><br></p><p>Refs:</p><p>[1]Giacomoni F, Le Corguillé G, Monsoor M, Landi M, Pericard P, Pétéra M, Duperier C, Tremblay-Franco M, Martin J-F, Jacob D, et al. Anal Chem. 2006:78(3):779–787. https://doi.org/10.1021/ac051437y</p>	<p>Identification experiments were performed on a TimsTOF mass spectrometer with acquisitions MS2 carried out for a limited selection of samples of interest. Metabolites were first identified using an in-house database containing the reference spectra of more than 1000 authentic standard compounds analysed in the same analytical conditions as UPLC-MS profiling.</p><p>Database queries were performed with a mass error of 0.005 Da and a retention time difference of 0.3 min. Database results were confirmed using appropriate standards when available, isotopic patterns and mass fragmentation analyses using collision induced dissociation MS2 data acquired on the same sample. Metabolites were classified accordingly to Sumner et al.,[1] concerning the levels of confidence in the identification process.</p><p><br></p><p>Ref:</p><p>[1] Sumner, LW., Amberg, A., Barrett, D., Beale, MH., Beger, R.,&nbsp;Daykin, CA., Fan, TW-M., Fiehn, O., Goodacre, R., Griffin, JL., Hankemeier, T., Hardy, N., Harnly, J., Higashi, R., Kopka, J., Lane, AN., Lindon, JC., Marriott, P., Nicholls, AW., Reily, MD., Thaden, JJ., Viant, MR.&nbsp;Proposed minimum reporting standards for chemical analysis.&nbsp;Metabolomics&nbsp;3, 211–221 (2007).</p>

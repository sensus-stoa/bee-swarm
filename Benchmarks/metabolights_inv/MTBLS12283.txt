Study Identifier	MTBLS12283
Study Title	Protozoal populations drive system-wide variation in the rumen microbiome
Study Description	<p>While rapid progress has been made to characterize the bacterial and archaeal populations of the rumen microbiome, insight into how they interact with keystone protozoal species remains elusive. Here, we reveal two distinct rumen community types (RCT-A and RCT-B) that are not strongly associated with host phenotype nor genotype but instead linked to protozoal community patterns. We leveraged a series of multi-omic datasets to show that the dominant Epidinium spp. in animals with RCT-B employ a plethora of fiber-degrading enzymes that present enriched Prevotella spp. a favorable carbon landscape to forage upon. Conversely, animals with RCT-A, dominated by genera Isotricha and Entodinium, harbor a more even distribution of fiber, protein, and amino acid metabolizers, reflected by higher detection of metabolites from both protozoal and bacterial activity. We reveal that microbiome variation transcends key protozoal and bacterial populations, which should act as an important consideration for future development of microbiome-based technologies.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Experimental design and measurement of key performance traits:</strong></p><p>An initial group of 80 animals representing 2 breeds of beef cattle <strong>Aberdeen-Angus cross (AAX) (n = 40)</strong> and<strong> Luing (n = 40)</strong> was selected for the experiment. Of these, 71 (<strong>AAX: n = 36</strong>; <strong>Luing: n = 35</strong>) successfully completed the designed sampling scheme. All animals were provided a typical basal diet consisting of whole crop barley (300 g/kg DM), grass silage (200 g/kg DM), barley (355 g/kg DM), maize dark grains (120 g/kg DM), molasses (15 g/kg DM) and minerals (10 g/kg DM). For half of the animals, the experimental design originally involved supplementation with <em>Asparagopsis taxiformis</em> red algae vegetative tissue (thallus) at 0.3% of the organic mass (OM). A. taxiformis is a feed additive which has been shown to reduce methane emissions in past studies22–25. However, due to adverse effects observed in animals during the planned 3-wk seaweed adaptation phase, supplementation was terminated after just 15 d. All animals were given a further 5 wk to adapt to basal feed before performance testing was carried out. Due to this delay, the heaviest 32 animals, balanced for breed, underwent a shorter performance test period of 4 wk instead of the normal 8 wk. During performance testing, daily feed intake was recorded using electronic feeders (HOKO, Insentec, Marknesse, The Netherlands). </p><p><br></p><p>Twice weekly, duplicated samples of each diet component were collected to determine dry matter content and to calculated dry matter intake (DMI). The body weight of each animal was measured weekly to estimate average daily gain (ADG) using a linear regression model including time on test. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated for each animal as average daily DMI divided by ADG.</p><p><br></p><p>At the end of the experimental period, the animals’ methane emissions were measured in respiration chambers. 1 wk prior to entering the respiration chambers, the cattle were single-housed in training pens, identical in size and shape to the pens inside the chamber, to adapt to individual housing. The cattle were allocated to 6 respiration chambers based on the criterion of minimization of the variation in body weight. They remained in the respiration chambers for 3 d, which included one day for adaptation and a 48-h measurement period for methane emissions.</p><p><br></p><p>Of the 71 animals that completed the trial, 24 were selected for multi-omic analysis, including equal numbers of the 2 breeds, and representing the full range of methane emissions. For a further subset of 6 animals (out of 24), samples were also analyzed for a time series collected during the experimental period using orogastric tubing, as described below.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Rumen content and tissue sample collection:</strong></p><p>On live animals, longitudinal rumen fluid samples (50 ml) were collected using a stomach tube (16 x 2700 mm Equivet Stomach Tube; Jørgen Kruuse A/S, Langeskov, Denmark) nasally and aspirating manually. Samples were collected prior to the adaptation phase to seaweed, before and after the performance test as well as immediately after leaving the respiration chambers. Additionally, rumen fluid samples (50 ml) were obtained after the animals were slaughtered in a commercial abattoir, immediately after the rumen was opened to be drained. Immediately after sampling, the rumen digesta was filtered through two layers of muslin and a 5 ml sample of the filtered liquid was transferred into a 30 ml universal containing 10 ml of PBS-Glycerol, then stored in a freezer at -80 °C.</p><p><br></p><p>Rumen cell wall samples were collected from the central region of the ventral sac before the rumen had been washed. The ruminal tissue was dipped into a 125 ml beaker containing a PBS solution to remove the ruminal digesta. The tissue was sliced using a sterile scalpel and transferred to a 30 ml universal tube containing 5 ml RNALater. Additionally, liver samples were taken by the meat inspector, with a section cut out using a sterile scalpel and then stored in a 30 ml universal tube with 5 ml RNALater. All tissue samples were stored in a freezer at -80 °C before being analyzed. Further details regarding the sampling and experimental procedures carried out at SRUC can be found in previously published studies<strong>[1][2]</strong> which followed a similar protocol.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Roehe R, Dewhurst RJ, Duthie CA, Rooke JA, McKain N, Ross DW, Hyslop JJ, Waterhouse A, Freeman TC, Watson M, Wallace RJ. Bovine Host Genetic Variation Influences Rumen Microbial Methane Production with Best Selection Criterion for Low Methane Emitting and Efficiently Feed Converting Hosts Based on Metagenomic Gene Abundance. PLoS Genet. 2016 Feb 18;12(2):e1005846. doi:10.1371/journal.pgen.1005846. PMID:26891056.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Duthie CA, Troy SM, Hyslop JJ, Ross DW, Roehe R, Rooke JA. The effect of dietary addition of nitrate or increase in lipid concentrations, alone or in combination, on performance and methane emissions of beef cattle. Animal. 2018 Feb;12(2):280-287. doi:10.1017/S175173111700146X. PMID:28701247.</p><p><br></p>	<p>4 different bovine sample types were extracted.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Bovine intestinal fluids:</strong></p><p>Insoluble material was filtered through Spin-X centrifuge tube filters (22 μm, Corning Costar) by centrifugation (3,000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C, Fisher Scientific Mega Star 600R42309168). Prior to analysis (reverse phase) the samples (20 μl) were diluted in solvent A A (80 μl, 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in water) and stable isotope-labelled internal standards were added to all samples (10 μl in water). For analysis, 2.5 ul were injected on the analytical column.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Bovine rumen digesta:</strong></p><p>Metabolite extraction of rumen digesta fluids (200 μl) was performed with pre-cooled methanol and water containing stable isotope-labelled internal standards (200 μl, 2:1 vol/vol, -20 °C). Stainless steel beads were added and samples were homogenized by 4 cycles of beating (4 x 60 s at 30 Hz) with cooling in between. Insoluble material and beads were pelleted by centrifugation (3,000 x g, 5 min, 4 °C, Fisher Scientific Mega Star 600R42309168). Supernatants were passed through Phree phospholipid removal cartridges (Phenomenex) using centrifugation (3,000 x g, 5 min, 4 °C). Prior to analysis (reverse phase) the samples (10 μl) were diluted in solvent A A (80 μl, 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in water). Stable isotope-labelled internal standards were added to all samples (10 μl in water). For analysis, 5 ul were injected on the analytical column.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Bovine rumen wall and liver tissue samples:</strong></p><p>Metabolite extraction of accurately weight liver and rumen wall samples (~50 mg) was performed with pre-cooled methanol and water containing stable isotope-labelled internal standards (300 μl, 2:1 vol/vol, -20 °C). Stainless steel beads were added, and samples were homogenized by 4 beating cycles (4 x 60 s at 30 Hz) with cooling in between. Samples were ultrasonicated to enhance extraction (5 min). Insoluble material and beads were pelleted by centrifugation (3,000 x g, 5 min, 4 °C, Fisher Scientific Mega Star 600R42309168) and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. The extraction procedure using methanol and water (2:1 vol/vol, -20 °C ) was repeated on the pellet. The combined supernatants were passed through Phree phospholipid removal cartridges (Phenomenex) using centrifugation (3,000 x g, 5 minutes, 4 °C) and an aliquot of the filtrate (200 ul) was evaporated under a gentle stream of nitrogen. Prior to analysis, the samples were reconstituted in a constant ratio of solvent to initial tissue weight (6:1 wt/wt), vortexed, and filtered through Spin-X filters (22 μm, Corning Costar) by centrifugation (3,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, Fisher Scientific Mega Star 600R42309168). A filtrate aliquot (10 μl ) was diluted in solvent A (80 μl, 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in water). Stable isotope-labelled internal standards were added to all samples (10 μl in water). For analysis, 5 ul were injected on the analytical column.</p><p><br></p>	<p>Untargeted metabolomic analyses was performed by <strong>MS-Omics</strong> (part of cmbio, Vedbæk, Denmark). All samples were analyzed using a Thermo Scientific Vanquish LC coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 240 MS instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). An electrospray ionization interface was used as the ionization source. Analysis was performed in positive and negative ionization mode under polarity switching. The semi-polar metabolites were analysed via reverse phase Ultra-performance liquid chromatography performed using an adapted version of the protocol previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. Eluent A consisted of 10 mM ammonium formate in water with 0.1% formic acid, and eluent B consisted of 10 mM ammonium formate in methanol with 0.1% formic acid.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Doneanu CE, Chen W, Mazzeo JR. UPLC-MS Monitoring of Water-Soluble Vitamin Bs in Cell Culture Media in Minutes, 2011, Waters Application Note720004042. <a href='https://www.waters.com/content/dam/waters/en/app-notes/2011/720004042/720004042-en.pdf' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.waters.com/content/dam/waters/en/app-notes/2011/720004042/720004042-en.pdf</a></p>	<p>For semi-polar metabolite analysis, the Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) was operated with the following parameters: full MS scan, a resolution of 60,000, AGC target Standard, maximum injection time mode auto, and a scan range of 65 to 975 m/z. <strong>Data-dependent MS/MS (dd-MS2)</strong> parameters included a TopN of 5, resolution of 30,000, AGC target of Standard, Auto maximum injection time, isolation window of 0.4 m/z, and stepped collision energy (NCE) of 20, 40, 60. Dynamic exclusion was set to 2.5s after 1 time to prevent repeated fragmentation of the same ion, with an intensity threshold of 1.0e^3 for ion selection, and an apex trigger with a trigger window of 30%. Isotope exclusion was enabled to focus on the most abundant isotopes, and lock masses were used to ensure calibration stability throughout the runs. An electrospray ionization interface was used as ionization source. Analysis was performed in positive and negative ionization mode in switching (alternating) polarity.</p>	<p>Metabolomics processing was performed untargeted using <strong>Compound Discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Scientific)</strong> and <strong>Skyline 22.2 (MacCoss Lab Software)</strong> for peak picking and feature grouping.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>	<p>A <strong>MS-Omics </strong>in-house annotation and curation pipeline written in<strong> MatLab (2022b, MathWorks)</strong> was applied.</p><p><br></p><p>Compound identification was performed at 4 levels:</p><p><strong><em>Level 1:</em></strong><em> identification by retention times (compared against in-house authentic standards, with an accepted deviation of 0.2 min), accurate mass (with an accepted deviation of 3 ppm), and </em><strong><em>MS/MS</em></strong><em> spectra (with a match factor above 60);</em></p><p><strong><em>Level 2a:</em></strong><em> identification by retention times (compared against in-house authentic standards, with an accepted deviation of 0.2 min), accurate mass (with an accepted deviation of 3 ppm);</em></p><p><strong><em>Level 2b:</em></strong><em> identification by accurate mass (with an accepted deviation of 3 ppm), and </em><strong><em>MS/MS</em></strong><em> spectra from in-house authentic standards and </em><strong><em>mzCloud (ThermoScientific)</em></strong><em> at a match factor &gt;60;</em></p><p><strong><em>Level 3:</em></strong><em> identification by accurate mass alone (with an accepted deviation of 3ppm), based on the </em><strong><em>Human metabolome database (v 5.0)</em></strong><em>.</em></p><p><br></p><p>In the case of known isotopes with indistinguishable retention time and fragmentation behaviour, co-eluting peaks were manually integrated and reported as compound groups (e.g. hexoses or sugar alcohols).</p><p><br></p>

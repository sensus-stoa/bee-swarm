Study Identifier	MTBLS12316
Study Title	Maternal Gut Microbiota Influences Immune Activation at the Maternal-Fetal Interface Affecting Pregnancy Outcome
Study Description	<p>Preeclampsia is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in pregnant women, affecting 5-8% of gestations worldwide. Its development is influenced by maternal immune abnormalities, metabolic disorders and gut dysbiosis. In this study, we show that gut dysbiosis in pregnant C57BL/6J dams leads to increased fetal resorption, impaired placental development and altered vascularization. These adverse outcomes are associated with key pathological features of preeclampsia, including hypoxia, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and reduction in uterine natural killer (NK) cell numbers. Furthermore, gut dysbiosis significantly perturbs placental carbohydrate metabolism, which impairs NK cell IFN-γ secretion. Notably, glucose supplementation restores placental NK cell function and reduces fetal resorption, suggesting that the observed impairment is reversible and dependent on a lower glycolytic rate. These findings highlight maternal gut microbiota as a key player in carbohydrate metabolism, with a pivotal role in modulating placental immunity and pregnancy outcome. The results provide valuable insights into potential metabolic biomarkers and suggest that targeting the gut microbiota may offer a strategy for preventing preeclampsia.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Colonic feces and placental samples from vancomycin-treated and controls dams were stored at -80 °C until processing. Metabolomics data analysis was performed on 24 samples, including fecal (ed14.5, control: n=6; vancomycin-treated dams: n=6) and placental samples (ed14.5, control: n=6; vancomycin-treated dams: n=6).</p>	<p>Metabolome extraction, purification and derivatization were conducted using the MetaboPrep GC kit (manufactured by Theoreo S.R.L, Montecorvino Pugliano [SA], Italy), according to the manufacturer's instructions. A 25 mg of sample was carefully transferred into a centrifuge tube, along with the extraction solution containing the internal standard.&nbsp;The sample and extraction mixture were then mixed at 1250 rpm for 30 min and sonicated for 60 min.&nbsp;The extracted metabolome was then centrifuged for 7 min at 16,000 rpm, keeping the centrifuge temperature below 4 °C.&nbsp;From the resulting mixture, 200 µL of the clear surface liquid (supernatant) was transferred to the Eppendorf tube containing the purification solution and then rapidly stirred at 1250 rpm for 30 s.</p><p><br></p><p>The mixture was then centrifuged at 16,000 rpm at a temperature of 4 °C to avoid solubilization of the solid phase. Subsequently, 175 μL of the liquid supernatant was transferred to a glass vial, promptly frozen at -80 °C and lyophilized overnight.</p><p><br></p><p>To prepare the freeze-dried samples for further analysis, the metabolites within them underwent derivatization using a two-step process. At first, 50 µL of methoxylamine hydrochloride in pyridine was added to the freeze-dried material and mixed at 1200 rpm for 90 min. Then, 25 µL of a derivatization solution containing N,O-Bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (BSTFA) and trimethylchlorosilane (TMCS) was added to the mixture. The vials were once again mixed at 1200 rpm for an additional 90 min. The resulting derivatized metabolites (75 µL) were transferred to a vial insert to facilitate injection into an autosampler.</p>	<p>Subsamples measuring 2 µL of the derivatized solution were introduced into the GC-MS system, which consisted of a GC-2010 Plus gas chromatograph coupled to a 2010 Plus single quadrupole mass spectrometer (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a CP-Sil 8 CB fused silica capillary GC column (30 m x 0.25 mm, with a film thickness of 1.00 µm) from Agilent. Helium was used as the carrier gas. The oven temperature was initially set at 100 °C and maintained for 1 min, then increased at a rate of 6 °C per min until a final temperature of 320 °C was reached, which was held for an additional 2.33 min. To ensure a constant linear velocity of 39 cm/s, the gas flow was adjusted accordingly, while the split flow was maintained at a ratio of 5:1.</p>	<p>The mass spectrometer, a single quadrupole, was set up for analysis using electron impact ionization (EI) at 70 eV. The analysis was performed in full scan mode, allowing the detection of all ionic fragments within the 35-600 m/z range, with a scan rate of 3333 amu/s, ensuring good resolution in ion separation. During the acquisition, a solvent cut time of 6.0 min was applied to eliminate solvent-derived ions and improve the quality of the signal. The total duration of the GC program was 40 min, including the complete chromatographic separation phase, during which mass data were acquired for each chromatographic peak. In this process, each peak was analyzed to identify the compounds present in the sample, contributing to the formation of the final mass spectrum.</p>	<p>GC-MS metabolomics results were compiled into a comma-separated values (CSV) file and loaded into specialized software (MetaboPredict, Theoreo srl, Montecorvino Pugliano, Italy) for statistical analysis. Before analysis, the chromatographic data underwent several preprocessing steps. Data alignment was achieved using the Parametric Time-Wrapping algorithm, followed by peak picking, integration and deconvolution. The resulting data were organized in a tabular format, with each sample represented by a row and each metabolite by a column. Normalization procedures were applied to the data, involving both transformation and scaling. A generalized logarithmic transformation was used for the data transformation, while scaling was performed using an auto-scaling approach that involved mean-centering and dividing each metabolite by its standard deviation. Additionally, normalization was carried out using the chromatographic peak area of the internal standard along with the exact sample weight.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was conducted using a standard methodology. In summary, untargeted metabolite identification involved comparing the mass spectrum of each peak with the NIST-2014 library. For this comparison, a maximum tolerance of 10 for the linear retention index difference was set, while the minimum matching spectra library search was set at 85% (representing a level 2 identification according to standard guidelines) whenever feasible. Metabolites that did not meet these criteria were classified as unknown, following level 4 guidelines. The signals per sample generated by the combination of gas chromatography and mass spectrometry were not subjected to further analysis if they were either absent in less than 80% of the samples or present at concentrations that were too low or exhibited poor spectral quality, making identification challenging. To validate the significance of the identified metabolites in distinguishing different classes, those VIPs were confirmed using independent analytical standards (corresponding to level 1 identification).</p>

Study Title	Gut Microbiome and Serum Metabolic Alterations in Recurrent Gestational Diabetes Mellitus
Study Description	<p>Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common complication of pregnancy, particularly affecting multiparous women with a history of GDM, who face a recurrence risk approaching 50%. This study investigates the association between gut microbiota modifications and metabolic profiles from early to mid-pregnancy and the recurrence of GDM in a cohort of 70 multiparous women with prior GDM. Conducted as a prospective cohort study at Beijing Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital from July 2018 to December 2020, participants underwent assessments including oral glucose tolerance tests (OGTT) and collection of fecal and serum samples. Microbial DNA was extracted, with the V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene sequenced, while serum metabolites were analyzed through non-targeted metabolomics using mass spectrometry. Results indicated significant alterations in gut microbiota composition, with specific genera associated with increased GDM risk. Notably, the study identified distinct metabolic patterns, revealing downregulation of several key metabolites in recurrent GDM cases compared to controls. Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted dysregulation in metabolic functions pertinent to serotonergic synapses, arachidonic acid metabolism, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, and caffeine metabolism. Overall, this research underscores the critical role of gut microbiota and metabolic changes in understanding GDM recurrence, providing insights that may inform targeted preventive strategies.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Participants in this study were recruited from Beijing Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital between July 2018 and December 2020, specifically targeting multiparous women with a documented history of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Serum samples were collected following an overnight fasting period during two time points: T1 at 8 to 12 weeks of gestation and T2 at 24 to 28 weeks of gestation.</p><p>Immediately after collection, the serum samples were aliquoted and stored at −80°C to preserve their integrity for future analyses. </p>	<p>Before analysis, the serum samples underwent a series of extraction and preparation steps. Initially, 100 μL of the serum sample was placed in an Eppendorf tube, and 400 μL of 80% methanol solution was added. The mixture was then vortexed thoroughly and kept on ice for 5 minutes. Following this, the sample was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 20 minutes at 4°C.</p><p>The supernatant was carefully collected and diluted with analytical-grade water to achieve a methanol concentration of 53%. This solution was again centrifuged at 15,000 g for 20 minutes at 4°C, and the final supernatant was prepared for analysis via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS).</p><p>To ensure quality control throughout the experimental process, we prepared 15 quality control (QC) samples by pooling equal volumes of experimental samples. These QC samples were used to balance the chromatography-mass spectrometry system and monitor instrument performance, thereby evaluating system stability during the analysis. Additionally, blank samples were included to eliminate background ions, further ensuring the accuracy of the results.&nbsp;</p>	<p>The analysis was performed using a&nbsp;Q Exactive™ HF/Q Exactive™ HF-X mass spectrometer&nbsp;and a&nbsp;Vanquish UHPLC system, both manufactured by&nbsp;Thermo Fisher Scientific&nbsp;in Germany. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a&nbsp;Hypersil Gold column&nbsp;(100 × 2.1 mm), designed for high-resolution separation.</p><p>Chromatography Conditions:</p><p>Column Temperature:&nbsp;40 °C</p><p>Flow Rate:&nbsp;0.2 mL/min</p><p>Injection Volume:&nbsp;[Specify injection volume if available; if not, state 'not specified']</p><p>Mobile Phase:</p><p>Phase A:&nbsp;0.1% formic acid in water</p><p>Phase B:&nbsp;Pure methanol</p><p>Gradient Elution Program:</p><p>0–3 minutes:&nbsp;Phase A decreased from 98% to 15%, while Phase B increased from 2% to 85%.</p><p>3–10 minutes:&nbsp;Mobile phase composition shifted to 100% methanol.</p><p>10–10.1 minutes:&nbsp;Returned to initial conditions (98% Phase A and 2% Phase B).</p><p>10.1–12 minutes:&nbsp;Maintained initial conditions for equilibration.</p><p>This gradient program ensured effective separation and elution of analytes, followed by re-equilibration of the column for subsequent runs.</p>	<p>The analysis was performed using a&nbsp;Q Exactive™ HF/Q Exactive™ HF-X mass spectrometer&nbsp;manufactured by&nbsp;Thermo Fisher Scientific&nbsp;in Germany.</p><p>Mass Spectrometry Settings:</p><p>Ion Source:&nbsp;Electrospray Ionization (ESI)</p><p>Ionization Modes:&nbsp;Positive and Negative</p><p>m/z Scan Range:&nbsp;100 to 1500</p><p>Spray Voltage:&nbsp;3.5 kV</p><p>Sheath Gas Flow Rate:&nbsp;35 psi</p><p>Auxiliary Gas Flow Rate:&nbsp;10 L/min</p><p>Capillary Temperature:&nbsp;320 °C</p><p>S-lens RF Level:&nbsp;60</p><p>Auxiliary Gas Heater Temperature:&nbsp;350 °C</p><p>Acquisition Mode:</p><p>Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA):&nbsp;MS/MS scanning was employed to enhance the detection and characterization of analytes in the samples.</p><p>These settings were optimized to ensure high sensitivity and accurate mass measurements for comprehensive analysis.</p>	<p>The transformation of raw data files (.raw) began with importing them into the CD 3.3 software, which is tailored for metabolomics data processing. The initial step involved screening metabolites based on critical parameters such as retention time and mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) to ensure reliable identification.</p><p>To enhance the accuracy of the identification process, peak areas were subsequently corrected using the first quality control sample (QC). After this correction, parameters for peak extraction were established, which included a mass deviation threshold set at 5 ppm, a signal intensity deviation limit of 30%, and specifications for minimum signal intensity along with considerations for adduct ions. This pipeline enabled the precise quantification of detected peaks.</p><p>For normalization of the quantitative results, a specific formula was applied: Sample Original Quantitative Value / (Total Quantitative Value of Sample Metabolites / Total Quantitative Value of QC Sample Metabolites). This formula facilitated the conversion of original quantitative values into relative peak areas. Additionally, compounds with a coefficient of variation (CV) greater than 30% among the QC samples were excluded from further analysis, ensuring that only reliable data were retained.</p><p>The entire data processing pipeline was executed on a Linux operating system, specifically CentOS version 6.6, and utilized software tools such as R and Python for various computational tasks and statistical analyses.</p>	<p>To identify features and annotate metabolites, the mass spectral data underwent a comprehensive analysis involving the prediction of molecular formulas. This was accomplished by utilizing both molecular ion peaks and their corresponding fragment ion peaks to enhance the reliability of the identification.</p><p>The predicted formulas were then compared against reference databases, including mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org), mzVault and Masslist, to confirm the identity of the metabolites. This comparison facilitated the validation of the features detected in the samples against established metabolite information.</p><p>Background ions were also addressed by analyzing blank samples to remove any noise from the data, improving the clarity and accuracy of the results.</p><p>Through this integrated approach of utilizing mass spectral data alongside reference databases, the analysis ensured thorough metabolite identification and annotation, allowing for confident results in the subsequent stages of the study.</p>

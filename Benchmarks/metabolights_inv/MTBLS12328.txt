Study Title	Exploring the gut microbiome and serum metabolome interplay in non-functioning pituitary neuroendocrine tumors.
Study Description	<p>The gut microbiome has emerged as a potential factor in cancer pathogenesis, but its role in non-functioning pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (NF-PitNETs) remains unclear. This study aimed to elucidate gut microbiome and metabolomic alterations in NF-PitNETs by comparing microbial diversity, pathogenic bacteria and serum metabolomic profiles between NF-PitNET patients and healthy controls. The gut microbiome was assessed through 16S rRNA sequencing, while serum metabolomics was analyzed using mass spectrometry. Correlation analyses identified potential links between microbial characteristics and metabolic markers. The results revealed that specific pathogenic bacteria, such as Bacteroides, were significantly enriched in NF-PitNET patients. Multi-omics correlations suggested that altered microbiota might contribute to NF-PitNET pathogenesis by modulating host metabolic pathways. These findings highlight the potential role of gut microbiome dysbiosis and its metabolic effects in NF-PitNET development, offering insights into possible therapeutic and diagnostic targets.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Serum samples were collected from 23 preoperative patients with newly diagnosed non-functioning pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (NF-PitNETs) and 17 healthy controls in Beijing, China. For NF-PitNET patients, blood was drawn preoperatively after an overnight fast (≥8 h) to avoid confounding effects of treatment, while with recent antibiotic/probiotic use (within 6 months), chronic gastrointestinal diseases, malignancies or dietary changes were excluded. Venous blood was collected into vacuum tubes, gently inverted, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the serum supernatant was aliquoted into 1.5 mL cryovials and stored at -80 °C until metabolomic analysis. All samples were obtained at a single morning time point to standardize fasting conditions.</p>	<p>For metabolite extraction, 50 µL of serum was mixed with 200 µL of prechilled methanol-acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) solution. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly for 30 s and incubated on ice for 10 min to precipitate proteins. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 15,000 x g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing equal aliquots from all individual samples.Before LC-MS analysis, the extracted metabolites were dried under a nitrogen stream and reconstituted in 80% methanol in LC-MS grade water. The prepared samples, including QC samples and solvent blanks, were transferred into vials for subsequent analysis.</p>	<p>Metabolomic profiling was performed using a Thermo Scientific UHPLC system coupled with a Thermo Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer. The separation was achieved on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 x 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm particle size). The mobile phases for positive ion mode consisted of eluent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and eluent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), while for negative ion mode, eluent A was 5 mM ammonium acetate (pH 9.0) in water and eluent B was acetonitrile. The solvent gradient was set as follows: 2% B from 0.0 to 1.5 min, 2-85% B from 1.5 to 3.0 min, 85-100% B from 3.0 to 10.0 min, 100-2% B from 10.0 to 10.1 min, and 2% B from 10.1 to 12.0 min for re-equilibration. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and an injection volume of 5 μL. Heated electrospray ionization (HESI) was used as the ionization source, with a sheath gas flow rate of 40 arb, an auxiliary gas flow rate of 10 arb, a capillary temperature of 320 °C, and a spray voltage of 3.5 kV in positive mode and 3.2 kV in negative mode.</p>	<p>Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer was operated in positive/negative polarity mode with spray voltage of 3.5 kV, capillary temperature of 320 °C, sheath gas flow rate of 35 psi and aux gas flow rate of 10 L/min, S-lens RF level of 60, Aux gas heater temperature of 350 °C.</p>	<p>The raw MS data were converted to mzML files using Compound Discoverer 3.1. The main parameters were set as follows: retention time tolerance, 0.2 min; actual mass tolerance, 5 ppm; signal intensity tolerance, 30%; signal-to-noise ratio, 3; and minimum intensity, et al. After peak detection, peak intensities were normalized to the total spectral intensity. The normalized data were used to predict the molecular formula based on additive ions, molecular ion peaks and fragment ions. Peaks were then matched with the mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org), mzVault, and MassList databases to obtain accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results. Metabolite annotation was performed using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) and LipidMaps. Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.2), Python (version 3.8.10) and MetaboAnalyst 5.0. When data were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were applied. Data processing, peak alignment and feature extraction were carried out using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and multivariate analysis, including PCA (Principal Component Analysis) and PLS-DA (Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis), was performed using SIMCA 16.0.2 (Umetrics, Sweden).</p>	<p>Peaks were then matched with the mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org), mzVault, and MassList databases to obtain accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results.

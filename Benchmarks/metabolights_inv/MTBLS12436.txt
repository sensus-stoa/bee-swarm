Study Title	Unveiling molecular changes in guabiju during Aspergillus brasiliensis cultivation: insights from untargeted metabolomics
Study Description	<p>Black aspergilli exhibit remarkable bioconversion potential by transforming plant matrices into bioactive compounds, although their metabolic processes remain underexplored. This study investigated the biotransformation of metabolites during the solid-state cultivation of guabiju (<em>Myrcianthes pungens</em>) by <em>Aspergillus brasiliensis</em> using an LC-MS-based untargeted metabolomics approach, aiming to elucidate dynamic changes in metabolite profiles over 0, 3, 7, 10 and 15 days of cultivation. Key metabolites, such as gallic acid, epigallocatechin, mono- and di- <em>O</em>-galloylquinic acids, quercetin and citric acid, increased during cultivation, with a significant rise in gallic acid release and citric acid production observed after seven days. Fungal metabolites including hexylitaconic acid also increased, indicating potential to produce itaconic acid-derived compounds. Data analysis indicated that most significant metabolic changes occurred within the first seven days, especially for tannin hydrolysis and organic acid production. These findings highlighted the potential of <em>A. brasiliensis</em> to enhance fermented foods through targeted biotransformation.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Guabiju fruits (Myrcianthes pungens) were donated by the Agronomic Experimental Station (EEA) of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS). After harvest, the fruits were cleaned, frozen (-80 °C), and lyophilized (Liobras L101). The powder was milled without the seeds (IKA A11), homogenized, vacuum-packed, and stored at 5 °C in a silica-containing box until further analysis. </p><p><br></p><p>Aspergillus brasiliensis BLf1 was isolated from Amazonian environment and deposited in the culture collection of the Biotechnology &amp; Biochemical Engineering Laboratory (BiotecLab, Food Science and Technology Institute, Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul, Porto Alegre, Brazil). The lyophilized spores were donated, resuspended in sterile yeast peptone dextrose (YPD) broth, and kept for 30 min. Subsequently, A. brasiliensis was cultivated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) incubated at 30 °C for 10 days.</p><p><br></p><p>The solid-state cultivations of A. brasiliensis were conducted in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 10 g of lyophilized guabiju, following the method adapted from Menezes et al. (2017). Each flask was supplemented with following solutions: 14 mL of mineral solution (6 g/L NaNO3, 0.52 g/L KCl, 0.52 g/L MgSO4.7H2O, and 1.53 g/L KH2PO4) and 4 mL of micronutrient solution (22 g/L ZnSO4.7H2O, 11 g/L H3BO3, 5 g/L MnCl2.4H2O, 5 g/L FeSO4.7H2O, 1.6 g/L CoCl2.6H2O, 1.6 g/L CuSO4.5H2O, 1.1 g/L (NH4)6Mo7O24.4H2O and 50 g/L EDTA). The pH of the solutions was adjusted to 6.5 with 1 M NaOH. The flasks were sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min (Altop, 2019; Shin et al., 2019; Somdee et al., 2023). After sterilization, 2 mL of pyridoxine (vitamin B6) solution (0.1 g/mL, pH adjusted to 6.5) was added. The final moisture content of the substrate was 29 ± 2%, with a water activity of 0.962. Each flask was inoculated with 1 x 10^8 spores of the fungus A. brasiliensis, measured using the Neubauer chamber counting. The flasks were incubated at 30 °C for 0, 3, 7, 10 and 15 days. Control experiments without fungus were prepared under the same conditions. All experiments were performed in sextuplicate (6 biological replicates for each time of cultivation). A total of 3 replicates were used for solvent extraction (untargeted metabolomics).</p><p><br></p><p>Altop A (2019). Effect of Solid-State Fermentation on Main Nutritional Components, Some Minerals, Condensed Tannin and Phenolic Compounds of Olive Leaves. Turkish Journal of Agriculture - Food Science and Technology, 7(1), 115–119. https://doi.org/10.24925/turjaf.v7i1.115-119.2231</p><p>Menezes BS, Rossi DM, Ayub MAZ (2017). Screening of filamentous fungi to produce xylanase and xylooligosaccharides in submerged and solid-state cultivations on rice husk, soybean hull, and spent malt as substrates. World Journal of Microbiology and Biotechnology, 33(3), 1–12. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11274-017-2226-5</p><p>Shin HY, Kim SM, Lee JH, Lim, ST (2019). Solid-state fermentation of black rice bran with Aspergillus awamori and Aspergillus oryzae: Effects on phenolic acid composition and antioxidant activity of bran extracts. Food Chemistry, 272(November 2017), 235–241. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2018.07.174</p><p>Somdee T, Thitusutthi S, Somdee T, Chumroenpaht T, Mungvongsa A (2023). Effect of solid-state fermentation on amino acid profile and phytochemicals of red rice bran. ScienceAsia, 49(1), 63–69. https://doi.org/10.2306/scienceasia1513-1874.2022.131</p>	<p>Each extraction was conducted in triplicate using the entire contents of the Erlenmeyer flask for each biological replicate. The solvent extraction was performed with 40 mL of a methanol and water mixture (MeOH:H2O 8:2, v/v) acidified with 0.35% formic acid. This solvent was chosen to maximize the extraction of polar low-molecular weight metabolites. All samples were agitated at 180 rpm on an orbital shaker for 20 min. The solids were separated by centrifugation at 12,000 x g for 15 min (5 °C), and the supernatant removed. The extracts were stored at -80 °C until further analysis. When necessary, the extracts were diluted (factor 2, 5 or 50) and filtered (hydrophilic PTFE, 0.22 µm).</p><p><br></p><p>Quality control (QC) samples were used to ensure good data quality and assist in data analysis (Broadhurst et al., 2018). A pooled QC sample was prepared using an equal volume (100 μL) of each sample extract. Process blanks, used to identify features derived from laboratory consumables, were created using the same extraction procedure, with solvent (acidified methanol and water) replacing the sample. Similarly, a solvent blank was also evaluated during the analytical run to detect impurities present in the solvent.</p><p><br></p><p>Broadhurst D, Goodacre R, Reinke SN, Kuligowski J, Wilson ID, Lewis MR, Dunn WB (2018). Guidelines and considerations for the use of system suitability and quality control samples in mass spectrometry assays applied in untargeted clinical metabolomic studies. Metabolomics, 14(6), 1–17. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-018-1367-3.</p>	<p>Untargeted full-scan data were obtained using a Nexera X2 UHPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan) and the Impact II MS (Bruker Daltonics, USA) with electrospray ionization (ESI) source and quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-TOF) detector. Samples were randomly injected into an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 x 50 mm, 1.8 μm particle size). The mobile phase was composed of a gradient of: (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. A linear gradient was applied: 0.0-0.5 min, 0% B; 0.5-8.0 min, 100% B; 8.0-9.0 min isocratic at 100% B; 9.0-10.0 min, 0% B. The mobile phase flow rate was 0.5 mL/min and the temperature was set at 40 °C. The analysis was performed with an injection volume of 3 μL per sample.</p>	<p>UHPLC was coupled to QTOF-MS with an ESI source that was operated in positive and negative ionization modes to enable comprehensive coverage of metabolites. Settings of the mass spectrometer (MS) were as follows: gas temperature of 200 °C, gas flow of 9 L/min, nebulizer of 4 bar, and mass range of 50-1000 <em>m/z</em>. Fragmentation data were collected using the mass spectrometer settings described above. Auto MS/MS scanning was conducted with collision energies between 11 and 30 eV, depending on the mass, width, and charge values. The precursor selection criteria included a maximum of 3 precursors per cycle. Active exclusion was enabled, with precursors excluded after 3 spectra and released after 0.83 min.</p>	<p>The conversion to mzML was performed directly by Data Analysis (Bruker). Data obtained from UHPLC-QTOF-MS analysis were processed using the open-source software MZmine3. This process groups mass spectral features according to expected isotopic patterns, adducts, and charge states, aligning them across all samples. Filtering of the data and statistical analysis were performed using R (version 4.4.0), following the methodology described by Dzakovich et al. (2024). Initially, features not at least 10 times higher in the quality control samples (QCs) than in the process blank were excluded from analysis. The analysis was then restricted to features that are present in 100% of QCs and those with a coefficient of variation lower than 30% in the QCs. Missing values were imputed with half of the lowest peak area value in the dataset. Data was clustered with the notame R package (Klåvus et al., 2020). All data was log2 transformed prior to analysis. The data were statistically analyzed using a combination of tools: univariate analysis (Student's t-test, ANOVA, and Tukey’s tests), and multivariate analysis (principal component analysis).</p><p><br></p><p>Dzakovich MP, Goggans ML, Thomas-Ahner JM, Moran NE, Clinton SK, Francis DM, Cooperstone JL (2024). Transcriptomics and Metabolomics Reveal Tomato Consumption Alters Hepatic Xenobiotic Metabolism and Induces Steroidal Alkaloid Metabolite Accumulation in Mice. Molecular Nutrition and Food Research, 68(4), 1–11. https://doi.org/10.1002/mnfr.202300239</p><p>Klåvus A, Kokla M, Noerman S, Koistinen VM, Tuomainen M, Zarei I, Meuronen T, Häkkinen MR, Rummukainen S, Babu AF, Sallinen T, Kärkkäinen O, Paananen J (2020). “Notame”: Workflow for Non-Targeted LC–MS Metabolic Profiling. Metabolites, 10(135), 1–35. https://doi.org/doi:10.3390/metabo10040135</p>	<p>Metabolites were annotated following the guidelines set by the Metabolomics Standard Initiative (Sumner et al., 2007). The identification of metabolites of interest was carried out using a multifaceted approach, using a combination of MS/MS fragmentation spectra, accurate mass, isotope analysis, database matches when available, and data available in the literature. Initially, sample spectra were compared with an internal standard library (Phenolic Lab Brazil). Subsequently, the comparison was performed with an in-house literature library, using MS/MS and MS spectral matching techniques.</p><p>Additionally, MS/MS fragmentation spectra were analyzed by searching public spectral libraries, using the GNPS platform (Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking, available at: http://gnps.ucsd.edu/) (Wang et al., 2016), and performing similarity analysis through spectral network. Two GNPS algorithms were employed: Classical Molecular Networking, and Library Search. The parameters for constructing the spectral network were optimized based on previous studies (Gauglitz et al., 2020), which included a minimum number of 4 matched peaks between the experimental and database spectra and a cosine similarity threshold greater than 0.7. Additionally, the MS/MS fragmentation data were compared against those of authentic standards.</p><p>Finally, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) was used as a reference database, where compounds annotated within the samples were matched with known compounds in the database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/).</p><p>All annotations were also subjected to a manual evaluation, considering fragmentation patterns, biological context, and relevant factors such as matrix composition, cultivation dynamics, and <em>Aspergillus</em>-derived metabolites.</p><p><br></p><p>Gauglitz JM, Aceves CM, Aksenov AA, Aleti G, Almaliti J, Bouslimani A, Brown EA, Campeau A, Caraballo-Rodríguez AM, Chaar R, da Silva RR, Demko AM, Di Ottavio F, Elijah E, Ernst M, Ferguson LP, Holmes X, Jarmusch AK, Jiang L, Dorrestein PC (2020). Untargeted mass spectrometry-based metabolomics approach unveils molecular changes in raw and processed foods and beverages. Food Chemistry, 302(June 2018), 125290. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2019.125290</p><p>Sumner LW, Amberg A, Barrett D, Beale MH, Beger R, Daykin CA, Fan TWM, Fiehn O, Goodacre R, Griffin JL, Hankemeier T, Hardy N, Harnly, J, Higashi R, Kopka J, Lane AN, Lindon JC, Marriott P, Nicholls AW, Viant MR (2007). Proposed minimum reporting standards for chemical analysis. Metabolomics, 3(3), 211–221. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-007-0082-2</p><p>Wang M, Carver JJ, Phelan VV, Sanchez LM, Garg N, Peng Y, Nguyen DD, Watrous J, Kapono CA, Luzzatto-Knaan T, Porto C, Bouslimani A, Melnik AV, Meehan MJ, Liu WT, Crüsemann M, Boudreau PD, Esquenazi E, Sandoval-Calderón M, Bandeira N (2016).

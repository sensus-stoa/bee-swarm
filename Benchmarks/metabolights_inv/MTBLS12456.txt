Study Identifier	MTBLS12456
Study Title	Metabolite control of enzyme activity links stress to biosynthetic regulation
Study Description	<p>Cells must continuously adjust metabolic output to maintain homeostasis under changing environmental conditions, yet the mechanisms that enable rapid and reversible control of pathway activity remain largely unknown. The methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway, of bacterial origin and conserved in plastid-bearing eukaryotes, including plants and apicomplexan parasites, produces isoprenoid precursors essential for growth and stress adaptation. Here, we identify methylerythritol cyclodiphosphate (MEcPP) as a dual-function metabolite that serves both as a biosynthetic intermediate and a direct modulator of enzyme activity. Genetic perturbations and high light stress revealed step-specific MEcPP accumulation independent of transcriptional regulation. Biochemical and protease-protection assays showed that MEcPP destabilizes and inhibits methylerythritol cytidylyltransferase (MCT) while modestly stabilizing hydroxymethylbutenyl diphosphate synthase (HDS). Molecular docking analyses indicate that MEcPP interacts directly with the MCT catalytic site, displacing the natural substrate and thereby attenuating enzyme activity, suggesting a competitive, feedback-like mechanism of metabolic control. These results define MEcPP as a metabolic feedback signal that translates stress-induced changes into targeted enzymatic control. This mechanism illustrates how pathway intermediates dynamically coordinate biosynthetic activity with environmental cues, representing a broadly conserved strategy for metabolite-driven control of cellular metabolism.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>All Arabidopsis thaliana lines used in this study were in the Columbia-0 (Col-0) background. Previously described lines include the ceh1 mutant and RNAi antisense lines targeting DXS, DXR and HDR (asDXS, asDXR and asHDR) (Xiao et al., 2012), as well as the dexamethasone (DEX)-inducible MEcPP-accumulating line, HDSi (Jiang et al., 2019).</p><p><br></p><p>DEX-inducible RNAi lines (DXSi, DXRi and HDRi) were generated following a similar protocol as for HDSi. Briefly, 100–200 bp fragments near the N-terminal regions of each cDNA sequence were amplified and cloned into the pDONR207 vector. Clones were sequenced, and positive clones were introduced into the pOpoff2 DEX-inducible RNAi vector using Gateway cloning. Constructs were transformed into Col-0 plants via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (Clough and Bent, 1998) and selected on 50 mg/L kanamycin. Homozygous lines were identified through genotyping. Primers used are listed in Supplemental Table 1.</p>	<p>Reactions were stopped by adding 50 µL extraction buffer (40% Methanol, 40% Acetonitrile, 20% double-distilled water acidified with 0.5% formic acid) and immediately stored at -20 °C. Next day the reactions were centrifuged at 12,000 x g for 5 min at 4 °C and transferred to a measuring vial.</p><p><br></p><p>For MEcPP and HMBPP</p><p>Reactions (25 uL) contained 25 µM MEP, 25 µM CTP, 50 mM Ammonium Bicarbonate pH 8, 8 µM DTT, 0.01 U pyrophosphatase, 80 µM MgCl2 and 30 nM MCT. Reactions were performed at 22 °C in 200 µL microfuge tubes in thermocycler (BioRad) for 4 min. Reactions were stopped by adding 50 µL extraction buffer (40% Methanol, 40% Acetonitrile, 20% double-distilled water acidified with 0.5% formic acid) and immediately stored at -20 °C. To test the effect of MEcPP or HMBPP on the reaction, varied concentrations (0, 50, 100, 150 and 200 µM) of MEcPP or HMBPP were added to the microfuge tube followed by reaction mix without MCT followed by MCT and allowed to incubate for 4 min before stopping the reaction with extraction buffer.</p>	<p>Dionex Ultimate 3000 binary RSLC system was used with an Accucore-150-Amide-HILIC column (150 x 2.1 mm; particle size 2.6 µM; Thermo Scientific 16726-152130) with a guard column containing the same column matrix (Thermo Scientific 852-00; 16726-012105). Inject volume was 2 µL of each reaction. Gradient elution was performed with acetonitrile (A) and 10 mM ammonium acetate pH 8.0 (B). The separation was conducted using the following gradient profile (t (min), %A, %B): (−2, 90, 10), (0, 90, 10), (12, 30, 70), (15, 30, 70), (16, 90, 10), (22, 90, 10). Flow rate was maintained at 280 µL/min. The column was kept at 35 °C.</p>	<p>Thermo Q-Exactive Focus mass spectrometer with a heated electro spray ionization source was used to perform LC-MS analysis. Mass spectra were acquired in negative mode with the following parameters: spray voltage, 3.50 kV; sheath gas flow rate 50, auxiliary gas flow rate 14, sweep gas flow rate 2, capillary temperature at 275 °C, S-lens RF level 100 and auxiliary gas heater temperature at 275 °C.</p>	<p>Spectral areas from each MCT-enzyme assay sample were transformed to be relative to the control (0 µM MEcPP or HMBPP) within each experiment. Relative data from each experiment were combined to calculate means. Data are mean ± standard error (SE). The analyses were carried out via a two-tailed Student’s T-Tests with a significance of P &lt; 0.05. With more than 2 variables a Least Significant Difference (LSD) test in RStudio (2023.09.0+463) was applied with a significance of P &lt; 0.05</p>	<p>Compound of interest was identified by accurate mass measurements (MS1, Thermo Trace Finder Software), retention time and mass transitions of purified standard and extract matrixes.</p>

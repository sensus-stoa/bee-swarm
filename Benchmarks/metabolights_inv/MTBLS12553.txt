Study Title	Exploring the Role of Gut Microbiota and Metabolomic Changes on Singing in Zebra Finches
Study Description	<p>Recent studies have demonstrated that the cognitive abilities of songbirds are linked to gut microbiome beta diversity. To explore whether the gut microbiome influences vocalization, a cognitive trait, we examined how antibiotics impact metabolism, microbiome and singing behavior in adult male zebra finches. Using untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry and metagenomic sequencing, we observed significant changes in stool metabolites, microbiome composition and vocal behavior after 14 days of antibiotic treatment. Metabolomic analysis revealed disruptions in the alanine, aspartate and glutamate pathways post-treatment. Furthermore, an analysis of phyla in the gut microbiome demonstrated a decrease in Firmicutes and an increase in Bacteroidetes. Treated birds sang fewer female-directed (FD) songs and produced more introductory notes compared to controls, suggesting possible alterations in neural pathways tied to motivation and vocalization. Despite the decrease in their number, the spectral and temporal properties of FD songs remained stable. Our findings suggest that antibiotic-induced changes in the gut microbiome may influence complex social behaviors such as vocal communication in zebra finches.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Twelve male adult zebra finches (≥120 days old) were obtained from an indoor aviary maintained at the Animal Facility at the National Brain Research Centre, Manesar. All experimental procedures performed on these zebra finches were approved by the statutory Institutional Animal Ethics Committee, NBRC (protocol NBRC/IAEC/2018/150) according to the guidelines of the Committee for the Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA), India.</p><p><br></p><p>Antibiotic treatment</p><p>All 12 adult male zebra finches were weighed prior to and at the end of the experiments.&nbsp;Of these birds, a set of 6 adult male zebra finches were orally administered a cocktail of drugs comprising of ampicillin (100 mg/kg), vancomycin (50 mg/kg), neomycin (100 mg/kg), metronidazole (100 mg/kg) and amphotericin B (1 mg/kg) in a solution containing 20% sucrose [[1] in mice]. Whereas ampicillin, vancomycin, neomycin and metronidazole are antibiotics, amphotericin B is an antifungal agent and was added to the cocktail to prevent fungal or any other opportunistic infection from arising due to antibiotic treatment. Treated birds received doses of the antibiotic cocktail in 20% sucrose, controls (n = 6) received doses of 20% sucrose twice daily for 14 days, at an interval of 12 h.</p><p><br></p><p>Collection of Stool samples</p><p>Stool samples were collected in Eppendorf tubes and stored immediately at -80 °C. The samples were then processed for LC-MS analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>Ref: </p><p>[1] Zarrinpar, A., Chaix, A., Xu, Z.Z.&nbsp;et al.&nbsp;2018. Antibiotic-induced microbiome depletion alters metabolic homeostasis by affecting gut signaling and colonic metabolism.&nbsp;Nat Commun&nbsp;9, 2872 (2018). DOI:10.1038/s41467-018-05336-9.</p>	<p>Frozen stool samples were thawed and then lyophilized. Lyophilized stool (5 mg) was used to extract metabolites using cold methanol in a 1:3 ratio, by vortexing and centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant, which contained metabolites, was collected and reconstituted in 15% methanol. A 5 µL sample of the supernatant was used for LC-MS analysis.</p>	<p>Metabolite profiling was performed at the Advanced Equipment Facility (AEF) of the Translational Health Science and Technology Institute (THSTI), Faridabad, on extracts obtained from the stool samples of experimental birds. An Ultimate 3000 UHPLC was used for the analyses of the samples. Metabolites were separated using both reverse phase, ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3&nbsp;(1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Waters) column, and HILIC, XBridge BEH Amide&nbsp;(3.5 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Waters) column, in independent runs. For reverse phase separations, mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in methanol, run at 0.3 mL/min. For HILIC, mobile phase A was 20 mM ammonium acetate in water (pH 9.0) and mobile phase B was 100% acetonitrile.</p>	<p>A Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid Mass Spectrometer&nbsp; was used to acquire LC-MS data. A heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source was employed to operate in both positive and negative ion modes, enabling detection of a wide range of polar and nonpolar metabolites. Full MS scans were acquired over an m/z range of 60–900 with a resolution of 120,000, while MS/MS scans were performed at a resolution of 15,000. The spray voltage was set to 3,500 V in positive mode and 2,800 V in negative mode. The capillary temperature was maintained at 300°C, with sheath gas and auxiliary gas flows set to 40 and 10 arbitrary units, respectively. </p>	<p>The Progenesis QI for Metabolomics (Water Corporation) software was used to process all LC-MS data according to the default settings. Retention time alignment, feature recognition, deconvolution, and elemental composition prediction were performed using the untargeted workflow of this software. Peaks in the pooled QC samples that had a coefficient of variation (CV) &lt;30% were retained for additional data analysis. </p>	<p>This was followed by using the Metascope plugin of Progenesis QI to annotate metabolites and the in-house library, which provides the mass, fragmentation patterns and retention durations for searching the database. For additional confirmation of the identities of metabolites, we have also employed a spectrum library that is online and accessible. In Progenesis QI, the cut-off for a retention time match was 0.5 min, whereas the threshold for spectrum similarity was greater than 30% fragmentation match. For the purpose of choosing the appropriate peaks, each detected feature was also inspected manually.</p>

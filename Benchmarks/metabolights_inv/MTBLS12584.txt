Study Title	Alleviation of high-fat diet-induced lipid metabolism disorders: Role of quinoa peptides in inhibiting high-activity BSH-producing gut microbiota and modulating of BA-FXR/TGR5 signaling
Study Description	Abstract Background: Lipid metabolism disorders contribute significantly to various metabolic diseases and are closely related to gut microbiota dysbiosis. Our previous study showed that oral gavage of quinoa peptides (QPep) restored gut microbiota composition and alleviated liver lipid metabolism disorders in high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obese mice. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms are still not fully understood. This study aimed to clarify how QPep improve lipid metabolism disorders in HFD mice. Results: Oral gavage of QPep significantly attenuated HFD-induced metabolic disorders in mice, as evidenced by reduced body weight gain and adipose mass, improved serum lipid profiles, decreased hepatic lipid accumulation, and ameliorated impaired glucose homeostasis. The microbiota clearance and fecal microbiota transplantation experiments demonstrated that the beneficial effect of QPep on lipid metabolism disorders depends on the gut microbiota. QPep selectively suppressed gut bacteria that encode bile salt hydrolase (BSH), with high enzymatic activity, reducing intestinal BSH activity while preserving conjugated bile acids (BAs). These accumulated conjugated BAs served as natural FXR antagonists, inhibiting the ileal FXR-FGF15 signaling pathway and stimulating hepatic BAs synthesis by upregulating CYP7A1/CYP27A1. Concurrently, the altered BAs profile strongly activated TGR5 in epididymal white adipose and ileum tissues, reprogramming systemic lipid metabolism and alleviating metabolic disorders. Conclusions: This study reveals a novel mechanism by which bioactive peptides derived from quinoa protein ameliorate lipid metabolism disorders via gut microbiota-BAs crosstalk. These findings demonstrate the potential of quinoa peptides as functional ingredients for metabolic syndrome management and suggest a strategy for precise gut microbiota regulation through dietary interventions.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Mice were fasted for 12 hours before sample collection. Blood samples were collected from anesthetized mice via retro-orbital sinus using heparinized capillary tubes. The collected blood was left at room temperature for 30 minutes and then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C to obtain serum. The serum samples were aliquoted and stored at −80°C until analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><br></p>	<p>Frozen serum samples were thawed on ice and vortexed briefly. A volume of 100 μL serum was mixed with 400 μL of ice-cold methanol:acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) for protein precipitation. The mixture was vortexed for 30 seconds and incubated at −20°C for 30 minutes, then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube and dried using a vacuum concentrator. The dried extracts were reconstituted in 100 μL of 50% methanol in water and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter before LC-MS analysis.</p><p>For quality control (QC), equal volumes of each sample extract were pooled to generate a QC sample. Solvent blank (50% methanol in water) was also included to monitor background signals.</p><p><br></p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a C18 column (ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3, 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters). The column temperature was maintained at 40°C, and the autosampler was kept at 4°C. The injection volume was 5 μL, and the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min.</p><p>Mobile phase A was water with 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The following linear gradient was applied:</p><p>- 0–1 min: 2% B&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>- 1–9 min: 2% to 98% B&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>- 9–12 min: 98% B&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>- 12–12.1 min: 98% to 2% B&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>- 12.1–15 min: 2% B (re-equilibration)</p><p>Total run time: 15 min. Samples were randomized during injection to reduce batch effects.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using a Q Exactive HF Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a HESI (heated electrospray ionization) source operating in positive ion mode.</p><p>The MS scan was acquired in full-scan mode over the m/z range of 70–1050 at a mass resolution of 60,000 (at m/z 200). The sheath gas and auxiliary gas were set to 40 and 10 arbitrary units, respectively. The capillary temperature was 320°C, and the spray voltage was set to 3.8 kV. The S-lens RF level was set at 50. The auxiliary gas heater temperature was 350°C.</p><p>Samples were analyzed in randomized order. External calibration was performed prior to analysis using standard calibration solution.</p><p><br></p>	<p>Raw LC-MS data were processed using Progenesis QI (Waters, v2.4). The workflow included peak detection, retention time alignment using pooled QC, global normalization, and missing value imputation using the minimum value. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching MS and MS/MS spectra to an in-house database and HMDB (±10 ppm). The resulting data matrix was exported as .csv for statistical analysis.</p><p><br></p>	<p>Putative metabolite identification was performed by matching accurate mass and MS/MS spectra to an in-house plant metabolite database and HMDB (Human Metabolome Database), with a mass tolerance of ±10 ppm. Annotation confidence followed MSI level 2 (putative annotation based on spectral similarity).</p><p><br></p>	<p>Mass spectrometry analysis was performed on a Q Exactive HF Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source operating in negative ion mode.</p><p>Full-scan MS data were acquired over a mass range of m/z 70–1050 with a resolution of 60,000 at m/z 200. The spray voltage was set to −2.5 kV. The capillary temperature was maintained at 320°C. Sheath gas and auxiliary gas were set to 40 and 10 arbitrary units (AU), respectively. The auxiliary gas heater temperature was 350°C. The S-lens RF level was set to 50.</p><p>Samples were analyzed in randomized order to reduce technical variation. External mass calibration was conducted prior to sample analysis using standard calibration solution.</p><p><br></p>

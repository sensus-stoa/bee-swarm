Study Title	Multi-omics reveals potential interactions of Roseobacter SASC036 and thiosulfate stress on Zostera marina
Study Description	<p> The dynamic balance of sulfur metabolism in the rhizosphere of seagrasses is closely related to their growth and survival, with processes such as oxidation, reduction, and disproportionation driven by rhizosphere microorganisms playing a crucial role. Roseobacter is a clade of marine bacteria that has important interactions with various marine organisms; however, the mechanisms of its interaction with seagrasses remain unclear. In this study, we conducted two microcosm experiments to investigate the interaction mechanisms between Roseobacter Sulfitobacter alexandrii SASC036, thiosulfate stress, and Zostera marina. Comprehensive results of transcriptomics and metabolomics showed that SASC036 exhibited statistically non-significant effects on Zostera marina growth and induced only minimal changes in rhizosphere metabolism. However, sodium thiosulfate demonstrated an indirect growth-promoting effect, and the combined application of SASC036 and thiosulfate elicited enhanced secretion of phenolic acid compounds in Zostera marina. Notably, 6-heptenyl glucosinolate was identified as a key stress-response metabolite in Zostera marina under biotic/abiotic challenges. The results of this study suggest that sulfide-substrate, thiosulfate-producing microorganisms have the potential to be used for bioremediation of seagrass meadow.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Five millilitres of the treatment solution was pipetted into a sterilized Eppendorf tube (15 ml), and the mixture was quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen for 20 min and then temporarily frozen in an ultralow temperature freezer at -80℃ until freeze-drying; these powders were used for subsequent UPLC-MS identification of the metabolomes.&nbsp;</p>	<p> First, 50 mg of freeze-dried root exudate sample was accurately weighed, 600 μL of a water and methanol mixture was added (1:4), and the mixture was subsequently milled at low temperature for extraction. Second, the extract was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4℃. A total of 380 μL of the supernatant was carefully pipetted into a 2 ml Eppendorf tube and dried with nitrogen. Finally, 120 μL of extraction solution (acetonitrile:water = 1:1) was added to the remix. The extracted metabolites were obtained through a 0.45 μm filter membrane. Twenty microlitres of each of the 26 samples were mixed to prepare the quality control (QC) sample, and 4 QC samples were inserted during the on-machine detections to evaluate the stability of the instrument and ensure the reliability of the results.</p>	<p> The chromatographic column used was an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 (100 mm×2.1 mm i.d., 1.7 μm; Waters, Milford, USA) with 2% acetonitrile water (containing 0.1% formic acid) as mobile phase A and acetonitrile as mobile phase B. The injection volume was 3 μL, the flow rate was 0.4 μL, and the column temperature was 40℃. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0-0.5 min, 95% A; 0.5-7.5 min, 65% A; 7.5-13 min, linear change to 5% and maintained for 1.4 min; and 14.4-14.5 min, linear change to 98% and maintained for 1.5 min.</p>	<p> The mass spectrometry conditions were as follows: sheath gas flow rate of 50 arb, auxiliary gas flow rate of 13 arb, scanning range of 70-1050 m/z, and capillary temperature of 320℃.</p>	<p>Eigenvalues with &gt;20% missing values within each group were removed, missing values were populated to the minimal values of all samples, and all the data were normalized by sumNormalizer and transfered to log10.</p>	<p>The raw data was imported into the metabolomics processing software ProgenesQI v3.0 (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, peak alignment, etc., and finally obtained a data matrix containing information such as retention time, mass to charge ratio, and peak intensity. Afterwards, the software was used for feature peak search library identification, matching MS and MS/MS mass spectrometry information with Meiji's self built plant specific metabolite database (MJDBPM). The mass error of MS was set to less than 10ppm, and metabolites were identified based on the secondary mass spectrometry matching score.</p>

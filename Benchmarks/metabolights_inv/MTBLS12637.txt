Study Identifier	MTBLS12637
Study Title	Metabolomics uncovers the dual effects of high-pressure and ultra-shear processing in juice
Study Description	This study investigated the chemical changes in a blended fruit and vegetable juice induced by innovative food processing technologies - high-pressure processing (HPP; 400 MPa, 40oC) and ultra-shear technology (UST; 400 MPa, 40 and 72 °C) - in comparison to conventional thermal treatments at 40 and 72 °C. Using an untargeted metabolomics approach, we explored the relationships between process parameters and compositional outcomes to evaluate implications for food quality. The results revealed clear compositional separation between untreated and treated samples, with pressure-based technologies further distinguishable from conventional thermal ones. Anthocyanins and other phenolics, such as proanthocyanidins, epicatechin, and phloridzin, degraded across all treatments. Concurrently, degradation products like 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid increased in abundance, suggesting thermal-like degradation pathways. Conversely, several compounds, including hydroxycinnamic acids, ellagic acid, and proline, showed increased levels in treated samples, likely due to cell disruption and release mechanisms. Among the technologies evaluated, heat induced the most pronounced compositional changes, while pressure and ultra-shear stress had more moderate effects. Overall, results suggest that compound release may outweigh degradation, emphasizing the value of untargeted metabolomics in capturing both loss and liberation of bioactive compounds during processing. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of how emerging processing technologies affect food composition and quality.

Study Protocol Description	<p>A juice blend was produced with apples (58%), leaves of red kale (10%), blackberries (11%), lime juice (7%), ginger (1%) and water (13%), made using a domestic juice extractor (Hamilton Beach Juicer Machine, 800W). All ingredients were sourced from a local market in Columbus, OH, USA. The resulting mixture was homogenized, divided into 50 mL aliquots in falcon tubes, and stored at -18°C. The fresh juice had a pH of 3.40 ± 0.03, total soluble solids of 10.3 ± 0.1 °Brix and water content of 88.47 ± 0.04 g/100g.</p><p>For the experiments, the samples were thawed in a water bath, homogenized, and kept in an ice bath until processing. Each experiment was carried out in five separate processing run replicates.</p><p>Samples were treated by 3 different technologies (conventional thermal treatment, HPP, and UST) under various conditions. The process parameters - temperature, pressure, and ultra-shear - were chosen to isolate and evaluate their individual effects on the chemical profile of the blended juice.</p><p>The untreated juice (UFJ) served as the control to evaluate the effects of all treatments, enabling direct comparison of changes in chemical composition. HPP was performed using a laboratory-scale high-pressure system (PT-1, Avure Technologies Inc., Kent, WA) (Gupta et al., 2011). To reduce heat loss during the pressure holding phase, the glycol bath surrounding the pressure chamber was preheated to the target process temperature of 40 °C. Sample temperature during treatment was monitored with a K-type thermocouple sensor (model KMQSS-04OU-7, Omega Engineering, Stamford, CT). A pressure transducer (model 3399 093 006, tecsis GmbH, Frankfurt, Germany) was used to measure the pressure applied to the sample. Data on pressure and temperature were collected and recorded through a computer-based data acquisition system. An aliquot of 2 mL of juice was placed into a high-barrier polyethylene film pouch with a 2 mL capacity, sealed using a heat sealer (MP-12, Impulse, Midwest Pacific, St. Louis, MO). The sealed pouches were then inserted into a 10 mL insulated polypropylene syringe, with the remaining void space filled with water. Based on the heat of compression of the test material, a process temperature of 40 ± 2 °C, and a target pressure of 400 MPa, the initial sample temperature was maintained below 10 ± 1 °C. The pouches were treated under the target conditions for 3 min, excluding the time required for compression (~45 s) and decompression (&lt;2 s). Once depressurization was complete, the pouches were promptly removed from the pressure vessel and cooled in an ice-water bath. Figure S1 displays the pressure-thermal processing history of the samples.</p><p>Ultra-shear technology experiments at 40 and 72oC (UST40 and UST72) were carried out with 400 MPa in a custom-built benchtop UST laboratory tester (PBI, Easton, MA) at 40 and 72 °C, respectively. The system includes a reservoir for the raw product (a 30-mL dispensing syringe), a pressure generation system, a pressure chamber, a shear valve, and a reservoir for the processed product. Pressure and temperature data at the shear valve were recorded using a data acquisition system (PBI). A detailed description of the setup can be seen elsewhere (Janahar et al., 2021).</p><p>Samples were preconditioned to approximately 5 °C were introduced into the UST system, where they were subjected to a pressure of 400 MPa and instantly depressurized through a shear valve without any holding time (Janahar et al., 2021). For the experiment at 40 °C, the shear valve was cooled during processing to maintain the target product temperature. Samples underwent a single processing cycle, comprising a pressurization phase followed by rapid depressurization. To ensure thermal stability, the initial two cycles were discarded, and only from the third cycle onward were samples collected under steady-state thermal conditions. For each treatment batch, juice processed by UST during five consecutive steady-state cycles was collected into sterile tubes and immediately cooled in an ice water bath (Guduru et al., 2024). Figure S2 and S3 show the pressure-thermal processing history of the US and UST samples, respectively.</p><p>To evaluate the effects of heat alone, juice samples were subjected to two thermal treatments: 40 ± 1 °C for 3 minutes and 72 ± 1 °C for 20 seconds, referred to as CT40 and CT72, respectively. These thermal treatments served as references to distinguish the combined effects of thermal and pressure (HPP), as well as thermal, pressure, and shear combinations (UST40 and UST72). It is important to note that microbiological considerations were outside the scope of this study. However, relevant findings have been extensively reported elsewhere (Hu et al., 2023a; Janahar et al., 2023). Treatments conducted below 40 °C are often considered as nonthermal, as heat under these conditions does not contribute to microbiological lethality (Janahar et al., 2021a). In contrast, treatments at higher temperatures must account for the lethal effects of both heat and any nonthermal inactivation mechanisms.</p><p>For CT40 and CT72 experiments, 3 mL samples were placed in aluminum thermal death time (TDT) tubes with an inner diameter of 15 mm, a thickness of 3 mm, and a height of 39 mm (Dhakal et al., 2018). The tubes were immersed in a water bath maintained at either 42 °C or 74 °C (NESLAB, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). A K-type thermocouple was inserted into the TDT tubes to monitor and record sample temperatures using a data logger thermometer (HH 374, Omega Engineering Inc., Stamford, Connecticut, USA). Following treatment, all samples were rapidly cooled to 10 °C in an ice-water bath. Thermal processing histories for CT40 and CT72 are presented in Figures S1 and S4, respectively.</p>	<p>The extraction of polar metabolites followed the method described by Mercali et al. (2025). Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling aliquots from all individual samples. Additionally, process blanks, prepared by performing the extraction procedure with water instead of juice, were included to identify any mass spectrometry features arising from laboratory consumables, as recommended by Broadhurst et al. (2018).</p>	<p>Untargeted full-scan data acquisition was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Agilent 1290 Infinity II) coupled to a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (QTOF-MS, Agilent 6546). Sample injection order was randomized to minimize systematic bias. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 x 50 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water with 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0% B for 0.5 min, ramping linearly to 100% B over 8.0 min, held at 100% B for 1.0 min, followed by re-equilibration at 0% B for 1.0 min (adapted from Bilbrey et al. (2021)). Samples were kept at 8 °C prior to injection, with a 3 μL injection volume.</p>	<p>Untargeted full-scan data acquisition was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Agilent 1290 Infinity II) coupled to a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (QTOF-MS, Agilent 6546). The QTOF-MS operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes, with data acquisition over an m/z range of 50-1700 u. Instrument settings for MS and MS/MS acquisition followed the parameters described by Mercali et al. (2025). MS/MS spectra for analytical standards were acquired in both ionization modes and are available in PhenolicsDB (Quiroz-Moreno, 2023).</p>	<p>The data generated from the UHPLC-QTOF-MS analysis were converted to *.mzML format using the ProteoWizard software (Chambers et al., 2012). The converted files were subsequently analyzed using the open-source platform MZMine 3.3.0 (Schmid et al., 2023) for peak deconvolution. Detailed parameter settings for the workflow are provided in Supplementary Material, Tables S1 and S2. The feature tables (*.csv) for negative and positive ionization modes are detailed in Tables S5 and S6 (Supplementary Material), respectively. Data processing was conducted in R (v4.4.0). Signal intensities were normalized by sample mass and total soluble solids (TSS). Metabolites showing less than a 10-fold increase in QC samples over blanks were excluded. Only features present in all QC samples with a coefficient of variation below 30% were retained. Missing values were imputed using half the minimum peak area. Clustering was performed using the notame package (Klåvus et al., 2020)to collapse ions resulting from the same metabolite. Final feature tables for negative and positive ionization modes are provided in Supplementary Tables S7 and S8, respectively. All data were log2-transformed prior to analysis. PCA was used for dimensionality reduction and cluster visualization, with plots generated via ggplot2 (Hadley Wickham., 2016). Significantly different features (p ≤ 0.05 after FDR multiple testing correction) were visualized using heatmaps with hierarchical clustering (Euclidean distance, Ward’s method). Unpaired t-tests with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction were performed using the rstatix package (Kassambara, 2023). Methodological details are described by Mercali et al. (2025).</p>	<p>Metabolites were annotated using various methods aligned with the Metabolomics Standards Initiative guidelines (Sumner et al., 2007). For level 1 identification, retention times and spectral data (MS and MS/MS) from QC samples were matched against PhenolicsDB in-house library of standards (Quiroz-Moreno, 2023). For level 2 annotation, raw MS/MS data in Agilent (*.d) format were converted to *.mzML files using ProteoWizard software (Chambers et al., 2012). The spectral data were then processed and deconvoluted using MZmine 3.3.0 (detailed in Supplementary Material, Tables S3 and S4). Negative and positive ionization modes were analyzed separately, resulting in two peak lists for metabolite identification. These lists were further analyzed using library search algorithms available on the Global Natural Products Social (GNPS) platform (Hunter &amp; Harold, 1975). Altogether 3 GNPS tools were employed: Classical Molecular Networking, Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN) and Library Search. Molecular network construction was based on a precursor ion mass tolerance of 0.02 Da, with fragment ion mass differences also set to 0.02 Da. Connections between nodes were established only if the cosine similarity score was greater than 0.7, with a minimum of four matched peaks in the fragmentation spectra. The resulting molecular networks were then compared against GNPS spectral libraries, applying identical data parameters to ensure consistency between sample and library spectra.</p>

Study Title	Biological Characteristics of SARS-CoV-2 Resistant Populations: A Cohort Comparison Study through Integrated Gut Microbiota Sequencing, Metabolomics and Proteomics
Study Description	<p>Objective: Most research reports on COVID-19 infections have focused on the correlation between the severity of the disease symptoms and immune deficits, while the mechanisms affecting the susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 remain largely unknown. The study aimed to comprehensively analyze the differences in immunity, gut microbiota, metabolism, and proteomics between the SARS-CoV-2 resistant population and susceptible population. Methods and Results: In this cohort comparison study, participants were rigorously selected based on inclusion and exclusion criteria in a continuous enrollment manner through continuous enrollment using combined questionnaires and clinical data, ultimately including 25 SARS-CoV-2 resistant volunteers versus 16 SARS-CoV-2 infected patients.The clinical information of the participants was recorded in detail, and fecal and blood samples were collected in a standardized manner for subsequent multi omics analysis, including gut microbiota sequencing, metabolomics, and proteomics. This study has preliminarily elucidated the characteristics of the gut microbiota, serum metabolites, and serum proteins in the SARS-CoV-2 resistant population. It exhibits a unique metabolic signature characterized by elevated levels of serum phosphatidylinositol and the abundance of Prevotella, which may serve as a potential predictive biomarker for resistance to SARS-CoV-2. Conclusion: Given the crucial role of phosphatidylinositol in cell membrane architecture and viral infectivity, this study provides a promising entry point for further research into the pathogenesis and prevention strategies of COVID-19.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Fasting peripheral venous blood was drawn from participants and collected in vacuum blood collection tubes without anticoagulants (red cap). The blood was allowed to clot and form layers by standing at room temperature for 1 hour. Using a medical low-speed centrifuge at 3000rpm for 10 minutes at room temperature, the blood was separated, and the serum was transferred to clean centrifuge tubes. Finally, a high-speed centrifuge at 12000rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C was used to transfer the serum to centrifuge tubes or cryogenic tubes, approximately 0.5mL per tube. To ensure integrity, multiple tubes were filled as much as possible and stored in the refrigerator at a freezing temperature of -80°C. Adequate dry ice was used for shipment to ensure sample stability for subsequent metabolomics and proteomics analysis.</p>	<p>The collected samples were thawed on ice, and metabolite were extracted with 50% methanol</p><p>Buffer. Briefly, 20 μL of sample was extracted with 120 μL of precooled 50% methanol, vortexed</p><p>for 1 min, and incubated at room temperature for 10 min; the extraction mixture was then stored</p><p>overnight at -20°C. After centrifugation at 4,000 g for 20 min, the supernatants were transferred</p><p>into new 96-well plates. The samples were stored at -80°C prior to the LC-MS analysis. In</p><p>addition, pooled QC samples were also prepared by combining 10 μL of each extraction</p><p>mixture</p>	<p>All samples were acquired by the LC-MS system followed machine orders. Firstly, all</p><p>chromatographic separations were performed using a Thermo Scientific UltiMate 3000 HPLC. An</p><p>ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100mm*2.1mm, 1.8µm, Waters, UK) was used for the</p><p>reversed phase separation. The column oven was maintained at 35°C. The flow rate was 0.4</p><p>ml/min and the mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water, 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B</p><p>(Acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid). </p>	<p>A high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer Q-Exactive (Thermo Scientific) was used to detect</p><p>metabolites eluted form the column. The Q-Exactive was operated in both positive and negative</p><p>ion modes. Precursor spectra (70–1050 m/z) were collected at 70,000 resolution to hit an AGC</p><p>target of 3e6. The maximum inject time was set to 100 ms. A top 3 configuration to acquire data</p><p>was set in DDA mode. Fragment spectra were collected at 17,500 resolution to hit an AGC target</p><p>of 1e5 with a maximum inject time of 80 ms.In order to evaluate the stability of the LC-MS during</p><p>the whole acquisition, a quality control sample (Pool of all samples) was acquired after every 10</p><p>samples.</p>	<p>The acquired MS data pretreatments including peak picking, peak grouping, retention time</p><p>correction, second peak grouping, and annotation of isotopes and adducts was performed using</p><p>XCMS software. LCMS raw data files were converted into mzXML format and then processed</p><p>by the XCMS, CAMERA and metaX[2] toolbox implemented with the R software. Each ion was</p><p>identified by combining retention time (RT) and m/z data. Intensities of each peaks were recorded</p><p>and a three dimensional matrix containing arbitrarily assigned peak indices (retention time-m/z</p><p>pairs), sample names (observations) and ion intensity information (variables) was generated. The online KEGG, HMDB database was used to annotate the metabolites by matching the exact</p><p>molecular mass data (m/z) of samples with those from database. If a mass difference between</p><p>observed and the database value was less than 10 ppm, the metabolite would be annotated and the</p><p>molecular formula of metabolites would further be identified and validated by the isotopic</p><p>distribution measurements. We also used a in-house fragment spectrum library of metabolites to</p><p>validate the metabolite identidification. The intensity of peak data was further preprocessed by metaX[2]. Those features that were</p><p>detected in less than 50% of QC samples or 80% of biological samples were removed, the</p><p>remaining peaks with missing values were imputed with the k-nearest neighbor algorithm to</p><p>further improve the data quality. PCA was performed for outlier detection and batch effects</p><p>evaluation using the pre-processed dataset. Quality control-based robust LOESS signal correction</p><p>was fitted to the QC data with respect to the order of injection to minimize signal intensity drift</p><p>over time. In addition, the relative standard deviations of the metabolic features were calculated</p><p>across all QC samples, and those &gt; 30% were then removed. Student t-tests were conducted to detect differences in metabolite concentrations between 2</p><p>phenotype.The P value was adjusted for multiple tests using an FDR (Benjamini–Hochberg). Supervised PLS-DA was conducted through metaX to discriminate the different variables between groups. The VIP value was calculated. A VIP cut-off value of 1.0 was used to select important</p><p>features.</p>	<p>Peak extraction is mainly achieved through the open-source software XCMS. Including peak alignment, peak extraction, normalization, deconvolution, and compound identification</p><p>Waiting steps. Post processing of extracted peaks mainly includes primary identification and quantitative analysis of metabolites.</p>

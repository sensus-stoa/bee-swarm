Study Title	Metabolomic analysis of the Portuguese man-of-war
Study Description	<p>Understanding the toxicity of the Portuguese man-of-war, <em>Physalia physalis</em> is of paramount importance, given its important ecological role and potential threats to human health and other organisms. The tentacles of <em>P. physalis</em> are quite distinct from those of other jellyfish, in that two distinct sizes of nematocysts are located on only one side of the tentacular palpons. To investigate the composition of the toxins or venom of <em>P. physalis</em>, a comprehensive metabolomic analysis of the tentacular palpons was conducted to identify the toxic metabolites present in<em> P. physalis</em>.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Adult <em>P. physails</em> colonies were collected from waters near Zhaoshu Island (Hainan, China) in January 2024, and transported back to laboratory aquariums for cultivation. Tentacular palpons (n=6) were excised from a healthy adult <em>P. physalis</em> colony and gently rinsed with filtered artificial sea water to remove surface impurities, followed by rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen and lyophilization using a freeze-dryer.</p>	<p>Tentacular palpons (n=5) were excised from a healthy adult<em> P. physalis</em> colony for toxin proteomic analysis. Each sample was treated with 300 μL of 8M urea containing protease inhibitor (10% of lysate volume). After centrifugation at 14,100×g for 20 min, the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method, and the remaining supernatant was stored at -80°C. A 50 µg aliquot of extracted proteins from each sample was reduced with 200 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 37°C for 1 h, then diluted 8-fold with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) buffer. Trypsin was added at a 1:25 (trypsin:protein) ratio, and digestion was performed overnight at 37°C. The reaction was terminated with 50 μL of 0.1% formic acid (FA). Samples were desalted using a C18 column pre-washed with 100 μL of 100% acetonitrile (ACN, centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 3 min) and 100 μL of 0.1% FA (same centrifugation). ≤30 μg of sample was loaded, followed by two washes with 100 μL of 0.1% FA and one wash with 100 μL of pH 10 water. Elution used a 10-step ACN gradient (6%, 9%, 12%, 15%, 18%, 21%, 25%, 30%, 35%, 50% in pH 10 water), with eluates pooled into three fractions (6%,15%,25%,50%; 9%,18%,30%; 12%,21%,35%). Fractions were lyophilized and stored at -80°C until LC-MS/MS.</p>	<p>Chromatography Instrument: RIGOL L-3000 High-Performance Liquid Chromatography System</p><p>Mobile Phase: A (0.1% formic acid in water); B (0.1% formic acid in ACN)</p><p>Gradient: Solvent B held at 4% for 8 min, increased to 10% over 3 min, 10–25% over 77 min, 25–50% over 10 min, 50–99% over 4 min, held at 99% for 6 min, decreased to 4% over 4 min, and held at 4% for 8 min.</p><p>Flow Rate: Loading pump: 300 nL/min for 15 min; separation: 300 nL/min for 120 min</p><p>Injection Volume: 10 μL (sandwich loading method)</p><p>Column Type: Reverse Phase (C18-based for desalting and separation)</p>	<p>Instrument: Thermo Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer</p><p>Scan Parameters: Full MS scan (m/z 250–1450) at 120,000 resolution; data-dependent CID MS/MS on top 20 ions with an isolation window. Normalized collision energy: 30%; activation time: 50 ms.</p><p>Acquisition Mode: Second-stage data collected in linear ion trap fast mode, with automatic gain control (AGC) 7,000, maximum injection time 35 ms, and dynamic exclusion time 18 s.</p><p>Identification Parameters: Precursor ion mass tolerance: ±15 ppm; fragment ion mass tolerance: ±0.5 Da; max missed cleavages: 2. Static modification: carbamidomethylation of Cys residues (57.021 Da); dynamic modifications: oxidation of Met residues (+15.995 Da).</p><p>Data Processing: MS/MS data analyzed using MaxQuant 1.5.2.8.</p>	<p>Raw mass spectrometry data (.raw format) were processed using MaxQuant 1.5.2.8 for protein identification and quantification. For label-free quantification, the total peak area normalization method was applied, where each protein's peak area in a sample was divided by the total peak area of all proteins in that sample to standardize the data. Subsequent statistical analyses (including principal component analysis, relative standard deviation calculation, and K-means clustering) were performed using R software (version 3.1.1) with packages such as scales, ggplot2, ggrepel, and scatterplot3d. Data scaling was conducted using UV scaling to ensure consistency across samples.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification and annotation were performed using multiple reference databases and bioinformatics tools. The main databases included:</p><p>KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes): Used for pathway annotation to determine the involvement of metabolites in biochemical pathways, with results summarized in files such as meta_kegg_anno.xls and meta_kegg_anno_categroy.xls.</p><p>HMDB (Human Metabolome Database): Employed for detailed metabolite information annotation, stored in meta_hmdb_anno.xls and meta_hmdb_anno_categroy.xls.</p><p>Lipidmaps: Specialized in lipid metabolite classification and annotation, with results documented in meta_lipidmaps_anno.xls, meta_lipidmaps_anno_categroy.xls, and visualized in meta_lipidmaps_anno.png (top 20 categories).</p><p>Annotation pipelines were integrated into the BMKCloud platform (www.biocloud.net), ensuring systematic matching of detected metabolites with database entries for functional classification and pathway mapping.</p>

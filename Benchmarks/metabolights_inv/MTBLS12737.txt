Study Title	Prediction of irritable bowel syndrome by integrating urine metabolites and gut microbiota
Study Description	<p><strong>Abstract</strong></p><p><strong>Background</strong></p><p>Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a functional gastrointestinal disorder characterized by recurrent abdominal pain and altered bowel habits. While gut microbiota alterations and metabolic disturbances have been implicated in IBS, their diagnostic utility remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the potential of integrating gut microbiota profiling and urinary metabolomics for improved IBS classification.</p><p><strong>Methods</strong></p><p>Fifty-four participants (27 healthy controls, 27 IBS patients) were recruited for gut microbiota and urinary metabolite analysis. Gut microbiota composition was assessed via 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and urinary metabolites were profiled using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the predictive accuracy of gut microbiota, urinary metabolites, and their combined model.</p><p><strong>Results</strong></p><p>LEfSe analysis identified that Bacteroides eggerthii and Desulfovibrio desulfuricans as enriched in IBS patients, whereas Hallella bergensis&nbsp;and Faecalitalea cylindroides were predominant in healthy individuals. Urinary metabolite analysis revealed significant structural variationsalterations in metabolite profiles, with IBS patients exhibiting elevated fructose levels and trends of increased serine, mannose, and galactose. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that urinary metabolomics outperformed gut microbiota profiling, while a combined approach integrating both datasets achieved the highest predictive accuracy.</p><p><strong>Conclusion</strong></p><p>These findings highlight the predictive potential of integrating urinary metabolomics with gut microbiota profiling for IBS classification. The multi-marker approach demonstrated superior predictive accuracy, suggesting a promising non-invasive strategy for IBS classification and patient stratification.</p><p> </p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Urine samples were freshly collected midstream in the morning at the hospital using sterile 50 mL conical tubes. All samples were transported in mini cooler bags containing ice packs and subsequently stored at −80 degree Celsius until analysis.</p>	<p><strong>Extraction</strong>: 100 uL of urine was mixed with 900 uL of methanol and vortexed for 5 min. After centrifugation at 15,928 x g for 10 min at 4°C, 400 uL of the supernatant was transferred and mixed with 20 uL of ribitol solution (0.5 mg/mL in water, internal standard). The mixture was dried using a centrifugal vacuum concentrator (VC2124, Hanil Scientific Inc., Korea) for 12 hours.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Derivatization</strong>: Dried extracts were derivatized by adding 100 uL of O-methoxyamine hydrochloride (20 mg/mL in pyridine), followed by ultrasonication at 4°C for 20 min. After vortexing for 1.5 min, samples were incubated at 30°C for 90 min in the dark with shaking (75 rpm). Next, 50 uL of MSTFA was added and the mixture incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Samples were centrifuged (13,572 x g, 5 min, 4°C), and 80 uL of the supernatant was transferred to GC vials with 100 uL inserts.</p>	<p>GC-MS analysis was performed using a QP2020 (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with an RTX-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Restek, Bellefonte, PA, USA). The injector temperature was set to 230°C, and the transfer line temperature was set to 250°C. The oven temperature was initially held at 80°C for 2 min, then ramped at 15°C/min to 330°C and held for 6 min. The carrier gas was helium with a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Injection was performed in splitless mode. Quality control (QC) samples were included to ensure stability, performance, and reproducibility.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection was conducted using a Shimadzu QP2020 instrument (Kyoto, Japan) under electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV. The ion source temperature was not explicitly stated but the detector temperature was set to 280°C. Data acquisition was performed in full scan mode over an m/z range of 50–600 with positive polarity.</p>	<p>Raw data was processed using Shimadzu GCMS Postrun Analysis software. Files were converted to ABF format and imported into MS-DIAL v.4.9 for peak detection, alignment, and deconvolution.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed in MS-DIAL v.4.9 based on EI-MS spectra and Kovats retention indices. Identification was performed with a retention index tolerance of 20, an EI similarity cut-off of 90%, and an identification score cut-off of 90%, referencing EI-MS and Kovats RI. The identified spectra were further matched to the NIST v. 20.0 library and standard reagents. Peak intensities were normalized to ribitol.</p>

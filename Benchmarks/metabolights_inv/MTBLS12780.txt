Study Title	Integrated transcriptomics and metabolomics analysis of gasa1 and gasa4 mutants in response to P.syringae infection in Arabidopsis
Study Description	<p>The Gibberellic acid-stimulated Arabidopsis (GASA) gene family encodes a class of cysteine-rich proteins characterized by an N-terminal signal peptide and a conserved C-terminal GASA domain containing 12 cysteine (Cys) residues. These plant-specific small proteins have been identified across various species, where they primarily regulate plant growth and abiotic stress responses. However, the molecular mechanisms by which AtGASA1 and AtGASA4 contribute to Arabidopsis immunity against P.syringae remain unclear. In this study, we found that the gasa1 and gasa4 mutants exhibited increased susceptibility to P. syringae infection compared to Col-0. To elucidate these roles in plant immunity, we performed transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. Transcriptomic profiling identified 957 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the gasa1 and gasa4 mutants upon pathogen infection, with significant enrichment in plant-pathogen interaction, glycerolipid metabolism, and MAPK signaling pathways. Metabolomic analysis revealed 900 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs), enriched in pathways including ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, and biosynthesis of cofactors. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses further demonstrated that DEGs and DAMs were co-enriched in key pathways, including plant hormone signal transduction, biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites, and cysteine and methionine metabolism. Notably, within the plant hormone signal transduction pathway, we found that AtGASA1 and AtGASA4 regulate the expression of multiple genes involved in the salicylic acid (SA) signaling pathway, such as ICS1, EDS1, PAD4, PR1, and PR5. Consistently, SA levels in the gasa1 and gasa4 mutants were significantly lower than in Col-0. Our findings provide new insights into the immune functions of AtGASA1 and AtGASA4 in plant immunity against P. syringae infection.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Metabolomics sample collection: Leaves from four-week-old Col-0, gasa1, and gasa4 mutants were collected at 12 h post-infection with Psm ES4326. Additionally, Col-0 leaves were collected at 0 h post-infection as a control. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C until further analysis. Six biological replicates were collected for each time point and treatment.</p>	<p>Metabolites were extracted by weighing 50 mg of the sample into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of a 6 mm grinding bead and 400 µL extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v) containing L-2-chlorophenylalanine (0.02 mg/mL) as internal standards. The samples were homogenized using a frozen tissue grinder for 6 min at -10°C and 50 Hz, then subjected to low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min at 5°C and 40 kHz. After incubation at -20°C for 30 min, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was transferred to injection vials for LC-MS analysis by UHPLC -Q Exactive HF-X. Additionally, 20 µL of the supernatant from each sample was pooled to generate a QC sample.</p><p><br></p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 µm; Waters, Milford, USA). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (95% water, 5% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (47.5% acetonitrile, 47.5% isopropanol, 5% water with 0.1% formic acid). The injection volume was set to 3 μL, and the column temperature was maintained at 40°C.</p><p><br></p>	<p>Samples were analyzed using electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive and negative ion modes. The ESI source parameters were set as follows: Scan range (m/z), 70–1050; Spray voltage (kV), 3.4 (ESI+) and 3.4 (ESI-); Heater temperature (°C), 350; Capillary temperature (°C), 320; Sheath gas flow rate (arb), 60; Auxiliary gas flow rate (arb), 20; Resolution (Full MS), 60000; Resolution (MS/MS), 15000; Normalized collision energy (eV), 20, 40, 60.</p><p><br></p>	<p>The raw data were processed using Progenesis QI v3.0 software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA).&nbsp;</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was conducted by matching MS/MS spectral data in the HMDB database (http://www.hmdb.ca/) with an m/z tolerance of less than 10 ppm. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). Metabolites with VIP≧1 and p-value&lt;0.05&nbsp;</p>

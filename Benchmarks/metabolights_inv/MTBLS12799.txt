Study Title	The role of mycobiome in terroir and during Muscat grapes fermentation unveiled by multi-omic analysis
Study Description	<p>The wine microbiome is a key determinant in shaping wine terroir. To date, a comprehensive understanding of how microbial signatures influence wine metabolic profile remains poorly understood. To address this, in the present study an integrated shotgun metagenomics and untargeted metabolomic approach was employed to investigate the wine metabolome and connect the composition and functions of microbiomes involved in wine fermentation of Muscat grapes harvested in Italy and Greece. Beta diversity highlighted the dissimilarity between Italian and Greek fungal terroirs. A marked reduction in diversity during fermentation underscored the dominance of the inoculated&nbsp;Saccharomyces cerevisiae&nbsp;starter culture. The LEfSe analysis revealed an enrichment of&nbsp;Torulaspora delbrueckii&nbsp;in Greek samples, while&nbsp;Kluyveromyces marxianus&nbsp;and&nbsp;lactis&nbsp;were more abundant in Italian samples. Functional analysis revealed geographic differences in nucleotide, fatty acids and lysine metabolisms. Significant shifts were observed in energy, carbohydrate, and amino acid metabolisms, reflecting terroir-specific microbial activity. The metabolomics data highlighted regional differences in oligosaccharides, glycosylated phenolics, peptide and amino acid turnover, and central redox metabolites, suggesting divergent microbial responses and metabolic trajectories shaped by terroir and fermentation conditions. Obtained results highlight the effectiveness of this multi-omics approach in identifying product-specific fungal communities and wine metabolite signatures, providing new tools that could be used to ensure wine authenticity and quality control.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Vitis vinifera L. Muscat grapes from the 2023 vintage were harvested in Piedmont (Italy) and Limnos Island (Greece) and vinified in two wineries following the same white winemaking protocol. Must and wine samples were collected in triplicate at three time points: before inoculation (D0), at 72 hours (D3), and at the end of fermentation (EF). Samples were immediately cooled, centrifuged (5000×g, 10 min, 4 °C), and stored at −20 °C for further analyses. Sample codes indicated country (I or G), fermentation stage (M, D0, D3, EF), and replicate (1–3).</p>	<p>Initially each samples was centrifuged in 14,000 rpm for 10 min. Then, 200 μL of the supernatant were diluted with 800 μL of water, and finally they were filtered by 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filters and placed into 2 mL dark LCMS glass vials. Quality Control (QC) samples were produced by mixing equal portions of each sample and then were treated like all other samples.</p>	<p>The LC-MS (Liquid Chromatography – Mass Spectrometry) analysis was performed by using an Acquity I Class UPLC connected with a Synapt XS HDMS-Qtof-MS mass spectrometer, and by using an Acquity TM Premier HSS T3 with VanGuard Fit, 1.8 µm, 2.1x100 mm column (Waters Corporation, Italy). The instrumental parameters applied were in accordance with the protocol developed by Saez et al. [41]. Briefly, the injection volume was 2 µL, the vials in the sample manager was kept at 10 °C, the column manager oven was set at 40 °C, the mobile phases were water (A) and methanol (B), both with 0.1% of formic acid, and the mobile phase flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. The gradient is as follows: 0.0 to 1.0 min (0% B), 3.0 to 12.0 min (10-40 %B), 12.0 to 16.0 min (40-100%B), 16.0 to 18.0 min (100% B), 18.0 to 18.10 min (100 to 0% B), 18.10-20.0 min (0% B).</p>	<p>The MS detector was set in Resolution mode for both polarities (ESI+ and ESI-). The source temperature was 120 °C, the desolvation temperature was 450 °C, the cone gas flow was 50 L/h, and the desolvation gas flow was 800 L/h. The capillary for ESI + was 3kV, and for ESI- was 2kV. The MSE method applied record the spectra in a range of 50 to 2000 Da in-continuum mode acquisition in a normal dynamic range, with a scan time of 0.2 seconds. For high energy function the collision energies applied in the transfer clactisollision cell differ for each polarity, in positive the collision energy is ramped from 20 to 40 V, whereas in negative from 20 to 50 V. A solution of 100 pg/ µL of Leucine Enkephalin was used as Lock mass solution and was constantly flowing at 10 µL/min during all the data acquisition, with a scan average of 3 in an interval of 15 seconds. The lock mass solution capillary was set at 3.0 kV and at 2.6 kV for ESI + and ESI - respectively.</p>	<p>The raw data was acquired using MassLynx v4.2 software and the data treatment (peak picking, alignment, etc) and metabolite annotation were performed by using the Progenesis QI software (Version 2.4, nonlinear Dynamics). Raw files were imported directly into the Progenesis QI software, after the peak picking, then runs were align by using a QC sample as alignment reference, by applying the maximum level of sensitivity, and by using only the features between 0.03 and 19.00 min (retention time limits).</p>	<p><br></p><p>First level annotation was made by using the internal database, by comparing the rt, mass accuracy and fragments of the standards against the one of the biomarkers. Then, by using Progenesis QI annotation tool, for the compound identification the external database of ChEBI, NIST, Human Metabolome Data Base and Pubchem were used, by applying a precursor tolerance of 5 ppm, a fragmentation tolerance of 10 ppm and an isotope similarity of 70%. Then all the tentative annotations were manually controlled and compared, when possible, with the internal data base. The annotation of the metabolites was made according to the four levels of annotations&nbsp;</p>

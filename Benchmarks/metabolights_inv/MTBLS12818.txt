Study Title	Dietary hispidulin ameliorated chemically-Induced colitis by inhibiting epithelial cell ferroptosis via the ACAT2-GPX4 axis and remodeling the gut microbiota
Study Description	<p>Hispidulin, a dietary flavonoid occurring naturally in brown algae, has attracted considerable interest owing to its functions in modulating inflammatory responses and neutralizing reactive oxygen species. This study examined the consequences of hispidulin in a model of chemically induced acute colitis. RNA sequencing (RNA_seq), 16s rRNA_seq, and non-targeted metabolome were conducted to determine the mechanisms underlying hispidulin-induced effects on colitis. The findings revealed that hispidulin exhibited significant therapeutic benefits against colitis. In addition, treatment with hispidulin led to a decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokine levels as well as serum FITC-dextran concentrations. RNA_seq analysis demonstrated a notable increase in colonic ACAT2 expression in mice with colitis following hispidulin treatment. Furthermore, hispidulin administration resulted in the upregulation of GPX4, a reduction in ROS levels, and inhibition of epithelial ferroptosis in mice with colitis. Both genetic inhibition of ACAT2 in vitro and AAV-mediated knockdown of ACAT2 in vivo substantially negated the effects of hispidulin on GPX4/GSH levels, ROS levels, and lipid hydroperoxide accumulation in both NCM460 cells and epithelial cells derived from DSS exposed murine models. 16s rRNA_seq results showed that hispidulin increased abundance of Lactobacillus, NK4A136_group, Oscillibacter, Peptococcus, Adlercreutzia and decreased abundance of Turicimonas in colitic mice. Non-targeted metabolome results showed that hispidulin facilitated the metabolism of galactose and promoted unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis in colitic mice. In summary, hispidulin reduced the advancement of acute colitis in mice by lessening ACAT2-mediated ferroptosis in epithelial cells, as well as altering composition of gut microbiota.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The samples were mouse (C57) fecal samples. The DSS model group (DSS group) and the DSS administration group (FUC&nbsp;group) were given DSS by drinking water for 7 days, and the FUC group was given Hispidulin by gavage. On April 7, 2025, feces were collected from the School of Pharmacy, Dalian Medical University, stored on drikold, and sent for metabolomics analysis.</p>	<p>1)Accurately weigh an appropriate amount of sample into a 2 mL EP tube, add 600 µL of 2-chlorophenylalanine (4 ppm) prepared in methanol, then vortex for 30 seconds.</p><p>2)Add steel beads and place the tube into a tissue grinder. Grind at 50 Hz for 120 seconds.</p><p>3)Ultrasonicate at room temperature for 10 minutes.</p><p>4)Centrifuge at 12,000 rpm, 4 °C for 10 minutes. Collect the supernatant and filter through a 0.22 μm membrane. Transfer the filtrate into a vial for analysis.</p><p>5)Take 20 µL from each sample to mix into a QC sample. The QC sample is used to correct deviations in the analysis results of the mixed samples and errors caused by the instrument itself. If the number of experimental samples exceeds 20, QC samples will be injected for quality control;&nbsp;&nbsp;if 20 or fewer samples are tested, QC injections will not be arranged.</p><p>6)Use the remaining samples for LC-MS detection.</p>	<p>1)Instrument: Thermo Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, ultra-high performance liquid chromatography system.</p><p>2)Column: ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3, 1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm (Waters, Milford, MA, USA).</p><p>3)Chromatographic separation conditions: Column temperature 40 °C; flow rate 0.30 mL/min; mobile phase for positive ion mode: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B2) and 0.1% formic acid in water (A2); mobile phase for negative ion mode: acetonitrile (B3) and 5 mM ammonium formate in water (A3); injection volume 2 µL.</p><p>4)Gradient elution program:</p><p>i. Positive ion mode: 0–1 min: 10% B2; 1–5 min: 10% to 98% B2; 5–6.5 min: 98% B2; 6.5–6.6 min: 98% to 10% B2; 6.6–8 min: 10% B2&nbsp;</p><p>ii. Negative ion mode: 0–1 min: 10% B3; 1–5 min: 10% to 98% B3; 5–6.5 min: 98% B3; 6.5–6.6 min: 98% to 10% B3; 6.6–8 min: 10% B3</p>	<p>1)Instrument: Thermo Q Exactive Focus mass spectrometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA.</p><p>2)MS Conditions: Electrospray ionization source (ESI), data collected in both positive and negative ion modes. Positive ion spray voltage: 3.50 kV; negative ion spray voltage: -2.50 kV; sheath gas: 40 arb; auxiliary gas: 10 arb; capillary temperature: 325 °C. Full MS scan was performed at a resolution of 70,000 with a scan range of m/z 100–1000. Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) was used for MS/MS fragmentation with a collision energy of 30 eV. MS/MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 17,500. Fragmentation was performed on the top 3 ions per cycle, with dynamic exclusion enabled to avoid unnecessary repeated fragmentation of the same ions.</p>	<p>1)Converting the raw mass spectrometry data files to mzXML format using the MSConvert tool from the ProteoWizard software package.</p><p>2)Performing peak identification, peak filtration, and peak alignment using the XCMS package in R. Key parameters include:bw = 2, ppm = 15, peakwidth = c(5, 30), mzwid = 0.015, mzdiff = 0.01, method = 'centWave'.</p><p>3)Generating a data matrix containing information such as mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), retention time (rt), and peak intensity, and exporting the data to Excel for further analysis.</p><p>4)To enable comparison across different data scales, total peak area normalization (Sum peak normalization) is applied for quantitative value correction.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed by searching and matching against spectral databases including HMDB, MassBank, METLIN, MoNA, mzVault, KEGG, and an in-house metabolite standard database. All metabolites in the quantitative list were compared and matched with MS1/MS2 fragment ion information from each database entry to achieve compound identification and annotation with corresponding database identifiers.</p>

Study Title	Toxoplasma gondii metabolic flexibility in response to iron deprivation
Study Description	<p>Iron is an essential nutrient for many critical cellular processes. As a consequence of diverse and dynamic host environments, the obligate intracellular parasite Toxoplasma gondii must adapt to iron limited conditions. To investigate the adaptations critical to parasite survival under these conditions, we conducted proteomic and metabolomic profiling of Toxoplasma cultured in iron depleted conditions. We find that iron depletion results in remodelling of the parasite proteome and triggers swift translational repression. This occurs before downregulation of the iron regulated translation factor ABCE1, indicating an upstream, ABCE1-independent mechanism. In this background of repressed translation, we also observe significant rewiring of energy metabolism. Iron depleted Toxoplasma have altered mitochondrial morphology and a profound reduction to mitochondrial respiration. Untargeted metabolomic analysis revealed tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) cycle dysregulation, characterised by accumulation of citrate and fumarate, both substrates of iron-dependent TCA cycle enzymes, and accumulation of glycolytic intermediates. We found iron deprived parasites continue to uptake glucose and maintain glycolytic output, comparable to iron replete conditions. Limiting glucose availability in culture media or genetic ablation of glucose uptake causes a significant increases in sensitivity to iron restriction. Conversely, limitation of mitochondrially metabolised glutamine improved parasite fitness in iron depleted conditions. Together, our results establish iron as a key regulator of parasite translation and metabolic flexibility and demonstrate carbon source availability as important in Toxoplasma adaptation to iron deprivation. </p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Metabolites were extracted from Toxoplasma tachyzoites cultured on human foreskin fibroblasts. </p><p>For control conditions 2 x 15 cm dishes containing confluent HFFs for control conditions and 4 x 15 cm dishes for iron deprived conditions were pooled to generate each replicate. For iron deprivation, confluent host cells in 15 cm dishes were incubated with D3 media containing 100 µM deferoxamine for 24 hours prior to infection. After 24 hours of infection dishes were moved to a container of ice water to rapidly quench metabolism. Cells were washed in ice-cold PBS twice to remove extracellular parasites and old media, before being scraped and passed through a 25 gauge needle to mechanically release parasites. Parasites were filtered from host material through a 3 µm filter before centrifugation at 1 °C for 25 mins at 3000 xg. Pellets were resuspended and washed in 1 ml ice-cold PBS before being counted using a haemocytometer. 4 x 10^7 parasites were resuspended in 200 µl of a chloroform /methanol/water mixture (1:3:1 ratio). Samples were shaken for 1 hour at 4 °C and centrifuged at 20,000 xg for 5 minutes at 4°C. Metabolites in the supernatant were collected. Samples were then stored at -80 until analysis by mass spectrometry.</p>	<p>Samples were extracted using a mixture of 1:3:1 Chloroform methanol water. 100 uL of extraction solvent was used per 20,000,000 tachyzoites. An identical tube without parasites was incubated alongside experimental samples to act as a blank. All samples were incubated shaking for 1 hour at 4°C before being centrifuged at 20,000 xg. Metabolites in supernatant were collected. A pooled sample was prepared using 5 ul from each sample.</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p><br></p>	<p>Metabolites were quantified by LCMS as previously described (Pountain and Barrett, 2019). Samples were separated by high performance liquid chromatography on a Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLC system (Thermo) using a ZIC-pHILIC column (Merck).&nbsp;Metabolite separation was performed at a column temperature of 25 °C using a linear gradient elution between two mobile phases: solvent A (20 mM ammonium carbonate in water) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The gradient began with a 20:80 ratio of A:B and was shifted to 80:20 over 15 minutes. This was followed by a rapid transition to 95:5, which was held for 2 minutes. The gradient was then quickly returned to the initial 20:80 ratio and maintained for an additional 9 minutes to re-equilibrate the column.</p><p>The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min, with an injection volume of 10 μL. Samples were kept at 5 °C prior to injection to preserve metabolite integrity. </p>	<p>Data were acquired using high-resolution mass spectrometry at a resolution of 70,000 (FWHM at m/z 200), with an AGC target of 1e6 and a scan range of m/z 70–1050. The instrument was operated with sheath gas set to 40, auxiliary gas to 5, and sweep gas to 1. The probe and capillary temperatures were maintained at 150 °C and 320 °C, respectively.</p><p>For positive ion mode, the source voltage was +3.8 kV, with an S-Lens RF level of 30.00, S-Lens voltage of –25.00 V, skimmer voltage of –15.00 V, inject flatapole offset of –8.00 V, and bent flatapole DC of –6.00 V. For negative ion mode, the source voltage was –3.8 kV.</p><p>To extend the calibration range for small metabolites, low-mass calibrants were added to the standard Thermo Calmix mixture: butylamine (m/z 74.096426) for positive ion electrospray ionisation (PIESI) and COF3 (m/z 84.9906726) for negative ion electrospray ionisation (NIESI). Lock-mass correction was applied throughout all analytical runs to enhance mass accuracy and calibration stability.</p>	<p>Data were converted to .mzXML files according to their ionization mode.&nbsp;</p>	<p>mzXML files were processed on the IDEOM platform. Peak detection was performed with XCMS and MZ match was used for peak annotation. IDEOM macro tools were used for data filtering and putative metabolite assignment. Features with poor peak shape or multiple peaks were excluded.</p><p>Data were normalised across conditions by median normalisation. Missing values were imputed with 1/5 the minimum detected value. Data were also log transformed and mean centred.</p>

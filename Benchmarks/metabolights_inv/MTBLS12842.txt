Study Title	Lignans redress autoimmunity and achieve curative effects on myasthenia gravis by ameliorating the gut microbiome
Study Description	<p>Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a serious inflammatory autoimmune disease characterized by continuous production of autoantibodies against targets in the neuromuscular junctions, resulting in muscle weakness. Autoimmunity has long been presumed to be irreversible. The current therapeutic regimens may alleviate the disease conditions, but curative outcomes can hardly be achieved. In this study, we assessed the curative potential of lignans on MG. Lignans, prevalent in edible plants such as flaxseed (flaxseed lignans, FL), have been shown to possess the intrinsic ability to inhibit inflammation. However, whether lignans may reverse the autoimmune state and provide curative therapies for MG is unknown.We established a C57BL/6J mouse model of experimental autoimmune myasthenia gravis (EAMG), using R97-116 peptide as the antigen, to evaluate the effects of FL on MG and investigate the molecular mechanisms of effects by multi-omics analyses.&nbsp;FL treatment effectively halted the progression of EAMG as reflected by the markedly alleviated muscle atrophy and significantly elevated muscle strength.&nbsp;The gut microbiome composition was profoundly remodeled, the gut barrier was significantly ameliorated, and the levels of the anti-R97-116 IgG autoantibody were radically lowered, all signs suggesting reversed conditions of the experimental MG by lignans.&nbsp;Mechanistically, the curative effects were associated with the inhibition of the Rap1gds1/Cdc42 signaling pathway.&nbsp;Lignans could reverse MG pathogenesis and achieve curative effects on MG by redressing the inflammatory autoimmune state through ameliorating the gut microbiome, modulating the metabolites, mitigating the gut barrier damage, and inhibiting the Rap1gds1/Cdc42 signaling pathway.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Fecal samples were collected from 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice. Mice were transferred to clean, sterile cages (one mouse per cage) 24 hours before sample collection to avoid cross-contamination of feces between individuals. </p>	<p>100 mg solid sample was added to a 2 mL centrifuge tube and a 6 mm diameter grinding bead was added. Frozen fecal samples were thawed on ice. Approximately 50 mg of each fecal sample was accurately weighed into a sterile EP tube, followed by the addition of 500 μL of extraction solvent (methanol:acetonitrile:water = 2:2:1, v/v/v) containing internal standards (e.g., L-2-chlorophenylalanine). The mixture was vortexed vigorously for 30 seconds to homogenize, then subjected to ultrasonic extraction in an ice bath for 30 minutes (power: 300 W, interval: 5 seconds). After sonication, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C to precipitate debris. The supernatant was carefully transferred to a new EP tube and dried under nitrogen flow at room temperature. The dried residues were reconstituted in 100 μL of 50% methanol (v/v) aqueous solution, vortexed for 1 minute, and centrifuged again at 12,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C. The final supernatant was collected and stored at -80°C until untargeted metabolomics analysis (e.g., LC-MS/MS).</p>	<p>The LC-MS/MS analysis of sample was conducted on a UHPLC-Q Executive HF-X system equipped with an&nbsp;ACQUITY&nbsp;HSS T3&nbsp;column&nbsp;(100 mm&nbsp;×&nbsp;2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm;&nbsp;Waters,&nbsp;USA) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water:acetonitrile&nbsp;(2:98, v/v)&nbsp;(solvent&nbsp;A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent&nbsp;B). The flow rate was 0.40&nbsp;mL/min&nbsp;and the column temperature was 40°C. The injection volume was 5 μL.</p>	<p>The UHPLC system was coupled to a UHPLC-Q Executive HF-X system Mass Spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in positive mode and negative mode. The optimal conditions were set as followed: source temperature at 400°C ; sheath gas flow rate at 40 arb; Aux gas flow rate at 10 arb; ion-spray voltage floating (ISVF) at&nbsp;-2800V&nbsp;in negative mode and 3500V in positive mode, respectively; Normalized collision energy ,&nbsp;20-40-60V&nbsp;rolling for MS/MS. Data acquisition was performed with the Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode. The detection was carried out over a mass range of&nbsp;70-1050&nbsp;m/z.</p>	<p>The following describes the methods used for converting raw metabolomics data to a format suitable for downstream analysis:</p><p><br></p><p>Raw data were generated as instrument-specific binary files (e.g., .raw files for Thermo Fisher mass spectrometers) during untargeted metabolomics analysis. These raw files were first converted to open, standardized formats to enable compatibility with downstream processing software.</p><p><br></p><p>Conversion was performed using&nbsp;ProteoWizard (version 3.0.21240), a widely used open-source tool for mass spectrometry data conversion and processing. The 'msconvert' module within ProteoWizard was employed with the following parameters:</p><p><br></p><p>Target format:&nbsp;mzML&nbsp;(a XML-based open format optimized for mass spectrometry data, retaining full metadata such as m/z values, ion intensities, retention times, and instrument parameters).</p><p>Filters applied:</p><p>Removal of low-intensity noise ions (intensity threshold: 1 count) to reduce file size without compromising biologically relevant signals.</p><p>Retention of all scan levels (MS1 and MS2 spectra) to preserve both precursor and fragment ion information.</p><p>Embedding of original metadata (e.g., sample ID, instrument model, acquisition date) to maintain data traceability.</p><p><br></p><p>Post-conversion, quality control checks were performed to validate the integrity of the converted files:</p><p><br></p><p>Confirmation that mzML files contained complete retention time and m/z arrays, with no loss of spectral data compared to the original .raw files.</p><p>Verification of consistent formatting across all samples using&nbsp;mzQC&nbsp;(a quality control format for mass spectrometry data) via the 'mzqc' tool, ensuring uniformity for subsequent batch processing.</p><p><br></p><p>Converted mzML files were then imported into downstream analysis pipelines (e.g., XCMS Online, MZmine 2) for peak picking, retention time alignment, and feature annotation, leveraging the standardized format to ensure reproducibility and compatibility across analytical tools.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry data were integrated with the HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/) and Metlin (http://metlin.scripps.edu/) databases&nbsp;to acquire metabolite information.</p>

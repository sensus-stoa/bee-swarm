Study Title	Metabolomic insights into the impact of cooking technologies on plant-based burger composition
Study Description	<p>This study evaluated the impact of different cooking methods (air frying, grilling, oven baking, and microwave cooking) on the chemical profile of chickpea-based burgers. For this purpose, an untargeted metabolomics approach was employed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-QTOF-MS). The results revealed that cooking significantly altered the metabolite composition of the samples. Multivariate and univariate analyses, including principal component analysis, hierarchical clustering, and ANOVA, consistently showed that the primary source of variation was the thermal treatment, with raw samples clearly separating from cooked ones. Notably, heat-sensitive compounds such as lipids and several flavonoids were degraded across all cooking methods. In contrast, some phenolic acids (caffeic acid and hydroxycinnamic acid) and flavonoids (luteolin, apigenin-7-apioglucoside, and apigenin-7-O-glucoside) exhibited increased relative intensities after cooking, suggesting the release of metabolites previously bound within cellular structures. Among the evaluated techniques, grilling induced the most pronounced metabolic alterations compared to raw samples, whereas microwave cooking had the least impact, showing better preservation of certain lipids and phenolic compounds. Nevertheless, none of the cooking methods caused major detrimental changes, indicating that all approaches are suitable for preparing chickpea-based burgers without compromising their chemical profile. This work highlights how the choice of cooking technology can modulate the metabolomic profile of plant-based products, providing valuable insights for consumers and the food industry regarding strategies to preserve nutritional quality while optimizing flavor and functionality.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Sample preparation:</strong> Chickpea-based burgers were developed for this study. This protein source was selected due to its high phenolic content and its capacity to contribute to desirable texture and flavor. Canned natural chickpeas (Fugini, São Paulo, Brazil) were used as the main ingredient. To mimic the appearance of commercial plant-based burgers, vegetables such as beetroot and carrot were incorporated to provide a pinkish color. Oat flour and sour starch were included as binding agents, while additional ingredients were added for seasoning. The complete formulation is detailed in Table S1 (Supplementary Material). Raw vegetables (beetroot, carrot, garlic, and onion) were blended in a food processor until a homogeneous mixture was obtained. Drained chickpeas were then added and gently pulsed to incorporate them without overprocessing (to avoid excess moisture). The mixture was transferred to a bowl, where the dry ingredients (oat flour, sour starch, and seasonings) were added. All components were thoroughly mixed until a uniform consistency was achieved. Burgers were manually shaped using a 10 cm diameter mold, with an average weight of 115 g each. The proximate composition (%) of the samples, determined using official AOAC analytical methods, is as follows: protein (13.38 ± 0.15), fat (0.31 ± 0.03), ash (4.98 ± 0.22), and carbohydrates (81.33 ± 0.16). The samples were stored at -20 °C and thawed under refrigeration (4 °C) for 24 h prior to the cooking experiments.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Cooking methods:</strong> Samples were cooked using four distinct cooking technologies: air fryer (AF-3, Mondial New Pratic, Brazil), electric oven (FE4222BR, Suggar, Brazil), electric grill (BPE01, Britânia, Brazil), and microwave (MDC212B, Menumaster, USA). Four replicates were prepared for each method. Except for the microwave, all appliances were preheated for 10 min, and cooking was carried out at 180 °C until the samples reached an internal temperature of 71 °C, in accordance with the safety guidelines established by the Brazilian Health Regulatory Agency (ANVISA). For oven cooking, the samples were placed on a baking tray with both upper and lower heating elements activated to ensure uniform heat distribution. In the electric oven and the air fryer, samples were flipped every 5 min, whereas on the grill, they were turned every 3 min. Due to the considerably faster heating mechanism of the microwave, samples reached the target temperature without acquiring the typical cooked appearance. Based on preliminary trials, a fixed cooking time of 8 min was established to ensure food safety while maintaining an appropriate texture. Microwave cooking was conducted at 960 W (30% of the device maximum power), and the samples were not flipped during the process. Throughout cooking, internal temperature was continuously monitored using a fiber optic thermometer (TS3, Weidmann Optocon, Germany) inserted into the center of each sample. After cooking, the samples were freeze-dried, ground into a fine powder, and stored at -20 °C until further analysis.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p>	<p>Compound extraction was carried out following a protocol adapted from Rodrigues et al. (2013). Briefly, 0.25 g of the freeze-dried sample was extracted using 1.5 mL of a methanol/water solution (8:2, v/v) acidified with 0.35% formic acid. The mixture was vortexed (AP56, Phoenix Luferco, Brazil) for 3 min, followed by centrifugation (MiniSpin, Eppendorf, Germany) at 10,000 g for 5 min. The resulting extract was collected and stored at -80 °C until further analysis. </p><p>To monitor data quality and ensure analytical consistency, quality control (QC) procedures were implemented in accordance with Broadhurst et al. (2018). A pooled QC sample was generated by mixing equal aliquots (200 μL) from each extract. Blank samples were also included to identify potential sources of contamination.&nbsp;Process blanks&nbsp;were prepared by following the entire extraction procedure using water instead of the sample material, allowing for the detection of background signals from consumables and reagents.&nbsp;Solvent blanks, consisting of the same extraction solvent (methanol:water 8:2, v/v), were used to detect possible contaminants present in the solvent system. All samples were filtered through hydrophilic PTFE syringe filters (0.22 μm) prior to metabolomic data acquisition.</p>	<p>Analyses were performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Nexera X2, Shimadzu, Japan). Samples were injected in randomized order onto an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 μm particle size; Waters, USA).The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The gradient elution program, adapted from Mercali et al. (2025), was as follows: 0–0.5 min, isocratic at 0% B; 0.5–8 min, linear gradient to 100% B; 8–9 min, isocratic at 100% B; 9–10 min, linear return to 0% B; and 10–11 min, isocratic at 0% B. The flow rate was set to 0.5 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 5 μL.</p>	<p>The UHPLC system was interfaced with a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (QTOF-MS) (Impact II, Bruker, USA) via an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in both positive and negative ionization modes to ensure broad metabolite coverage. Mass spectrometer settings were as follows: gas temperature at 200 °C, drying gas flow at 10 L/min, nebulizer pressure at 4.5 bar, and a mass scan range (m/z) of 50-1000. MS2 data were acquired using the same MS settings, employing auto MS/MS scanning mode with a collision energy of 10 eV.</p>	<p>Data obtained from the UHPLC-QTOF-MS analysis were processed using the open-source software MZMine 3.9.0 (Schmid et al., 2023) for peak detection and deconvolution. The software groups mass spectral features based on expected isotope patterns, adducts, and charge states, and aligns them across all samples. The processing workflow included the following steps: MS1 mass detection, chromatogram building, chromatogram deconvolution, isotope grouping, feature alignment, gap filling, isotope pattern filtering and removal of duplicate features. All parameter settings are provided in the Supplementary Material (Tables S2 and S3). Finally, the feature tables were exported for data analysis (Tables S4 and S5 of the Supplementary Material).</p><p>Subsequent data filtering and statistical analysis were conducted in R (version 4.4.1), following the methodology described by Dzakovich et al. (2024). Initially, only features detected in 100% of the quality control (QC) samples and exhibiting a coefficient of variation below 30% in QCs were retained. Features that did not show at least a 10-fold higher intensity in QCs compared to process blanks were also excluded. Missing values were imputed using half of the minimum peak intensity detected in the dataset. Data were then clustered using the&nbsp;notame&nbsp;R package (Klåvus et al., 2020) to collapse ions resulting from the same metabolite. The final feature tables for positive and negative ionization modes are available in Supplementary Tables S6 and S7, respectively. All data were log2-transformed and median-centered prior to statistical analysis. Differential abundance analysis was conducted using unpaired&nbsp;t-tests (p ≤ 0.05), with multiple testing correction applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method (rstatix&nbsp;package). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for data visualization (ggplot2&nbsp;package). Significantly different features (p ≤ 0.05 after FDR multiple testing correction) were visualized using heatmaps with hierarchical clustering (Euclidean distance, Ward’s linkage method).</p>	<p>Metabolite annotation was performed according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative&nbsp;guidelines (MSI) employing different levels of identification confidence (Sumner et al., 2007). For Level 1 identification, metabolites were matched against an in-house library of standards (Phenolic Lab Brazil) based on both retention time and spectral data (MS1 and MS2). &nbsp;Manual inspection was also performed for compounds included in the library.</p><p>For Level 2 annotation, MS2 fragmentation spectra were compared to publicly available spectral libraries, including the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) (Aron et al., 2020). Classical Molecular Networking was performed with the following parameters: precursor ion mass tolerance of 0.02 Da, a minimum of four matched fragment peaks, and a cosine similarity score above 0.7. Additional databases, such as HMDB, MoNA, and the NIST Mass Spectral Search Program, were also used to compare fragmentation patterns of compounds of interest.</p>

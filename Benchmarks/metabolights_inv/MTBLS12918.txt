Study Title	Environmental hazard profile of Gadolinium: Mechanistic insights into its ecotoxicity towards Euglena gracilis from a multi-level investigation
Study Description	<p>As emerging environmental contaminants, rare earth elements (REEs) pose a growing ecotoxicological threat, making a mechanistic understanding of their toxicity in aquatic primary producers like microalgae crucial for accurate ecological risk assessment. This study investigated the multi-level adverse effects of gadolinium (Gd³⁺), a representative REE contaminant, on Euglena gracilis by integrating physiological, ultrastructural, and non-targeted metabolomic approaches. While background environmental concentrations of Gd are low, levels in anthropogenically impacted systems can exceed ~60 mmol/L, providing the context for this toxicological investigation. Gd³⁺ exhibited potent, dose-dependent toxicity, causing a 35.77% inhibition of growth at 80 µmol/L, with a calculated EC₅₀ for growth inhibition of 110.5 µmol/L (95% CI: 95.8 to 127.5 µmol/L), alongside detrimental morphological shifts and severe chloroplast damage. While total photosynthetic pigments and paramylon decreased in the culture, their per-cell levels paradoxically increased, signifying a severe cellular stress response. Non-targeted metabolomics revealed extensive metabolic reprogramming, with lipid metabolism identified as the primary target of Gd³⁺-induced cellular damage, accounting for 65.8% of the differential metabolites. This disruption included the significant upregulation of lysophospholipids, a nearly 400-fold increase in the signaling molecule 12-HETE, and the perturbation of key bioenergetic pathways like oxidative phosphorylation. In conclusion, Gd³⁺ exerts multi-target toxicity in E. gracilis through direct chloroplast damage, drastic reprogramming of lipid metabolism leading to membrane disruption and altered signaling, and consequent disorders in energy metabolism. These findings advance REE ecotoxicology and highlight that the disruption of lipid metabolism is a key toxic mechanism and a potential sensitive biomarker for REE-induced hazardous effects, providing crucial evidence to inform environmental risk assessments and establish water quality benchmarks for these materials.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>In the experimental group, Euglena gracilis was cultured in a gadolinium-supplemented medium with a concentration of 80 μmol/L, while in the control group, the algae were grown in a normal medium. Each treatment was performed in triplicate. On the 8th day, algal cells with an approximate fresh weight of 30 mg were rapidly harvested from each treatment group and the control group, respectively. The harvested cells were immediately quenched in liquid nitrogen to halt their metabolic activity, and were subsequently stored at -80 °C until metabolite extraction was conducted.</p>	<p>First, 1000 μL of extraction solution (methanol:acetonitrile:water, 2:2:1, v/v/v), containing an isotope-labeled internal standard mixture) was added to the sample. Subsequently, the sample was ground at 35 Hz for 4 min and then sonicated for 5 min in an ice-water bath. After that, this step (grinding followed by sonication) was repeated 3x times, and the sample was further sonicated in an ice-water bath for 10 min. The sample was then allowed to stand at -40 °C for 1 h. Following this, the sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (with a relative centrifugal force (RCF) of 13,800 x g and a radius of 8.6 cm) at 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was collected into a sample vial for instrumental analysis. Finally, equal volumes of supernatant were separately taken from all samples, mixed to prepare quality control (QC) samples, and these QC samples were also subjected to instrumental analysis.</p>	<p>For this project, a Vanquish ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed, and a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 chromatographic column (2.1 mm x 50 mm, 2.6 μm) was used for the chromatographic separation of target compounds. The mobile phase A for liquid chromatography was an aqueous phase containing 0.01% acetic acid, while mobile phase B was a mixture of isopropanol and acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). The temperature of the autosampler tray was maintained at 4 °C and the injection volume was 2 μL.</p>	<p>The Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer is capable of acquiring full-scan MS (MS¹) and tandem MS (MS²) data under the control of the control software (Xcalibur, Version: 4.4, Thermo). The detailed parameters are as follows: Sheath gas flow rate: 50 Arb; Aux gas flow rate: 15 Arb; Capillary temperature: 320 °C; Full MS resolution: 60,000; MS/MS resolution: 15,000; Collision energy: Stepped Normalized Collision Energy (SNCE) of 20/30/40; Spray Voltage: 3.8 kV (positive ion mode) and -3.4 kV (negative ion mode).</p>	<p>After the raw data was converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard software, it underwent processing steps including peak detection, peak extraction, peak alignment and peak integration using a self-developed R package (with XCMS as the core). Subsequently, metabolite annotation was performed by matching the processed data against a self-constructed tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) database in BiotreeDB (Version 2.1), and the cutoff value for the algorithm scoring was set to 0.3.</p>	<p>The data processing and annotation pipeline is as follows: raw data is first converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard. Subsequently, a self-developed R package (with XCMS as the core) is used to perform peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration. Then, metabolite annotation is conducted by matching the processed data against the self-constructed tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) database BiotreeDB (V2.1), with a cutoff value of 0.3 for algorithm scoring. In the subsequent step, differentially expressed metabolites are screened by combining univariate (t-test) and multivariate (Principal Component Analysis [PCA], Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures-Discriminant Analysis [OPLS-DA]) statistical methods.The reference databases include the self-constructed BiotreeDB (V2.1) MS/MS database, as well as public databases such as the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, for metabolite information indexing), KEGG COMPOUND (for pathway annotation), and PubChem (for substance ID mapping).</p>

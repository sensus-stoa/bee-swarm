Study Title	Soil legacy effects in Soybean Intercropping Enhance Tobacco Resistance to Fusarium Root Rot
Study Description	<p>Soil-borne diseases, with their high incidence and frequency in monoculture systems, pose a major challenge in contemporary agricultural production. Intercropping can promote beneficial soil legacy effects, thereby effectively mitigating the occurrence and damage of soil-borne diseases. In this study, we employed an integrated approach combining 16S rRNA sequencing, ITS amplicon sequencing, and untargeted metabolomics to systematically compare the differences in soil microbial community structure and metabolite profiles between soybean-tobacco intercropping and tobacco monoculture systems. Furthermore, we elucidated the mechanisms through which these differences influence the incidence of tobacco root rot. The results showed that intercropping significantly enhanced the survival rate of tobacco plants under Fusarium.spp infection (P &lt; 0.01). Furthermore, intercropping markedly increased soil microbial community diversity and significantly reduced the relative abundance of Fusarium (by 53.17%). Additionally, intercropping disrupted the cooperative relationships between Fusarium and other microbial taxa, leading to reduced connectivity within the interaction network and a notable decline in its ecological competitive advantage. Metabolomic analysis revealed that intercropping promoted the accumulation of antimicrobial metabolites such as indole, and indole content was significantly negatively correlated with Fusarium abundance (P &lt; 0.05). Further integrated microbiome-metabolome analysis demonstrated that intercropping fostered a more complex microbial-metabolite interaction network, which helped suppress the recolonization of pathogenic Fusarium. In conclusion, this study provides a theoretical basis for leveraging intercropping systems to modulate the rhizosphere micro-environment and control soil-borne diseases, offering new insights for developing sustainable green control strategies.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>This study utilized tobacco cultivar Yunyan 87 and soybean cultivar Shandou 125 as experimental materials. Tobacco seedlings were transplanted on May 1, and soybeans were sown on June 10. When the co-growth period reached 90 days (at which point both tobacco and soybeans had reached full harvest maturity), rhizosphere soil samples of tobacco and soybeans were collected. Each treatment consisted of six soil samples, with two samples collected per replicate and three random sampling points selected for each sample.</p><p><br></p><p>A sterile soil auger with a diameter of 4 cm was used to collect soil samples from the 10–20 cm depth layer. After removing impurities (including roots, plant and animal residues, and stones), the three subsamples from each quadrat were thoroughly mixed, promptly transferred into sterile zip-lock bags, and temporarily stored in a 4°C cooling box during transportation. Finally, the composite samples were divided into two aliquots for subsequent use.</p>	<p>Precisely weigh 100 mg of sample into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, then accurately add 600 µL of methanol solution containing 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine (4 ppm) and vortex for 30 s.</p><p><br></p><p>Add steel beads and place the tube in a tissue grinder, grinding at 55 Hz for 90 s.</p><p><br></p><p>Sonicate the mixture at room temperature for 30 min, then place it on ice for 30 min.</p><p><br></p><p>Centrifuge at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min. Collect the supernatant and filter it through a 0.22 μm membrane. Transfer the filtrate to a detection vial for LC-MS analysis.</p><p><br></p>	<p>An ultra-performance liquid chromatography system (Thermo Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was employed with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL.</p><p><br></p><p>For positive ion mode, the mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B2) and 0.1% formic acid in water (A2). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–1 min, 10% B2; 1–5 min, 10%–98% B2; 5–6.5 min, 98% B2; 6.5–6.6 min, 98%–10% B2; 6.6–8 min, 10% B2.</p><p><br></p><p>For negative ion mode, the mobile phase included acetonitrile (B3) and 5 mM ammonium formate in water (A3). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–1 min, 10% B3; 1–5 min, 10%–98% B3; 5–6.5 min, 98% B3; 6.5–6.6 min, 98%–10% B3; 6.6–8 min, 10% B3.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection was performed using a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes. The spray voltage was set at 3.50 kV for positive ion mode and –2.50 kV for negative ion mode. Sheath gas and auxiliary gas were set at 40 arb and 10 arb, respectively. The capillary temperature was maintained at 325 °C. Full MS¹ scanning was conducted at a resolution of 60,000 over the mass range of m/z 100–1000. Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) was applied for MS² fragmentation with a collision energy of 30%. MS² spectra were acquired at a resolution of 15,000, with the top 4 most intense ions selected for fragmentation. Dynamic exclusion was enabled to avoid unnecessary MS/MS acquisition.</p>	<p>The raw mass spectrometry data files were converted to mzXML format using the MSConvert tool within the ProteoWizard software package (v3.0.8789).</p>	<p>Peak detection, peak filtering, and peak alignment were performed using the R XCMS package with the following parameter settings: bw = 2, ppm = 15, peakwidth = c(5, 30), mzwid = 0.015, mzdiff = 0.01, method = 'centWave'. A quantitative feature table was generated after these processing steps. Systematic errors were corrected using support vector regression (SVR) based on quality control (QC) samples. Subsequently, only features with a coefficient of variation (CV) less than 30% in the QC samples were retained for further analysis.</p>

Study Title	Microplastics Induce Damage to Cattle and Potential Health Risks Through Microbial Mediation: A Rumen Microbiota-Gut-Liver Axis Perspective_Rumen Fluid
Study Description	<p>Microplastics pollution is prevalent in livestock environments, especially in ruminant feed. Exposure to microplastics can damage the gastrointestinal barrier, alter microbiota, and lead to organ damage. Despite its impact on cattle farming and potential human health risks, the effects of microplastics on cattle remain unexplored. This study investigates microplastic-induced toxicity through the rumen microbiota-gut-liver axis in beef cattle. We found that Microplastic exposure reduced body weight gain and liver development, induced pathological changes in the liver, increased lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels in both blood and muscle, and triggered systemic inflammation, particularly affecting the liver. Alterations in rumen microbiota and metabolites were associated with liver inflammation. Furthermore, microplastics damaged the rumen, jejunum, and colon barriers, resulting in microbial dysbiosis, metabolic disorders, and activation of LPS synthesis pathways. Metabolomics of rumen fluid identified key metabolites-including 4-Fluoro-3-phenoxybenzoic acid and Isovalerylglutamic acid, which correlated with inflammatory markers (LPS, TNF-α, and IL-6). Transcriptomic analysis further corroborated inflammation in blood and liver, revealing activation of NF-κB pathway primarily via Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), which inhibited hepatocyte proliferation and promoted apoptosis.</p><p>Collectively, this study reveals that microplastics impair cattle liver function via the rumen microbiota-gut-liver axis. Microplastics disrupt the rumen microbiota, activate the LPS synthesis, and damage the intestinal barrier, allowing LPS to enter the bloodstream and subsequent liver damage. Additionally, LPS will also remain in the muscles, which to some extent exacerbates the risk of LPS in meat products to human health. Reducing the use of plastic products in animal husbandry and modulation of the rumen microbiota may provide a potential strategy to alleviate microplastic-induced hepatotoxicity.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>One day prior to the experiment, the body size and weight of each calf were recorded. On day 40, blood sample (7mL) was collected via jugular venipuncture from each calf prior to morning feeding. A 2 mL aliquot was transferred into a blood collection tube, gently inverted to mix, and immediately snap-frozen on dry ice in an insulated container for hematological analysis. The remining 5 mL blood sample was centrifuged (3000 r/min) for 15 min at 4°C, and the separated plasma was stored at −80°C for subsequent testing. On day 41, calves were fasted for 12 h prior to euthanasia via jugular vein exsanguination.&nbsp;</p>	<p>Transfer 50 μL of the sample into a 2 mL centrifuge tube.</p><p>Add 200 μL of pre-cooled methanol:acetonitrile&nbsp;(1:1, v/v), and vortex for 30 s.</p><p>Place in a -20°C freezer for 30 min.</p><p>Centrifuge at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min, take 200 μL of the supernatant, and vacuum concentrate until completely dry.</p><p>Add 150 μL of 50% methanol (containing 5 ppm 2-chlorophenylalanine) for reconstitution, and vortex for 30 s.</p><p>Centrifuge at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min, take the supernatant and pass it through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. Transfer the filtrate into a detection vial.</p><p>Combine 10-20 μL of each sample filtrate into one QC sample. For fewer than 5 samples, no QC is required. The QC sample is used to evaluate instrument stability and data reliability.</p>	<p>Using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 Å, 1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min, column temperature of 40°C, autosampler temperature of 8°C, and an injection volume of 2 μL.</p><p>Mobile phases for positive and negative modes: Mobile phase A is 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B is acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid).</p>	<p>The Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer, controlled by Xcalibur software (Version: 4.7, Thermo), was used to perform DDA mass spectrometry data acquisition in positive and negative ion modes, respectively. The HESI source was employed with the following parameters: spray voltage 3.5 kV/-3.0 kV, sheath gas 40 arb, auxiliary gas 10 arb, capillary temperature 320°C, auxiliary gas temperature 300°C, primary resolution 60,000, scan range 70-1000 m/z, AGC Target Standard, Max IT 100 ms. The top 4 most responsive ions were selected for secondary fragmentation with a dynamic exclusion time of 4 s. The secondary resolution was set to 15,000, HCD collision energy 30%, AGC Target Standard, and Max IT Auto.</p><p>All formal samples and QC samples were analyzed using the aforementioned chromatography and mass spectrometry methods. Prior to formal sample injection, 2-4 QC samples were injected to equilibrate the system. During the injection process, one QC sample was injected every 5-10 samples. For fewer than 5 samples, no QC was performed. These measures were implemented for subsequent data evaluation and quality control.</p>	<p>The acquired .raw data files were imported into MS-DIAL software (version 4.9.221218) for peak extraction, alignment, filtering, and metabolite identification. Peaks not detected in more than 50% of the QC samples were filtered out. Undetected peaks were filled using the software's Gap filling algorithm, followed by normalization.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed using the PSNGM (PerSonalbio Next-Generation Metabolomics Database), which includes an in-house standard library, the mzCloud database (https://www.mzcloud.org/), LIPID MAPS (https://www.lipidmaps.org/), HMDB (https://hmdb.ca/), MoNA (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/), NIST_2020_MSMS, and an AI-predicted MSMS spectral library. The main database search parameters were as follows: MS1 tolerance for identification 0.01, MS2 tolerance for identification 0.05, smoothing level 3, minimum peak height 10000, minimum peak width 5, mass slice width 0.05, and identification score cut off 70.</p>

Study Title	Association Study on the Regulation of Muscle Development-Related Genes, Proteins, and Metabolites in Different Parts by Gastrointestinal Flora During Various Developmental Stages of Liaoning Cashmere Goats
Study Description	<p> Introduction: Muscle growth and development in Liaoning cashmere goats (a dual-purpose breed for cashmere and meat) and its regulation by intestinal flora are key to improving meat production, yet relevant molecular mechanisms remain unclear.</p><p> Objectives: To reveal the molecular mechanism of muscle growth and development in Liaoning cashmere goats and the regulatory role of intestinal flora, thereby improving their meat production performance.</p><p> Methods: Muscle tissues (intercostal, hindlimb biceps femoris, longissimus dorsi) and digestive contents (rumen, small intestine, cecum, feces) from goats at 6, 12, 18 months were collected. Transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, 16S rRNA sequencing, and multi-omics integration analysis were used.</p><p> Results: HBFM exhibited optimal development at 12 months of age, which was influenced by g_Lachnospiraceae_NK3A20_group in the small intestine. The HOXC10 gene drove myocyte proliferation, while the MYL3 gene and its encoded protein synergistically participated in muscle physiological changes, involving the cAMP signaling pathway. LDM development was more active at 12 months, regulated by g_Christensenellaceae_R-7_group in the rumen and cecum, leading to significant expression of the MEF2A gene and MYH6 protein. Additionally, the COL1A1/COL1A2 genes participated in intramuscular fat deposition through purine metabolism. ICM showed vigorous early proliferation at 6 months, regulated by g_Aeriscardovia in the small intestine, which promoted high expression of Actin. L-malic acid and L-serine affected muscle growth and water-holding capacity in this region. At 18 months, the GDI protein influenced fat deposition in ICM through the cofactor biosynthesis pathway. Multi-omics integration analysis revealed that microorganisms such as g_norank_f_UCG-011 were significantly associated with numerous metabolites, and genes like FKBP5 synergistically regulated muscle development with proteins such as Actin.</p><p><em>Conclusion:</em> The development of different muscle parts in Liaoning cashmere goats is coordinately regulated by specific genes, proteins, metabolites, and intestinal flora, providing key targets for targeted breeding to improve meat production performance.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>In 2023, we collected muscle tissues from three anatomical sites of LCGs at different ages, with 6 biological replicates per group. The collected muscles were cut into appropriately sized samples. The specific group designations were as follows: intercostal muscle at 6 months of age (ICM-6), hindlimb biceps femoris muscle at 6 months (HBFM-6), and longissimus dorsi muscle at 6 months (LDM-6); intercostal muscle at 12 months (ICM-12), hindlimb biceps femoris muscle at 12 months (HBFM-12), and longissimus dorsi muscle at 12 months (LDM-12); intercostal muscle at 18 months (ICM-18), hindlimb biceps femoris muscle at 18 months (HBFM-18), and longissimus dorsi muscle at 18 months (LDM-18). </p><p>The collected muscle samples were placed in lyophilization tubes and immediately stored on dry ice for a short term (no more than 12 hours). After transportation, they were stored in a -80°C refrigerator for long-term preservation, with a maximum storage period of 2 months.</p>	<p>Sample Extraction and Preparation Methods</p><p>Before LC-MS analysis, the 54 goat muscle tissue samples underwent standardized extraction and preparation. First, 50±5mg of each sample was accurately weighed into a 2mL centrifuge tube with a 6mm-diameter grinding bead, followed by the addition of 400μL extraction solution (methanol:water = 4:1, v:v) containing four internal standards (e.g., L-2-chlorophenylalanine at 0.02mg/mL). The samples were then ground for 6 minutes at -10℃ and 50Hz using a frozen tissue grinder, and subjected to low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 minutes at 5℃ and 40KHz. After standing at -20℃ for 30 minutes, the samples were centrifuged at 13000g and 4℃ for 15 minutes, and the supernatant was transferred to an injection vial for subsequent analysis .</p><p>Control Samples for the Assay</p><p>Quality control (QC) samples were prepared as the main control for the assay. Specifically, 20μL of supernatant was taken from each sample and mixed to form QC samples with the same volume as the experimental samples. These QC samples were processed and detected using the identical method as the experimental samples, and one QC sample was inserted every 5-15 analytical samples during instrument analysis to monitor the stability of the entire detection process . No other control samples such as solvent blanks or standards were mentioned in the report; the experiment relied on QC samples and internal standards to ensure data reliability.</p>	<p>The chromatography part of the experiment used the UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system (manufacturer: Thermo Scientific) as the LC-MS instrument platform, and the chromatographic column was ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (specifications: 100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 µm; manufacturer: Waters, Milford, USA) . The mobile phase consisted of Phase A (95% water + 5% acetonitrile, containing 0.1% formic acid) and Phase B (47.5% acetonitrile + 47.5% isopropanol + 5% water, containing 0.1% formic acid) . For the elution gradient: under positive ion mode, the flow rate was consistently 0.4 mL/min—at 0 min, the proportion was 100% Phase A and 0% Phase B; at 3 min, it was 80% Phase A and 20% Phase B; at 5 min and 6.3 min, it was 0% Phase A and 100% Phase B; at 6.4 min and 8 min, it returned to 100% Phase A and 0% Phase B ; under negative ion mode, the flow rate was also 0.4 mL/min, with Phase A gradually decreasing from 100% to 0% and then reverting to 100%. Other chromatographic settings included an injection volume of 3 μL and a column temperature of 40℃.</p>	<p>he mass spectrometry analysis used the&nbsp;Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer&nbsp;(manufacturer: Thermo Scientific). The ion source adopted electrospray ionization (ESI), and both positive and negative ion scanning modes were used for signal collection. The m/z scanning range was set to 70-1050. Specific parameters included: a sheath gas flow rate of 50 arb, a heater temperature of 425℃, a capillary temperature of 325℃, a spray voltage of 3500 V for positive ion mode and -3500 V for negative ion mode, an S-Lens RF Level of 50, normalized collision energies of 20, 40, and 60 eV, a resolution of 60000 for Full MS, and a resolution of 7500 for MS 2.</p>	<p>The raw data from the LC-MS analysis was first imported into the metabolomics software Progenesis QI (manufactured by Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for data transformation. The software executed a series of processing steps including baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment to generate a data matrix containing key information such as retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and peak intensity. Subsequently, the software was used for characteristic peak database searching and metabolite identification: the MS and MS/MS data were matched with metabolomic databases (including mainstream public databases such as http://www.hmdb.ca/ and https://metlin.scripps.edu/, as well as self-built databases), with the MS mass error set to less than 10 ppm, and metabolites were identified based on the secondary mass spectrometry matching score. This pipeline transformed the original raw detection data into a structured data matrix that could be used for subsequent bioinformatics analysis.</p>	<p>The metabolite identification process in this experiment followed a standardized pipeline and relied on professional software and multiple databases. First, the raw LC-MS data was imported into the metabolomics software Progenesis QI(manufactured by Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for preprocessing, including baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment, to obtain a data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and peak intensity. Then, the software was used for characteristic peak database searching and metabolite annotation: MS and MS/MS mass spectrometry information of the features were matched with reference databases, with the MS mass error set to less than 10 ppm, and metabolites were identified based on the secondary mass spectrometry matching score. The reference databases used included mainstream public databases such as HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/) and Metlin(https://metlin.scripps.edu/), as well as a self-built database. The final identified metabolites were summarized in the data matrix, which also included information such as metabolite names, corresponding database IDs (e.g., HMDB numbers, Lipidmaps numbers, KEGG Compound IDs), molecular formulas, and mass errors, providing a basis for subsequent analysis.</p>

Study Title	Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids modulate gut microbiota-derived 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid to alleviate type 1 diabetes mellitus
Study Description	<p>Background</p><p>Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are known to protect against type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), but how they act through the gut-islet axis is not fully understood. This study explored how Omega-3 PUFA-influenced gut microbiota and their metabolites help protect pancreatic islets in T1DM. The objective of this study was to determine whether gut microbiota and its metabolites sever as mediators of the protective effect of Omega-3 PUFAs and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.</p><p>Methods</p><p>We used fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) to test the effects of Omega-3 PUFA-derived gut microbiota in NOD mice. Immune cell changes in the islets were analyzed using transcriptomics, flow cytometry, and immunohistochemistry. Metabolomics identified key metabolites in serum related to gut microbiota changes. Co-culture experiments examined the role of specific metabolites in macrophage polarization and β-cell function.</p><p>Results</p><p>Omega-3 PUFA-treated and FMT mice showed reduced islet inflammation and an increased abundance of the Eubacterium coprostanoligenes group. Enhanced M2 macrophage polarization was observed in the islet microenvironment of FMT mice. Among gut microbiota metabolites, 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid (18β-GA) was strongly linked to E. coprostanoligenes and stood out as a key molecule. In co-culture experiments, 18β-GA shifted macrophages to an M2 phenotype, which boosted insulin production and secretion in β-cells.</p><p>Conclusions</p><p>Omega-3 PUFA-derived gut microbiota and the metabolite 18β-GA play a key role in protecting pancreatic islets in T1DM by modulating macrophage polarization and improving β-cell function. These findings suggest new ways to use gut microbiota for T1DM treatment.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Fecal samples were collected from donor mice fed with an omega-3 PUFA-enriched diet or control diet. After broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment, recipient NOD mice underwent fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) with freshly prepared fecal slurry resuspended in anaerobic sterile PBS. Serum samples were collected after FMT. All samples were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until metabolomic analysis.</p>	<p>Briefly, samples were stored at -80 °C and then thawed on ice, before vortexed for 10 seconds. A 50 μL sample aliquot was combined with 300 μL of an extraction solution (ACN:Methanol, 1:4, V/V) containing internal standards. The mixture was vortexed for 3 mins and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 mins at 4 °C. A 200 μL supernatant was collected and kept at -20 °C for 30 mins before being centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 3 mins at 4 °C. Finally, 180 μL of the clarified supernatant was transferred for LC-MS analysis.</p>	<p>Sample extracts were analyzed using an LC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC: ExionLC AD; MS: QTRAP System). Analytical conditions: Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm), 40 °C, 0.4 mL/min flow rate, and 2 μL injection. The solvent system was water (0.1% formic acid) and acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid) with a gradient: 95:5 V/V at 0 min, 10:90 V/V at 11.0 min, 10:90 V/V at 12.0 min, 95:5 V/V at 12.1 min, and 95:5 V/V at 14.0 min.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry was conducted on a QTRAP LC-MS/MS system with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, using both positive and negative ion modes. Key parameters: source temperature 500 °C, ion spray voltage 5500 V (positive) and -4500 V (negative), gas pressures at 55 psi (GSI), 60 psi (GSII), and 25 psi (CUR). Tuning and calibration used polypropylene glycol solutions, and MRM transitions were monitored for each metabolite.</p>	<p>Missing values were first imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm. Subsequently, the coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated for each metabolite in the QC samples, and only those with a CV value less than 0.3 were retained for downstream analysis. Mass spectrometry data processing was performed using Analyst software (version 1.6.3).</p><p>The figure below displays the Total Ion Chromatogram (TIC) of pooled quality control (QC) samples, which represents the summed intensity of all detected ions at each time point over the chromatographic run. Also shown is a Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) multi-peak plot (i.e., extracted ion chromatogram, XIC), where the x-axis indicates the retention time (RT) of each detected metabolite, and the y-axis corresponds to ion signal intensity measured in counts per second (cps).</p>	<p>Metabolite identification and quantification of the samples were performed based on a local metabolite database. The multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) chromatograms display all detectable metabolites within the samples, with each chromatographic peak represented by a different color corresponding to an individual metabolite. Characteristic ions of each metabolite were selected by the triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, and the ion intensities (counts per second, CPS) of these characteristic ions were recorded in the detector. The acquired raw files were processed using MultiQuant software, where chromatographic peaks were integrated and manually corrected as necessary. The peak area of each chromatographic peak represented the relative abundance of the corresponding metabolite, and the integrated peak area data for all metabolites were subsequently exported and saved.</p><p>To enable comparison of metabolite abundance across different samples, chromatographic peaks of each metabolite were aligned and corrected according to retention time and peak shape information, thereby ensuring the accuracy of qualitative and quantitative analysis. The figure below illustrates an example of quantification and alignment for a randomly selected metabolite across different samples, where the x-axis represents the retention time (min) and the y-axis represents the ion signal intensity (cps).</p>

Study Title	Deciphering the Anti-Biofilm Mechanism of Anthraquinone-2-Carboxylic Acid Against MRSA by Comprehensive Multi-Omics Analysis
Study Description	<p>Background: Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) biofilms exhibit high levels of drug resistance and cause nosocomial infections in humans, thus posing a sustainable global health threat. This underscores the critical need to develop novel antibiofilm therapeutics with high efficacy and well-characterized mechanisms. In this study, we investigated the MRSA biofilm eradication mechanism of anthraquinone-2-carboxylic acid (ACA), a potent natural antibiofilm agent.&nbsp;</p><p>Methods: The molecular mechanism underpinning the destruction of MRSA biofilm by ACA was systematically explored using integrated proteomic and metabolomic analysis. TMT-based proteomics quantified changes in protein expression, while complementary metabolomic data provided insight into associated pathway alterations. Transcript levels of key differentially expressed genes identified through omics analysis were validated by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), with intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation measured using the DCFH-DA method. The selected biofilm-associated biomarkers were rigorously validated using targeted parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) mass spectrometry.&nbsp;</p><p>Conclusions: This study systematically elucidates the molecular mechanisms underlying the ACA-mediated disruption of MRSA biofilms, thereby establishing a robust theoretical framework for the rational design of ACA-based antibiofilm therapeutics. The identified pathways may provide novel targets for developing next-generation antibiofilm agents and pharmacodynamic biomarkers for MRSA biofilm treatment monitoring.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The MRSA strain was cultivated according to the previously described experimental method [16]. MRSA strains were initially cultured on tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates at 37 °C. Single colonies were then inoculated into Luria-Bertani (LB) liquid medium supplemented with 0.5% (w/v) glucose and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm). The resulting bacterial suspension was adjusted to an optical density corresponding to 1.0 × 106 colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL) using sterile 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For biofilm formation, 1 mL aliquots of the standardized bacterial suspension were transferred to individual wells of a sterile 24-well plate. The plate was incubated statically at 37°C in the dark for 24 hours to allow development of mature biofilms. Following incubation, non-adherent cells were removed by gentle washing with 1 × PBS prior to further experimentation. Fresh LB broth containing either the test compound or DMSO (control) was then added, and the culture was incubated under the same conditions for an additional 8 h.After washing the treated biofilm three times, each sample was resuspended in PBS buffer (pH 7.2-7.4, Beijing Labgic Technology Co., Ltd, China) and transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes for storage at - 80 °C.</p>	<p>After washing the treated biofilm three times, each sample was resuspended in PBS buffer (pH 7.2-7.4, Beijing Labgic Technology Co., Ltd, China) and transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes for storage at - 80 °C. Thawed aliquots were mixed with 200 μL of ice-cold water and 800 μL of pre-chilled methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) for sample processing. The mixtures were then: (1) sonicated in an ice bath for 30 min, (2) incubated at - 20 °C for 1 h to precipitate proteins, and (3) centrifuged at 16,000 × g (4 °C) for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis.</p><p>The remaining precipitate was resuspended in 200 μL of SDT buffer, and protein concentration was quantified using the BCA assay. Bacterial counts across samples were normalized based on protein content. For mass spectrometry analysis, equal volumes of supernatant (normalized by bacterial count) were dried using a high-speed vacuum concentrator. Prior to LC-MS analysis, the dried samples were reconstituted in 50 μL of methanol-water (1:1, v/v), centrifuged again at 16,000 × g (4 °C) for 20 min, and the final supernatant was injected for analysis</p>	<p>The samples were analyzed using a SHIMADZU Nexera LC-30 UHPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The chromatographic conditions were as follows: injection volume of 6 μL, column temperature maintained at 40 °C, and flow rate set to 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B). The gradient elution program was: 0 - 2 min, 0% B; 2 - 6 min, 0 - 48% B (linear); 6 - 10 min, 48 - 100% B (linear); 10 - 12 min, 100% B (isocratic); 12 - 12.1 min, 100 - 0% B (linear); and 12.1 - 15 min, 0% B (isocratic).</p>	<p>Analysis was performed using a Q ExactiveTM Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source operating in positive and negative ionization modes. The ionization parameters were as below: spray voltage of 3.8 kV (positive) and 3.2 kV (negative); capillary temperature at 320°C; sheath gas flow rate of 30 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas flow rate of 5 arbitrary units; probe heater temperature of 350 °C; and S-Lens RF level at 50. Full-scan MS data were acquired over a mass range of 70 - 1,050 m/z for 15 min with the following parameters: resolution of 70,000 at m/z 200, AGC target of 3 × 106, and maximum ion injection time of 100 ms. For data-dependent MS/MS acquisition, the top 10 most intense precursor ions were selected from each full scan with an isolation window of 2 m/z. Fragmentation was performed using HCD with normalized collision energies of 20, 30, and 40 eV. MS/MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 17,500 at m/z 200 with an AGC target of 1×105 and maximum ion injection time of 50 ms.</p>	<p>Raw data were processed using MSDIAL software for peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction</p>	<p>&nbsp;Metabolite identification was performed by matching precise exact mass (mass tolerance &lt; 20 ppm) and secondary mass spectra (mass tolerance &lt; 0.02 Da) against public databases (HMDB, MassBank) and BayPol in-house metabolite standard library . In the extracted-ion features, only the variables having more than 50% of the nonzero measurement values in at least one group were kept.</p>

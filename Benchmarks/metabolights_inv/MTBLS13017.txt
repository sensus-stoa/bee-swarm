Study Title	Limosilactobacillus reuteri in Promoting Growth Performance and Regulating Lipid Metabolism in Zuili Black Pigs
Study Description	<p>Abstract</p><p>Background</p><p>The gut microbiota is a key regulator of host lipid metabolism. In indigenous pigs, variation in growth performance and intramuscular fat (IMF) deposition has been linked to microbiota-host interactions, yet the specific taxa and metabolites that govern host lipid handling remain incompletely defined. We compared the gut microbiome, metabolome and transcriptome of high-body-weight (HBW) versus low-body-weight (LBW) Zuili Black Pigs to identify differentially abundant taxa and evaluate their roles and mechanisms in lipid regulation and growth promotion.</p><p>Results</p><p>Comparative profiling of the gut microbiome, metabolome, and transcriptome clearly distinguished HBW and LBW Zuili Black Pigs. The two groups differed in gut microbial composition; notably, Limosilactobacillus reuteri was enriched in HBW pigs and correlated positively with growth performance and IMF. Lipid-related metabolites dominated the differential features, and growth-associated gene co-expression modules were enriched for lipid-metabolic pathways. Concordantly, HBW pigs showed elevated hepatic expression of lipid uptake/transport genes (Mtp, Fatp4, Cd36, Abca1) and increased intestinal expression of Fatp4, Abca1, and Npc1, consistent with enhanced lipid handling and higher IMF. In vivo, L. reuteri supplementation in mice increased body weight gain and average daily gain, improved small-intestinal morphology (greater villus height and villus-to-crypt ratio), and raised colonic phenylacetic acid (PAA). Mechanistically, L. reuteri produced PAA when provided phenylalanine, and HBW pigs exhibited higher colonic PAA levels. Taken together, these findings support an L. reuteri-phenylalanine-PAA axis that engages host lipid-metabolic programs to promote lipid absorption, deposition, and growth.&nbsp;</p><p>Conclusions</p><p>Our findings demonstrate that L. reuteri converts phenylalanine to PAA, thereby activating host lipid-metabolic pathways to promote lipid absorption and deposition, ultimately contributing to improved growth performance. These results nominate the L. reuteri-PAA axis as a tractable microbiome target for enhancing growth traits in indigenous pig populations, with potential to inform probiotic and breeding strategies aimed at sustainable production and meat-quality improvement.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Forty healthy Zuili Black pigs (80 days old) were enrolled in a feeding trial. At 160 days of age, based on body weight, ten HBW (108.20 ± 0.93 kg) and ten LBW ( 96.14 ± 0.66 kg) were selected from the cohort. All pigs were managed under similar husbandry conditions, with free access to feed and water during the trial period. The experimental pigs were fasted for 12 hours prior to slaughter and weighed thereafter. Following electrical stunning and exsanguination, blood samples were collected from the anterior vena cava into procoagulant tubes. After standing at room temperature for 30 minutes, the samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 minutes. Ileal and colonic contents were collected into cryovials, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer at -80 °C.</p>	<p>A total of 100 mg of colonic content was accurately weighed and mixed with 1 mL of prechilled 50% methanol solution. The mixture was vortexed for 1 minute, incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes, and then stored overnight at -20 °C. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 4,000 g for 20 minutes, and the supernatants were transferred into a new 96-well plate. Additionally, 10 μL of each extract was pooled to create a quality control (QC) sample, which was used to monitor the stability and reproducibility of the analytical process.</p>	<p>Liquid chromatography separation was performed on an UltiMate 3000 UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) using an ACQUITY UPLC T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA) for reversed-phase separation. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (5 mM ammonium acetate and 5 mM acetic acid in water) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The flow rate was set to 0.3 mL/min with the following gradient elution program:0-0.8 min, 2% B; 0.8-2.8 min, 2%-70% B; 2.8-5.6 min, 70%-90% B; 5.6-6.4 min, 90%-100% B; 6.4-8.0 min, 100% B; 8.0-8.1 min, 100%-2% B; 8.1-10 min, 2% B.</p>	<p>LC–MS/MS acquisition</p><p><br></p><p>Metabolites eluting from the column were analyzed on a high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer (TripleTOF 6600, SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). The Q-TOF was operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes with the following settings: curtain gas 30 psi; ion source gases 1 and 2, 60 psi each; interface heater temperature 500 °C. The ion spray floating voltage was 5,000 V in positive mode and 4,500 V in negative mode. Data were acquired in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode. The TOF MS scan range was m/z 60–1,200. A 150 ms survey scan was followed by up to 12 product-ion scans when precursor ions with intensity &gt;100 counts/s and charge state 1+ were detected. Dynamic exclusion was set to 4 s. To ensure data quality, mass calibration was performed after every 20 samples, and a pooled quality-control (QC) sample was injected after every 10 samples to monitor LC–MS stability throughout the run.</p><p><br></p><p>2.1.4 Data analysis</p><p><br></p><p>Raw data were processed in XCMS for peak detection, grouping, retention-time correction, secondary grouping/alignment, and isotope/adduct annotation. Metabolite identification was performed with metaX, and identified features were annotated against the HMDB and KEGG databases. Subsequent analyses included metabolite quantification, sample correlation analysis, and differential analysis. For differential metabolites, KEGG pathway enrichment, interaction-network analysis, and metabolite correlation analysis were conducted. Statistical analyses were carried out in R (version 4.0).</p>	<p>Using ProteoWizard msconvert to convert .wiff (along with .wiff.scan) to .mzML. The latest version also supports WIFF2 (SCIEX OS).</p><p>sciex.com</p><p>Common settings: write index, zlib compression, and prioritize peakPicking (centroiding) at the top of the filter list. Typically, centroiding is applied only to MS1 (msLevel=1-).</p><p>sciex.com</p><p>If your acquisition involves polarity switching, it is recommended to export two separate files:</p><p>Retain only positive mode: --filter 'polarity positive'</p><p>Retain only negative mode: --filter 'polarity negative'</p><p>(This will give you clear POS/NEG sets of .mzML, matching MW's 'one Analysis per mode.')</p>	<p>Metabolites eluted from the column were analyzed using a high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer, the TripleTOF 6600 system (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). Raw MS data were processed using XCMS software for peak detection, grouping, retention time correction, secondary peak grouping, and annotation of isotopes and adducts. MetaX software was used for metabolite identification and functional annotation based on common databases such as KEGG. Subsequently, quantitative analysis, sample correlation analysis, and differential metabolite analysis were conducted. Functional analyses of differential metabolites included KEGG enrichment analysis, interaction network construction, and metabolite correlation analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0).</p>

Study Title	WT 2.0: Unveiling the “dark matter” in the metabolome using all-ion stepwise fragmentation acquisition mode (ASFAM) and deep learning-based feature extraction
Study Description	<p> Current metabolomics methods often miss low-abundance compounds and yield incomplete or ambiguous MS2 spectra, resulting in the presence of “dark matter” within the metabolome. Here, we introduce WT 2.0, which employs an all-ion stepwise fragmentation acquisition mode (ASFAM) to acquire comprehensive MS1 data, providing stepping 1 m/z windows for all precursor ions, and thereby capturing (theoretically) all deconvolution-free high-resolution MS2 data, as well as MS2 for low-abundance precursors that evade MS1 scans. A tailored algorithm extracts these low-response features, and a deep learning model predicts their high-resolution precursor ion m/z values. A Siamese network subsequently aligns features across samples. When applied to rice leaf LC-MS profiles, WT 2.0 increases detected features (with MS2) by 150% versus data-dependent acquisition (DDA) and 70% versus data-independent acquisition (DIA), and doubles high-confidence identifications compared to DDA while achieving a 360% increase over DIA. Under various stress conditions in rice leaves, WT 2.0 identifies 3-formylindole, β-carboline, and feruloyl putrescine as novel stress-responsive metabolites missed by conventional methods. In plasma LC-MS profiles, WT 2.0 reveals novel metabolic alterations in stage I lung cancer: increased free bile acids (cholic acid and deoxycholic acid) and decreased conjugated bile acids (glycocholic acid, taurocholic acid, and tauro-ursodeoxycholic acid) relative to benign lung disease. A panel of 20 plasma metabolites, featuring these bile acids, diagnoses LC with an AUC of 0.996. By integrating ASFAM, specialized feature extraction, predictive modeling, and advanced alignment, WT 2.0 significantly enhances detection and identification coverage, providing a powerful tool to expand metabolome analyses across diverse research applications.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Thirty rice accessions utilized for validation experiments were cultivated in paddy fields located in Sanya, Hainan Province, China. Rice leaf samples preparation: For validation experiments, mature leaves from at least three individual rice plants at the tillering stage were combined into a single sample, with three biological replicates per accession. For stress experiments, leaves from at least 30 rice seedlings per treatment were pooled into a single sample, with three biological replicates per treatment.</p><p>For the abiotic stress studies, six representative rice cultivars were selected: MH63 (Japonica), ZS97A (Japonica), ZH11 (Indica), NIP (Indica), Fanhaopi (Aus), and Karayal (Aus).Seeds were sterilized and soaked in water for three days until the radicles emerged, followed by germination at 30 °C for an additional day. Uniformly germinated seedlings were then transferred into a 96-well hydroponic tray shielded from light. Hydroponic experiments were conducted using a standard rice culture solution, with nutrient solutions renewed every two days. Rice seedlings were hydroponically cultured in a growth chamber under controlled conditions: 14-hour photoperiod at 28 °C, 10-hour dark period at 25 °C, illumination intensity at 120 µmol/m²/s, and relative humidity at approximately 70%. When seedlings reached two weeks of age, they were subjected to drought, salt, and heat stresses. Drought stress treatment was performed by exposure to 20% PEG 6000 for 48 hours. Salt stress was induced by treatment with 150 mM NaCl for 24 hours, and heat stress was applied at 42 °C for 14 hours. Untreated seedlings were used as the control group.</p>	<p>All leaf samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until analysis. Extraction of rice leaf metabolites: Leaf samples were freeze-dried for 7 days using a lyophilizer (Scientz-100F/A, Ningbo, China) and subsequently ground into fine powder with a ball mill (MM 400, Retsch, Germany) at 30 Hz for 1 min. Metabolites were extracted overnight by adding 1 mL of 70% methanol (containing 0.01 mg/L lidocaine as internal standard) per 100 mg of leaf powder.</p>	<p>Ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC, CBM40A, Shimadzu, Japan) was employed with a Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 µm, Waters, USA). The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (0.04% aqueous acetic acid) and solvent B (0.04% acetic acid in acetonitrile). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0 min, 95% solvent A/5% solvent B; 10 min, 5% solvent A/95% solvent B; 12 min, 5% solvent A/95% solvent B; 12.1 min, 95% solvent A/5% solvent B; and maintained at 95% solvent A/5% solvent B until 15 min. The flow rate was set to 0.35 mL·min-1, column temperature maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volumes were 5 µL.</p>	<p>ASFAM, DDA, and DIA scans were conducted using a Sciex ZenoTOF 7600 quadrupole-time-of-flight (QTOF) mass spectrometer (Sciex, Canada). For ASFAM acquisition, precursor ions in the range of 75–1075 Da were equally divided into 10 segments, each covering a 100 Da mass window for MS1 scans (experiment 1). The high-resolution multiple reaction monitoring (HR-MRM) mode of ASFAM (experiments 2–101) included 100 MRM transitions with incremental steps of 1 Da, matching the corresponding MS1 acquisition range. For each MRM event, the Q3 scan was configured in a start-stop mode, with the starting mass at 50 Da and the stopping mass set at Q1+3. Given that the ratio of stopping-to-starting mass could not exceed 10, the starting mass was adjusted to (Q1+3)/10 if (Q1+3)/10 was greater than 50 Da. The settings for validation experiments using DDA and DIA modes were as follows: In DDA mode, each MS1 scan triggered up to 100 MS2 scans, with a precursor intensity threshold set at 0. For DIA mode, each method consisted of 20 MS2 windows, each covering 5 Da. Ion-source parameters for all three modes were consistent: Gas 1, Gas 2, Curtain Gas, and collision-activated dissociation (CAD) gas were set at 50, 60, 35, and 8 psi, respectively. Ion-source temperature was maintained at 550 °C, and the spray voltage for positive ionization was set at 5500 V. Declustering potential (DP) for TOF MS scans was set to 50 V, collision energy (CE) was set to 8 V, and CE for all MS2 acquisitions was fixed at 35 eV. The Zeno threshold for both DDA and ASFAM modes was adjusted to 20,000 cps to match the DIA mode setting.</p>	<p>High abundance features (HFs) were extracted using MS-DIAL software71. For single-sample analysis, the ten ASFAM data files were simultaneously imported into MS-DIAL with the following parameters: file type, SWATH; minimum peak height, 100 amplitude; mass slice width, 0.03 Da; smoothing level, 1 scan; minimum peak width, 3 scans. Adduct ions selected in positive-ion mode were [M+H]+, [M+H–H2O]+, [M+NH4]+, [M+Na]+, [M+K]+ and [M]+; in negative-ion mode, [M–H]−, [M–H2O–H]− and [M–2H]2− were chosen, with other parameters set to default. Low abundance features (LFs) were extracted using an in-house program developed for ASFAM data.</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p>[1] Tsugawa, H. et al. MS-DIAL: Data-independent MS/MS deconvolution for comprehensive metabolome analysis. Nat Methods 12, 523 (2015).</p>	<p>Mass spectral libraries from MassBank and the MS-DIAL metabolomics MSP spectral kit were integrated and deduplicated using the library integration and deduplication tool of WT 2.0 method to facilitate metabolite annotation. Redundant signals in MSP-formatted 'peak list result' were initially filtered using the WT 2.0 MSP results screening tool based on the TXT formatted results. These filtered MSP results underwent metabolite annotation using SIRIUS software, and the MS-DIAL and SIRIUS annotations were then merged using the annotation results integration tool.</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p>[1] Tsugawa, H. et al. MS-DIAL: Data-independent MS/MS deconvolution for comprehensive metabolome analysis. Nat Methods 12, 523 (2015).</p><p>[2] Duhrkop, K. et al. SIRIUS 4: a rapid tool for turning tandem mass spectra into metabolite structure information. Nat Methods 16, 299-302 (2019).</p>

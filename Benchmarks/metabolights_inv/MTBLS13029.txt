Study Title	Sulfur-Form-Driven Remodeling of Rhizosphere Microbial Communities and Metabolic Networks in Zostera marina
Study Description	<p>Seagrasses form highly productive coastal ecosystems that play critical roles in carbon sequestration, nutrient cycling, and biodiversity maintenance. However, these ecosystems are often exposed to sulfur-rich environments, where sulfide accumulation poses severe stress to plant growth and survival. To elucidate the physiological and ecological responses of Zostera marina to different sulfur sources, we investigated plant growth performance, the abundance of key sulfur-metabolism genes, and root exudate profiles under treatments with sodium sulfide, thiosulfate, sulfate, and thiourea. Our results showed that thiosulfate treatment significantly enhanced shoot elongation and chlorophyll content, suggesting improved photosynthetic capacity and energy utilization, whereas sulfide exhibited both nutritional and toxic effects. DNA-based quantification revealed distinct shifts in the potential functional guilds: the abundance of dsrA and sqr increased significantly under thiosulfate and sulfide treatments, while apr and soxB responded differentially to sulfur forms. Metabolomic analyses of root exudates further demonstrated that several amino acids and small peptides, together with secondary metabolites, were markedly enriched or depleted under different sulfur treatments, with significant enrichment observed in pathways related to ABC transporters, amino acid biosynthesis, nucleotide metabolism, and plant hormone signaling. Collectively, these findings highlight that different sulfur sources not only modulate the potential functional capacity of rhizosphere microbial communities but also restructure root metabolic outputs, ultimately influencing seagrass growth and resilience. This study provides novel insights into the ecological role of sulfur cycling in the seagrass rhizosphere and suggests that thiosulfate may act as a key intermediate mediating plant–microbe interactions under sulfide stress.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Five millilitres of the treatment solution was pipetted into a sterilized Eppendorf tube (15 ml), and the mixture was quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen for 20 min and then temporarily frozen in an ultralow temperature freezer at -80℃ until freeze-drying; these powders were used for subsequent UPLC-MS identification of the metabolomes.</p>	<p>First, 50 mg of freeze-dried root exudate sample was accurately weighed, 600 μL of a water and methanol mixture was added (1:4), and the mixture was subsequently milled at low temperature for extraction. Second, the extract was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4℃. A total of 380 μL of the supernatant was carefully pipetted into a 2 ml Eppendorf tube and dried with nitrogen. Finally, 120 μL of extraction solution (acetonitrile:water = 1:1) was added to the remix. The extracted metabolites were obtained through a 0.45 μm filter membrane. Twenty microlitres of each of the 26 samples were mixed to prepare the quality control (QC) sample, and 4 QC samples were inserted during the on-machine detections to evaluate the stability of the instrument and ensure the reliability of the results.</p>	<p>The chromatographic column used was an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 (100 mm×2.1 mm i.d., 1.7 μm; Waters, Milford, USA) with 2% acetonitrile water (containing 0.1% formic acid) as mobile phase A and acetonitrile as mobile phase B. The injection volume was 3 μL, the flow rate was 0.4 μL, and the column temperature was 40℃. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0-0.5 min, 95% A; 0.5-7.5 min, 65% A; 7.5-13 min, linear change to 5% and maintained for 1.4 min; and 14.4-14.5 min, linear change to 98% and maintained for 1.5 min.</p>	<p>The mass spectrometry conditions were as follows: sheath gas flow rate of 50 arb, auxiliary gas flow rate of 13 arb, scanning range of 70-1050 m/z, and capillary temperature of 320℃.</p>	<p>Eigenvalues with &gt;20% missing values within each group were removed, missing values were populated to the minimal values of all samples, and all the data were normalized by sumNormalizer and transfered to log10.</p>	<p>The raw data was imported into the metabolomics processing software ProgenesQI v3.0 (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, peak alignment, etc., and finally obtained a data matrix containing information such as retention time, mass to charge ratio, and peak intensity. Afterwards, the software was used for feature peak search library identification, matching MS and MS/MS mass spectrometry information with Meiji's self built plant specific metabolite database (MJDBPM). The mass error of MS was set to less than 10ppm, and metabolites were identified based on the secondary mass spectrometry matching score.</p>

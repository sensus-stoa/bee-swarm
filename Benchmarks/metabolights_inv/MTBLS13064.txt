Study Title	Lily polysaccharides alleviate colitis through the microbiota/N8AS/cGAS-STING signaling axis
Study Description	<p>With the rising incidence of IBD and limitations of current therapies, novel strategies are urgently needed. Recent studies have highlighted natural products in colitis treatment. Here, we investigated the therapeutic effects and mechanisms of lily polysaccharides (LP) in DSS-induced ulcerative colitis (UC). LP supplementation, alone or combined with N8-acetylspermidine (N8AS), alleviated weight loss and intestinal inflammation, restored microbial homeostasis, and increased beneficial bacteria like Bacteroidetes. LP significantly elevated N8AS levels, and exogenous N8AS also ameliorated UC symptoms. Mechanistically, LP and N8AS inhibited the cGAS–STING signaling pathway, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and promoted intestinal barrier repair in vitro. These results suggest that LP exerts anti-colitic effects via modulation of the microbiota/N8AS/cGAS–STING axis. This study identifies LP as a promising natural therapeutic for UC by linking microbiota regulation, metabolic modulation, and immune signaling inhibition.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Male ICR mice aged 7-8 weeks were selected. Adaptive feeding for 7 days at a temperature of 25 degrees Celsius, 12 / 12 light cycles, and free access to drinking water. The acute ulcerative colitis model was constructed by 2 % DSS treatment for 7 days. During the model construction, the body weight change and fecal morphology of the mice were monitored. Exogenous supplementation of LP was performed on days 1-7 of the model construction, with supplemental doses of 150 mg / kg. On Day 9 of treatment, the mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and euthanized by cervical dislocation. The colon contents were collected for subsequent detection.</p>	<p>Approximately 50 mg of each sample was placed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube. To each tube, 500 μL of precooled methanol containing 5 ppm 2-chlorophenylalanine was added, along with two stainless steel beads, followed by vortexing for 30 s. The tubes were then subjected to high-throughput tissue grinding at 55 Hz for 60 s, and this grinding step was repeated once. Subsequently, the samples were sonicated in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min and then incubated at -20°C for 30 min. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and collected into vials for analysis. To prepare quality control (QC) samples, 10–20 μL of the filtered supernatant from each sample was combined into a single QC sample, which was used to assess instrument stability and data reliability.</p>	<p>All chromatographic separations were conducted using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 Å, 1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Waters, USA). The flow rate was maintained at 0.4 mL/min, the column temperature was set to 40°C, and the autosampler was kept at 8°C. The injection volume was 2 μL.</p><p><br></p><p>For positive ion mode, the mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The gradient elution was programmed as follows: 0–1 min, 5% B; 1–7.5 min, 5% to 98% B; 7.5–8.5 min, 98% B; 8.5–9 min, 98% to 5% B; 9–12 min, 5% B.</p><p><br></p><p>For negative ion mode, the mobile phase consisted of solvent A (5 mM ammonium acetate in water) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The gradient was set as: 0–1.5 min, 5% B; 1.5–6 min, 5% to 50% B; 6–8 min, 50% to 98% B; 8–9 min, 98% B; 9–9.1 min, 98% to 5% B; 9.1–12 min, 5% B.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer controlled by Xcalibur software (version 4.7, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in both positive and negative ion modes under data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. A HESI (heated electrospray ionization) source was used with spray voltages set at 3.5 kV (positive mode) and -3.0 kV (negative mode). The sheath gas flow rate was 40 arbitrary units, auxiliary gas was 15 arbitrary units, the capillary temperature was 325°C, and the auxiliary gas heater temperature was 350°C. The full MS (MS1) resolution was set to 60,000, with a scan range of 100–1000 m/z. The AGC target was set to “Standard” and the maximum injection time (Max IT) to 100 ms. The top 4 most intense precursor ions were selected for MS/MS fragmentation. A dynamic exclusion of 8 s was applied. MS/MS (MS2) resolution was set to 15,000 with a normalized HCD collision energy of 30%. The AGC target for MS2 was “Standard” and Max IT was set to “Auto”.</p><p><br></p><p>All experimental and QC samples were analyzed using the chromatographic and mass spectrometric conditions described above. Prior to injecting the test samples, 2–4 QC samples were run to equilibrate the system. During the batch analysis, one QC sample was injected every 5–10 samples to assess data quality and ensure system stability.</p>	<p>The raw data files (.raw format) were imported into the commercial software Compound Discoverer 3.3 (version 3.3.2.31, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Peak extraction, alignment, and retention time correction were performed using the software’s advanced algorithms for peak detection and peak quality scoring. The unique peak rating calculation and filtering functions greatly reduced interference from background noise and low-quality peaks.</p><p><br></p><p>Features that were not detected in more than 50% of QC samples were removed. Missing values were imputed using the software’s Fill Gaps function, and the data were normalized based on the sum of peak areas.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was carried out based on an in-house spectral library and several public databases, including mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org/), LIPID MAPS (https://www.lipidmaps.org/), HMDB (https://hmdb.ca/), MoNA (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/), and the NIST_2020_MSMS library. The mass tolerance for precursor ions (MS1) was set to 15 ppm, and the MS2 match factor threshold was set to 50.</p>

Study Title	Metabolomics data from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum Probio87 intervention in adults with functional constipation
Study Description	<p>To evaluate the therapeutic potential of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum Probio87 in adults with functional constipation (FC), a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial was conducted. A total of 101 adults meeting the Rome IV diagnostic criteria for FC were enrolled. All participants received a standardized healthy lifestyle intervention and were randomly allocated to receive either Probio87 (n = 51) or placebo (n = 50) for 8 weeks, followed by a 4-week follow-up assessment.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Fecal samples were collected from human participants under standardized conditions. Each participant provided a freshly voided stool sample in a sterile container. Samples were collected without any preservatives and immediately placed on ice after defecation. Within two hours of collection, samples were transported to the laboratory under cold-chain conditions. Upon arrival, each fecal sample was homogenized and aliquoted into sterile cryovials. All aliquots were immediately frozen and stored at −80 °C until metabolomic extraction and analysis.</p>	<p>Fecal samples were collected from human participants under standardized conditions. Each participant provided a freshly voided stool sample in a sterile container. Samples were collected without any preservatives and immediately placed on ice after defecation. Within two hours of collection, samples were transported to the laboratory under cold-chain conditions. Upon arrival, each fecal sample was homogenized and aliquoted into sterile cryovials. All aliquots were immediately frozen and stored at −80 °C until metabolomic extraction and analysis.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a Hypersil GOLD C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm particle size). The column oven was maintained at 40 °C.</p><p>The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–1 min, 2% B; 1–9 min, linear increase to 98% B; 9–12 min, held at 98% B; 12.1–15 min, re-equilibration at 2% B. The flow rate was set to 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL.</p><p>All samples, including pooled QC and solvent blanks, were analyzed in a randomized order to minimize instrument drift and carryover. The column was equilibrated with initial mobile phase composition for at least 5 min prior to each run to ensure retention time stability.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection was performed using a high-resolution Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source. The system was operated in both positive and negative ionization modes to achieve broad metabolite coverage.</p><p>Full-scan mass spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 70–1050. The spray voltage was set to 3.5 kV in positive mode and −2.8 kV in negative mode. The sheath gas and auxiliary gas flow rates were maintained at 40 and 10 arbitrary units, respectively. The capillary temperature was 320 °C, and the auxiliary gas heater temperature was 350 °C.</p><p>The resolution for full MS scans was set to 70,000 (at m/z 200), with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1 × 10^6 and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. Data-dependent MS/MS acquisition was performed using stepped normalized collision energies (20, 40, 60 eV).</p><p>Instrument performance was monitored throughout the analytical sequence using pooled QC samples injected every 10 samples to ensure signal stability and mass accuracy.</p>	<p>Raw LC–MS data files obtained from the Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer were first converted to the open-format mzXML using ProteoWizard MSConvert (version 3.0). The converted files were then processed for peak detection, deconvolution, alignment, and normalization using XCMS (version 3.16.0) in the R statistical environment (version 4.3.1).</p><p>Peak picking was performed using the centWave algorithm with optimized parameters for high-resolution data. Retention time correction was achieved using the obiwarp method, and peak grouping was applied across all samples to generate a feature table. Missing values were filled using the fillPeaks function.</p><p>To minimize analytical variation, signal intensities were normalized to total ion current (TIC) and further log-transformed prior to statistical analysis. Features with a relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt;30% in quality control (QC) samples were excluded to ensure data reliability.</p><p>All processed data matrices were exported as CSV files for subsequent multivariate statistical and pathway analysis.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed based on accurate mass, MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and retention time information. The processed peak list generated from XCMS was imported into MS-DIAL (version 5.1) for feature annotation and compound identification.</p><p>Spectral matching was conducted against multiple public databases, including HMDB (Human Metabolome Database), KEGG, and Lipidmaps, using a mass accuracy tolerance of ≤5 ppm for precursor ions and ≤10 ppm for fragment ions. Peaks with MS/MS spectra showing cosine similarity scores ≥0.8 were considered confidently matched.</p><p>For features with available authentic standards, identification was confirmed by comparing retention time and MS/MS spectra to reference compounds analyzed under identical conditions (Level 1 identification, according to the MSI guidelines). Other metabolites without reference standards were annotated based on MS/MS similarity and database matches (Level 2 or Level 3 identification).</p><p>All identified and annotated metabolites were manually verified to ensure spectral quality and biological plausibility.</p>

Study Title	Aerobic exercise and gut flora: a key link to improved cognitive impairment in mice with Parkinson's disease
Study Description	<p>Parkinson's disease (PD) is a common neurodegenerative disorder. It is marked by motor dysfunction and cognitive decline. In recent years, scientific studies have found that PD's pathogenesis may be tied to an imbalance in the gut microbiota. This offers new perspectives for PD treatment. Modulating the gut microbiota is recognized as a potential way to enhance PD symptoms. While aerobic exercise can positively influence the gut microbiota, research on how the gut microbiota mediates aerobic exercise's effects on PD cognitive impairment is still limited. Thus, this study aimed to explore the potential mechanisms by which aerobic exercise improves cognitive impairment in PD patients. It does so by modulating the gut microbiota's structure and, in turn, improving cognitive function. Through this study, we hope to offer new strategies and a theoretical basis for treating PD cognitive impairment.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>This study employed terminal cardiac puncture for whole blood collection. A total of six SPF-grade ICR mice per group were selected for the experiment. Following anesthesia with isoflurane inhalation, the mice were positioned in a supine posture and secured to a thermostatic surgical table using sterile medical tape. After shaving the surgical area, triple disinfection was sequentially performed using povidone-iodine and alcohol. Prior to puncture, a 1 mL sterile syringe was pre-rinsed with a 0.5% sodium heparin solution (with air expelled and kept ready for use). The operator wore sterile gloves, palpated to locate the point of maximal apical impulse, and inserted the needle into the ventricle at a 45-degree angle. Upon confirmation of blood reflux, blood was drawn at a uniform rate, with the collection volume calculated based on body weight approximately 0.8–1.5 mL per mouse (20–30 g). The collected whole blood was immediately gently mixed with a pre-cooled anticoagulant (blood:sodium heparin = 1 mL:20 μL) and transferred into a pre-chilled 4°C centrifuge tube. After centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C, the plasma layer was aspirated using a sterile pipette, aliquoted, and stored in a -80°C freezer.</p>	<p>First, we added 100 μL of each sample to 400 μL of an 80% methanol aqueous solution. We then vortexed and shook the mixture to extract the supernatant. Next, we diluted this supernatant to a 53% methanol solution. After that, we centrifuged it again and collected the supernatant for LC - MS analysis. For quality control (QC), we took a medium - volume aliquot from each experimental sample and mixed them together to create the QC samples. As for blank samples, we used a 53% methanol aqueous solution instead.</p>	<p>The separation was performed using a Hypersil Gold (C18) column maintained at 40 °C. A mobile phase gradient consisting of 0.1% formic acid (A) and methanol (B) was delivered at a constant flow rate of 0.2 mL/min.</p>	<p>The mass spectrometry scan was set to a range of m/z 100-1500. The electrospray ionization (ESI) source parameters were configured as follows: Spray Voltage: 3.5 kV; Sheath Gas Flow Rate: 35 psi; Auxiliary Gas Flow Rate: 10 L/min; Capillary Temperature: 320 °C; S-Lens RF Level: 60; Auxiliary Gas Heater Temperature: 350 °C; Polarity: positive and negative. MS/MS fragmentation was performed using data-dependent scanning.</p>	<p>The acquired data files (.raw) were processed using the CD 3.3 software suite. An initial filtering of metabolites was performed based on parameters such as retention time and mass-to-charge ratio. To enhance identification accuracy, peak area correction was applied using the first Quality Control (QC) sample. Peak picking was then conducted with the following settings: a mass tolerance of 5 ppm, a signal intensity tolerance of 30%, a minimum signal intensity threshold, and specified adduct ions. Concurrently, peak area quantification was performed.</p><p><br></p><p>The targeted ions were integrated, and molecular formula prediction was carried out by analyzing the molecular and fragment ions. These predictions were subsequently matched against the mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org/), mzVault, and Masslist databases. Background ions were removed by subtracting the blank sample.</p><p><br></p><p>The raw quantitative results were normalized using the formula: (Original quantitative value of a sample) / (Sum of metabolite quantitative values in the sample / Sum of metabolite quantitative values in the QC1 sample) to obtain the relative peak areas. Compounds with a coefficient of variation (CV) of the relative peak area exceeding 30% in the QC samples were discarded. The final output comprised the identified metabolites and their relative quantification results. All data processing steps were performed on a Linux operating system (CentOS version 6.6) utilizing the R and Python software environments.</p>	<p>The identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG database , HMDB , and LIPIDMaps databases.</p><p><br></p><p>For multivariate statistical analysis, the metabolomics data were processed using the metaX software. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) were performed, from which the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score for each metabolite was obtained. In the univariate analysis, a Student's t-test was applied to calculate the statistical significance (P-value) of each metabolite between the two groups, and the fold change (FC) was also computed. The default criteria for screening differential metabolites were set as follows: VIP &gt; 1, P-value &lt; 0.05, and FC&nbsp;2 or FC&nbsp;0.5.</p><p><br></p><p>Volcano plots were generated using the R package ggplot2, which integrates the three parameters—VIP score, log2(Fold Change), and -log10(P-value)—to facilitate the selection of metabolites of interest.</p><p><br></p><p>Cluster heatmaps were plotted using the R package Pheatmap, with metabolite data normalized using z-scores.</p><p><br></p><p>Correlation analysis between differential metabolites, based on Pearson correlation coefficients, was conducted using the cor() function in R. Statistical significance was assessed with the cor.mtest() function, where a P-value &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The correlation matrix was visualized using the corrplot package in R.</p><p><br></p><p>Bubble plots for functional enrichment were generated with the R package ggplot2. The KEGG database was utilized to investigate metabolite functions and pathways. A metabolic pathway was considered enriched when the ratio x/n &gt; y/n, and it was deemed significantly enriched when the pathway's P-value was less than 0.05.</p>

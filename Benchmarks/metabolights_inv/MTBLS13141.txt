Study Title	Pla2g7 regulates bone homeostasis via Alox12/12-HETE/Gpr31 signaling axis
Study Description	<p>Bone homeostasis mainly depends on the equilibrium of osteoclasts and osteoblasts, overactivated osteoclasts play a pivotal role in the progression of osteoporosis. Here, we revealed that Pla2g7 (phospholipase A2 group VII) was positively correlated with bone resorption in clinic. By single-cell RNA-seq data analysis, Pla2g7 was found highly enriched in osteoclasts along the developmental trajectory, which promoted osteoclast differentiation. Inhibition of Pla2g7 by Darapladib impaired both human and mice osteoclast differentiation, meanwhile, Pla2g7-deficient mice showed higher bone mass and restored the ovariectomy-induced bone loss. Mechanistically, we identified that Alox12 (arachidonate 12-lipoxygenase) mediated-arachidonic acid metabolism is a key determinant in Pla2g7 enhanced osteoclast differentiation. Its metabolite 12-HETE (12-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid) activated Gpr31 to regulate osteoclast formation via p38 MAPK pathway and mitochondrial energy metabolism. Collectively, our study uncovers an Alox12/12-HETE/Gpr31 axis that regulates Pla2g7-induced osteoclast differentiation, and provides a new insight for osteoporosis treatment.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMMs) were seeded at a density of 1×1061 \times 10^{6}1×106 cells per 6-cm culture dish and maintained under standard humidified conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) for initial proliferation. For osteoclast differentiation, recombinant receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) was added, and the cells were continuously cultured for five days, either in the absence or presence of the selective <strong>PLA2G7 inhibitor</strong> <strong>Darapladib</strong>. At the end of the induction period, mature or differentiated cells were carefully washed with cold PBS, harvested using a cell scraper, and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve RNA and protein integrity for subsequent molecular analyses.</p>	<p>The frozen samples were gently thawed on ice before metabolite extraction using a 50% methanol buffer. For each sample, 20 μL of material was mixed with 120 μL of prechilled 50% methanol, followed by vigorous vortexing for 1 min to ensure thorough extraction. The mixtures were then left to equilibrate at room temperature for approximately 10 min and subsequently placed at −20 °C overnight to enhance protein precipitation. After centrifugation at 4,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, the clear supernatants were carefully collected and transferred into clean 96-well plates. All extracts were preserved at −80 °C until LC-MS analysis. To monitor instrument stability and data reproducibility, pooled quality-control (QC) samples were prepared by combining 10 μL aliquots from each individual extract.</p>	<p>All samples were analyzed in sequence on the LC–MS platform following the predefined acquisition order. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Thermo Scientific UltiMate 3000 HPLC system equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, UK) operated under reversed-phase conditions. The column temperature was kept constant at 35 °C, and the flow rate was maintained at 0.4 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The elution was performed under the following linear gradient: 0–0.5 min, 5% B; 0.5–7 min, 5–100% B; 7–8 min, 100% B; 8–8.1 min, 100–5% B; and 8.1–10 min, 5% B for column equilibration. The injection volume for each sample was 4 μL.</p>	<p>The LC system was interfaced with a TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA) equipped with a DuoSpray™ ion source. Data acquisition was carried out in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes to ensure a comprehensive metabolite coverage. The ion spray voltage was maintained at +5500 V in the positive mode and −4500 V in the negative mode. The curtain gas was set to 35 psi, while ion source gases 1 and 2 were adjusted to 55 psi and 60 psi, respectively, to stabilize nebulization and desolvation. The ion source temperature was held at 550 °C throughout the analysis to promote efficient ion generation and minimize solvent interference.</p><p>Mass spectrometric data were acquired in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode, enabling automatic switching between full-scan MS and MS/MS acquisition. Time-of-flight (TOF) survey scans were collected over an m/z range of 100–1200 with a resolving power of approximately 30,000, followed by MS/MS acquisition of the 20 most intense precursor ions in each cycle using collision-induced dissociation (CID). The collision energy was set to 35 ± 15 eV, optimized for metabolite fragmentation, and the total duty cycle for one IDA experiment was maintained at 1.0 s to balance sensitivity and spectral coverage.</p>	<p>The acquired LC–MS data were subjected to a series of preprocessing steps to ensure accurate feature extraction and alignment across all samples. Raw data files were first converted into the mzXML format using the ProteoWizard toolkit, providing compatibility with downstream analysis. Subsequent data processing, including peak detection, grouping, retention time alignment, and re-grouping, was carried out using the XCMS package in R. In addition, isotopic patterns and adduct ion species were annotated with the CAMERA package to improve feature identification. The processed peak table was further refined and normalized using the metaX toolbox, which enabled correction of batch effects and preparation of the data matrix for subsequent statistical and multivariate analyses.</p>	<p>The raw LC–MS files generated on the Sciex ExionLC–TripleTOF platform (.wiff) were converted to open format (mzML) using ProteoWizard msConvert. Raw data were processed using MS-DIAL for peak detection, deconvolution, alignment and tentative annotation. Peak picking and alignment parameters included a minimum peak height of 1,000 counts, a retention time tolerance of 0.2 min and a precursor mass tolerance of ±10 ppm. Fragment ion matching was performed with a tolerance of ±0.05 Da. Lipid and metabolite annotation was carried out by matching accurate mass, retention time and MS/MS fragmentation patterns against public reference databases including LIPID MAPS, LipidBlast and HMDB, and confirmed against in-house spectra where available. Spectral matches with similarity scores ≥0.7 were considered putative identifications. Data were normalized using probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN). QC samples were used to monitor system stability and correction of batch effects (QC-based LOESS/other correction as applicable). Differential analysis was performed using PLS-DA to obtain VIP scores and Student’s t-test to compute P values; significance thresholds were set at VIP &gt; 1, P &lt; 0.05 and fold change (FC) &gt; 1.2.</p>

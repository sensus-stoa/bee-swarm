Study Title	Different Metabolic Pathways Associated With Total Cortisol Exposure and the Cortisol Time Profile: A Randomized Crossover Trial
Study Description	<p>Excess cortisol exposure and disruption of the circadian cortisol profile are both associated with adverse clinical outcomes. The aim of this study was to identify unique metabolites and metabolic pathways associated with total cortisol exposure and/or variation of the cortisol time profile. Eighteen adults with primary adrenal insufficiency (AI) received the same daily dose of hydrocortisone (HC) administered as a once-daily (OD) dual-release tablet and conventional HC tablets 3 times daily (TID) in a randomized, 12-week, cross-over trial. Serum and urine samples were collected during 24 h in-house standardized pharmacokinetic sampling days and metabolites were detected using liquid and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS and GC-MS).&nbsp;Total cortisol exposure was calculated by the area under the serum cortisol concentration-time curve and variability of the cortisol time profile was quantified by calculating the lag–1 autocorrelation from serum cortisol concentrations over 24 hours. During TID administration, total cortisol exposure was 20% higher and the cortisol time profile variability was larger, compared to OD administration. In total, 2406 metabolites were detected. Serum metabolites uniquely correlated with total cortisol exposure were involved in arginine biosynthesis, and alanine-aspartate-glutamate and tryptophan metabolism. Serum metabolites uniquely correlated with the cortisol time profile variability were involved in primary bile acid biosynthesis and cysteine-methionine metabolism. We identified different groups of metabolites and metabolic pathways that specifically correlate with total exposure and time profile variability of serum cortisol. These findings suggest that total cortisol exposure and the time profile of serum cortisol have independent metabolic and regulatory effects in the human body.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Eighteen patients diagnosed with primary adrenal insufficiency &gt; 6 m prior to study entry were included. All patients had received stable GC replacement therapy with <strong>hydrocortisone </strong>(<strong>HC</strong>) 20-40 mg/d for ≥ 3 m prior to study entry and the dose was kept constant throughout the study. Patients were randomized into<strong> two 12-wk treatment periods</strong> to receive dual-release <strong>HC </strong>treatment once daily (<strong>OD</strong>) and conventional HC treatment thrice daily (<strong>TID</strong>) in a crossover manner. All patients underwent a standardized in-house pharmacokinetic sampling for serum cortisol profiling over 24 h at the beginning of each treatment period. Blood samples were collected over the 24 h period and 24 h urine samples were collected as well. Serum samples from eight timepoints (baseline and 25 min, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 16 h and 24 h post first tablet intake) were used for cortisol and metabolite analysis in this study. Patients remained in the clinical trial unit during the entire sampling day and received standardized meals at pre-defined time points. <strong>OD </strong>dual-release <strong>HC </strong>tablets were administered orally in a fasting state in the morning at 08:00 h and the <strong>TID </strong>conventional <strong>HC </strong>tablets were administered orally at 08:00, 12:00, and 16:00 h. Serum and urine samples were stored at - 80 °C until the analysis of metabolomics. All analyses were performed in one run.</p>	<p>Blood serum samples</p><p>Sample preparation of blood serum was performed as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. In detail, 900 µL of extraction buffer (90/10 v/v methanol: water) including internal standards for both GC-MS and LC-MS were added to 100 µL of serum. LC-MS internal standards included: 13C9-Phenylalanine, 13C3-Caffeine, D4-Cholic acid, salicylic acid-D6,13C9-Caffeic Acid all obtained from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA). GC-MS&nbsp;internal standards included: L-proline-13C5, alpha-ketoglutarate-13C4, myristic acid-13C3 and cholesterol-D7, obtained from Cil (Andover, MA, USA), and succinic acid-D4, L-glutamic acid-13C5,15N, putrescine-D4, hexadecanoic acid-13C4, D-glucose-13C6, D-sucrose-13C12, obtained from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA).</p><p><br></p><p>The sample was shaken at 30 Hz for 2 min in a mixer mill and proteins were precipitated at 4 °C on ice. The sample was centrifuged at 4 °C, 14 000 rpm, for 10 min. The supernatant, 200 µL for LCMS analysis and 50 µL to GCMS analysis, was transferred to micro vials and evaporated to dryness in a speed-vac concentrator. Solvents were evaporated and the samples were stored at - 80 °C until analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>A small aliquot of the remaining supernatants were pooled and used to create quality control (QC) samples, aliquots of this pooled material were used as QC samples throughout the analytical run. MS/MS analysis (LCMS) was run on the QC samples for identification purposes.</p><p><br></p><p>Urine samples</p><p>The urine samples (100 µL aliquots) were thawed at room temperature prior to processing. Each aliquot was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove particulates. From the resulting supernatant, 10 µL was transferred into LC-MS vials pre-aliquoted with 100µL 50% methanol containing LC-MS internal standards at a final concentration of 0.0625 ng/µL. A 2 µL injection volume was used for analysis. Samples were first analyzed using electrospray ionization in positive mode (ESI+), followed by re-analysis in negative mode (ESI-).</p><p><br></p><p>A QC pool was prepared by combining equal volumes from a representative subset of urine samples, and aliquots of this pooled material were used as QC samples throughout the analytical run. MS/MS analysis (LCMS) was run on the QC samples for identification purposes.</p><p>The samples were analyzed in batches according to a randomized run order on both GC-MS and LC-MS.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> A J, Trygg J, Gullberg J, Johansson AI, Jonsson P, Antti H, Marklund SL, Moritz T. Extraction and GC/MS analysis of the human blood plasma metabolome. Anal Chem. 2005 Dec 15;77(24):8086-94. DOI:10.1021/ac051211v. PMID: 16351159.</p>	<p><strong>LC-MS</strong></p><p>Before LCMS analysis of the serum samples was re-suspended in 10 µL methanol +10 µL water. Each batch of samples was first analyzed in positive mode. After all samples within a batch had been analyzed, the instrument was switched to negative mode and a second injection of each sample was performed.&nbsp;The chromatographic separation was performed on an <strong>Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC-system (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany)</strong>. 2 μL of each sample were injected onto an Acquity UPLC HSS T3, 2.1 x 50 mm, 1.8 μm C18 column in combination with a <strong>VanGuard precolumn (2.1 mm x 5 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA)</strong> held at 40 °C. The gradient elution buffers were A (H2O, 0.1 % formic acid) and B (75/25 acetonitrile:2-propanol, 0.1 % formic acid), and the flow-rate was 0.5 mL/min. The compounds were eluted with a linear gradient consisting of 0.1 to 10 % B over 2 min, B was increased to 99 % over 5 min and held at 99 % for 2 min; B was decreased to 0.1 % for 0.3 min and the flow-rate was increased to 0.8 mL per min for 0.5 min; these conditions were held for 0.9 min, after which the flow-rate was reduced to 0.5 mL per min for 0.1 min before the next injection.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GC-MS</strong></p><p>Derivatization and GCMS analysis were performed as described previously<strong>[1]</strong>. 0.5 μL of the derivatized sample was injected in splitless mode by a<strong> L-PAL3 autosampler (CTC Analytics AG, Switzerland)</strong> into an<strong> Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph </strong>equipped with a <strong>10 m x 0.18 mm fused silica capillary column</strong> with a chemically bonded<strong> 0.18 μm Rxi-5 Sil MS stationary phase (Restek Corporation, U.S.)</strong> The injector temperature was 270 °C, the purge flow rate was 20 mL/min and the purge was turned on after 60 seconds. The gas flow rate through the column was 1 mL/min, the column temperature was held at 70 °C for 2 min, then increased by 40 °C per min, up to 320 °C, and held there for 2 min.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>A J, Trygg J, Gullberg J, Johansson AI, Jonsson P, Antti H, Marklund SL, Moritz T. Extraction and GC/MS analysis of the human blood plasma metabolome. Anal Chem. 2005 Dec 15;77(24):8086-94. DOI:10.1021/ac051211v. PMID: 16351159.</p>	<p><strong>LC-MS</strong></p><p>The compounds were detected with an <strong>Agilent 6550 Q-TOF mass spectrometer </strong>equipped with a jet stream electrospray ion source operating in positive or negative ion mode. The settings were kept identical between the modes, with exception of the capillary voltage. A reference interface was connected for accurate mass measurements; the reference ions purine (4 μM) and HP-0921 (Hexakis(1H, 1H, 3H-tetrafluoropropoxy)phosphazine) (1 μM) were infused directly into the MS at a flow rate of 0.05 mL min-1 for internal calibration, and the monitored ions were purine m/z 121.05 and m/z 119.03632; HP-0921 m/z 922.0098 and m/z 966.000725 for positive and negative mode respectively. The gas temperature was set to 150 °C, the drying gas flow to 16 L /min and the nebulizer pressure 35 psi. The sheath gas temp was set to 350°C and the sheath gas flow 11 L/min. The capillary voltage was set to 4000 V in positive ion mode, and to 4000 V in negative ion mode. The nozzle voltage was 300 V. The fragmentor voltage was 380 V, the skimmer 45 V and the OCT 1 RF Vpp 750 V. The collision energy was set to 0 V. The <strong>m/z range was 70 - 1700</strong>, and data was collected in centroid mode with an acquisition rate of 4 scans s-1 (1977 transients/spectrum).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GC-MS</strong></p><p>The GC-column effluent was introduced into the ion source of a <strong>Pegasus BT time-of-flight mass spectrometer, GC/TOFMS (Leco Corp., St Joseph, MI, USA)</strong>. The transfer line and the ion source temperatures were 250 °C and 200 °C, respectively. Ions were generated by a 70 eV electron beam at an ionization current of 2.0 mA, and 30 spectra/s were recorded in the mass range<strong> m/z 50 - 800</strong>. The acceleration voltage was turned on after a solvent delay of 150 s. The detector voltage was 1800-2300 V.&nbsp;</p>	<p><strong>LC-MS</strong></p><p>For the LC-MS data, the data pre-processing was performed using the <strong>Agilent MassHunter Profinder version B.10.0.2 (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA)</strong>.&nbsp;An in-house LC-MS library built up by authentic standards run on the same system with the same chromatographic and mass-spec settings was searched for using the Batch Targeted feature extraction with a m/z tolerance of 20 ppm and a retention time window of 0.1 min. For untargeted pre-processing the <strong>Batch Recursive Feature Extraction algorithm</strong> within <strong>Masshunter Profinder</strong> was used .</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GC-MS</strong></p><p>For the GC-MS data, all non-processed MS-files from the metabolic analysis were exported from the <strong>ChromaTOF</strong> software in <strong>NetCDF</strong> format to <strong>MATLAB R2021a (Mathworks, Natick, MA, USA)</strong>, where all data pre-treatment procedures, such as base-line correction, chromatogram alignment, data compression and Multivariate Curve Resolution were performed.</p>	<p><strong>LC-MS</strong></p><p>The annotation of the metabolites in the targeted pre-processing was based on MS, retention time and MS/MS information, and compared against an in-house library of authentic standards.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GC-MS</strong></p><p>The extracted mass spectra were identified by comparisons of their retention index and mass spectra with libraries of retention time indices and mass spectra<strong>[1]</strong>. Mass spectra and retention index comparison was performed using<strong> NIST MS 2.2</strong> software. Annotation of mass spectra was based on reverse and forward searches in the library. Masses and ratio between masses indicative of a derivatized metabolite were especially notified. The mass spectrum with the highest probability indicative of a metabolite and the retention index between the sample and library for the suggested metabolite was ± 5 (usually less than 3) the deconvoluted “peak” was annotated as an identification of a metabolite.</p><p><br></p><p>Confidence level for annotation of the metabolites was set according to systems used in the field<strong>[2]-[4]</strong>. Five levels of confidence were defined: Level 1 (Confirmed identification): Identification based on direct comparison with an authentic reference standard analysed under identical analytical conditions. Agreement in accurate mass (m/z), retention time (RT), and, in most cases, MS/MS fragmentation spectra was required. <strong>Level 1*</strong> <strong>(Isomeric mixture)</strong>: Applied when authentic standards confirmed the presence of a set of structural isomers, but chromatographic resolution or isomer-specific MS/MS fragmentation could not be achieved. These were reported as isomeric mixtures (e.g., “compound A/compound B”).<strong> Level 2</strong> <strong>(Putative annotation/structure)</strong>: Assignment based on spectral library match and/or diagnostic MS/MS evidence, but without direct confirmation using an authentic standard. <strong>Level 3 (Tentative annotation/compound class)</strong>: Annotation based on accurate mass, isotope distribution, and database search, providing either a tentative structure or assignment to a compound class. <strong>Level 4</strong> <strong>(Molecular formula)</strong>: Detected features with accurate mass and molecular formula but without sufficient evidence for structural or class assignment. <strong>Level 5 (Unique feature)</strong>: Accurate mass measured without confirmed formula.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Schauer N, Steinhauser D, Strelkov S, Schomburg D, Allison G, Moritz T, Lundgren K, Roessner-Tunali U, Forbes MG, Willmitzer L, Fernie AR &amp; Kopka J.

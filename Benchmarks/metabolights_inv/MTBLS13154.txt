Study Title	Steam-exploded okara ameliorates hepatic lipid metabolism in high-fat diet-induced obese C57BL/6J mice: Evidence from multi-omics characterization
Study Description	<p>Okara, a byproduct of soybean processing, possesses valuable nutritional and physiological properties. Steam explosion, an efficient physical modification technique, can increase the soluble dietary fiber (SDF) content in okara and potentially amplify its health benefits, such as anti-obesity effects and intestinal regulation. This study employed steam explosion to modify okara and investigated the ameliorative effect of modified okara (MO) on hepatic lipid metabolism in high-fat diet (HFD) -induced obese mice and its potential mechanisms. The modified okara exhibited significantly increased SDF content and improved hypolipidemic and antioxidant properties. Histopathological analysis revealed that MO intervention significantly suppressed body weight gain by 54%, markedly reduced fat accumulation in hepatic and adipose tissue, and significantly suppressed inflammatory cytokine production in HFD mice. Furthermore, microbiome, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses were conducted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. The results showed that MO modulated the gut microbiota composition by increasing the abundance of Dubosiella, Akkermansia, and CAG-485 while decreasing Faecalibaculum and Malihella. Hepatic transcriptomics revealed that MO modulated the expression of key genes involved in lipid metabolism (e.g., Cyp4a14 and Ehhadh) and activated relevant metabolic pathways such as the PPAR signaling pathway. Metabolomics analysis identified significant alterations in phospholipids, fatty acids, and bile acid metabolism. These findings provided mechanistic insights into MO's anti-obesity properties and supported its potential application as a functional food ingredient for metabolic disorder management.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Pathogen-free male C57BL/6J mice were divided into 4 groups: (1) N group: mice were fed with normal diet; (2) HFD group: mice were fed with high-fat diet (HFD); (3) O group: mice were fed with HFD supplemented with 20% okara; (4) MO group: mice were fed with HFD supplemented with 20% modified okara (MO). Throughout the 8-week intervention period, hepatic tissues were harvested, rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, the samples were stored at -80&nbsp;for further analysis.</p>	<p>Approximately 50 mg of liver tissue was rapidly transferred into 200 μL of methanol/acetonitrile/water (2:2:1, v/v) and homogenized in a cryogenic grinder for 30 min. An additional 300 μL of methanol/acetonitrile/water (2:2:1, v/v) was added, followed by vortex mixing and incubation at 20 °C for 2 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, 350 μL of the supernatant was concentrated in a vacuum centrifugal concentrator for 2 h, and reconstituted with 100 μL of methanol/water (3:7, v/v). After thorough mixing, the solution was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and 75 μL of the supernatant was transferred to an injection vial. Quality control (QC) samples were interspersed within the sample sequence to monitor analytical bias, evaluate system stability, and assess the reliability of experimental data.</p>	<p>Equipment: UPLC-QTOF-MSE system equipped with an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm)</p><p>Chromatographic Conditions:&nbsp;</p><p>Column: BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm; Waters Corp., USA), maintained at 25 °C</p><p>Flow rate: 0.5 mL/min</p><p>Injection volume: 1 μL</p><p>Mobile phase:</p><p>Phase A: Aqueous solution containing 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide (pH adjusted to 9.75)</p><p>Phase B: Pure acetonitrile</p><p>Gradient elution program:</p><p>0–0.5 min: 95% B (isocratic)</p><p>0.5–7 min: Linear decrease from 95% to 65% B</p><p>7–8 min: Linear decrease from 65% to 40% B</p><p>8–9 min: 40% B (isocratic)</p><p>9–9.1 min: Rapid increase from 40% to 95% B</p><p>9.1–12 min: 95% B (column re-equilibration)</p>	<p>Equipment: UPLC-QTOF-MSE system (Acquity UPLC I-class, Waters Co., LTD. US)</p><p>Mass Spectrometric Conditions:&nbsp;</p><p>Mass range: 60–1000 Da</p><p>Nebulizer gas pressure: 60 psi (arbitrary units)</p><p>Auxiliary gas pressure: 60 psi (arbitrary units)</p><p>Gas temperature: 650 °C</p><p>Ion source temperature: 600 °C</p><p>Spray voltage: ±5500 V</p><p>Capillary voltage: 2 kV</p><p>Cone voltage: 40 V</p><p>Collision energy: 35 eV</p>	<p>Peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction were performed using XCMS software. Data extracted by XCMS underwent metabolite structural identification and data preprocessing, followed by experimental data quality assessment and final data analysis. After normalizing the processed data to total peak intensity, analysis was conducted using R software, including multivariate data analysis such as PLS-DA and OPLS-DA. The robustness of the method model was assessed using seven-fold cross-validation and reaction substitution testing.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification and analysis were performed using the Personalbio GenesCloud in-house database (https://www.genescloud.cn ) and public databases (Luo et al., 2017; Zhaobing et al., 2018). Metabolites in biological samples were structurally identified by matching retention times, molecular weights (with mass error &lt;10 ppm), secondary fragmentation spectra, and collision energies. All identification results underwent rigorous manual secondary verification and confirmation.

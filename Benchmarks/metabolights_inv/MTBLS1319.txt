Study Title	Bioaccumulation of therapeutic drugs by human gut bacteria: duloxetine accumulation at different concentrations (L.saccharolytica;E.coli assays)
Study Description	<p>Bacteria in the gut can modulate the availability and efficacy of therapeutic drugs. Interactions at this level have only recently started to being systematically mapped and the main underlying mechanism proposed is chemical transformation of drugs by microbes (biotransformation). Here, we investigated the depletion of 15 structurally diverse drugs by 25 representative gut bacterial strains. This revealed 70 bacteria-drug interactions, 29 of which had not been reported before.</p><p>Over half of the new interactions can be ascribed to bioaccumulation, that is bacteria storing the drug without chemically modifying it, and in most cases without their growth being affected. As a case in point, we studied the molecular basis of bioaccumulation of the widely used antidepressant duloxetine by using click-chemistry, thermal proteome profiling and metabolomics. We find that duloxetine binds to several metabolic enzymes and changes metabolite secretion of the respective bacteria. When tested in a defined microbial community of accumulators and non-accumulators, duloxetine markedly altered the community composition through metabolic cross-feeding. We further validated our findings in an animal model, showing that bioaccumulating bacteria attenuate the behavioral response of Caenorhabditis elegans to duloxetine.</p><p>Taken together, bioaccumulation by gut bacteria might be a common mechanism that alters drug availability and bacterial metabolism, with implications for microbiota composition, pharmacokinetics, side effects and drug responses, likely in an individual manner.</p><p><br></p><p>Linked studies;</p><p><strong>UPLC-MS/MS assays </strong>of <em>Bifidobacterium animalis </em>subsp. lactis are reported in <a Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Growth conditions </strong></p><p>Unless otherwise indicated, all bacteria were grown as liquid cultures in Gut Microbiota Medium (GMM). Cultivations were carried out in a Vinyl Anaerobic Chamber (COY, USA) at 37 °C with oxygen below 20 ppm, 15% carbon dioxide and 1.8-2% hydrogen. Main gas in the anaerobic chamber was nitrogen. All experimental cultures were started from the second passage culture after inoculation from a glycerol or DMSO stock. All media, buffer, glass and plasticware used in the study were exposed to the anaerobic conditions at least 12 h prior to their usage.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Bacteria-drug interaction screen</strong></p><p>For all drugs, a fixed concentration of 50 μM was used, which is within the colon concentration range estimated for many drugs. Triplicates for each bacterium-drug interaction and a single bacteria-free control were screened per plate and drug. The screen was carried out under anaerobic conditions in 96-well plates (Nunclon Delta Surface 163320, NUNC) with 150 μl GMM as the growth medium sealed with a Breathe-Easy® sealing membrane (Z380059, Sigma-371 Aldrich). Plates containing 100 μl of the medium and 75 μM of the drug were prepared beforehand, stored at -20 °C and used as needed.</p><p>Frozen plates were introduced into the anaerobic chamber the evening before inoculation. Wells were inoculated with 50 μl of a second passage culture to reach starting OD578 of 0.01. Growth was monitored by measuring optical densities using an Eon Microplate Spectrophotometer (BioTek) approximately every 2 hr for the first 10 hr, and then approximately every 6 hr.</p><p>After 48 hr, plates were removed from the anaerobic chamber and the bacteria were spun down (4000 rpm, 10 min). Supernatant (100 μl) was extracted in 300 μl ice cold acetonitrile:methanol (Biosolve, ULC grade) in 500 μl polypropylene plates (Corning Costar 3957) to remove compounds interfering with liquid chromatography. Plates were closed with a lid (Corning, storage mat 3080) and after shaking, incubated at 4 °C for 15 min. Samples were then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and 300 μl of the supernatant were transferred to a new plate (Corning Costar 3362).</p>	<p>After 48 hr of incubation, culture plates were removed from the anaerobic chamber and 10 μl aliquots of ‘total’ culture (medium + bacteria) samples were transfered to a new 96 well plate. The remaining culture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to harvest the supernatant and 10 μl aliquots of ‘supernatant’ culture added to another 96 well plate.</p><p>Ice cold (1 ml, -20 °C) extraction solvent (see recipes in associated paper) was added to the 10 μl aliquots of both total culture and supernatant and samples vortexed for 5 min. Sample aliquots (25 μl) were further diluted with 500 μl of extraction solvent in polypropylene deep 96-well plates (Corning Costar 3957).</p><p>Plates were closed with a lid (Corning, storage mat 3080), incubated for 15 min at -20 °C followed and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Aliquots of 100 μl of extracted supernatant were then transfered to WebSeal 96-well plates for LCMS analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Control samples</strong></p><p>Three internal standards were used: fluoxetine, sulfamethizole and sulfamethazine at final concentrations of&nbsp;2.5 nM, 100 nM and 40 nM respectively. In addition, to account for the complex matrix effects, drug standards were prepared by adding the drug to the C. saccharolyticum supernatants or cell lysates and extracting from these in acetonitrile:methanol in the same manner as for the samples. Solvent blanks (acetonitrile:methanol) and media-only controls (incubated as the samples) were run intermittently to ensure that there was no drug carry-over from the system.</p>	<p>Sample separation was performed on a Thermo Vanquish UHPLC system using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100Å, 1.8 µm; 2.1 X 100 mm; Waters UK, part number: 186003539) maintained at 40 °C. The injection volume was 2 μl. A gradient was maintained with buffer A (0.1 % formic acid in 1000 ml of water) and buffer B (0.1 % formic acid in 1000 ml of methanol) for 10 min with flow rate of 0.3 ml/min: 10% solvent B for 2 min ramped up to 90% for next 2 min, held for 2 min followed by 4 min of equilibration to the initial condition (10% of solvent B).</p>	<p>The analytes were detected using a Q-Exactive plus with HRMS full scan at the mass resolving power R=35000 in a mass range of 60-900 m/z. For identification of analytes; the data-dependent tandem (MS/MS) scans were obtained along with full scans using higher-energy collision dissociation (HCD) with normalized collision energies of 30, 35 and 40 units (at R=17500), which were then compared with MS/MS spectra obtained from authentic standards. The MS parameters in the Tune software (Thermo Scientific) were set as: ESI positive voltage of 4 kV, sheath gas 30 and auxiliary gas 5 units, S-Lens 65 eV, capillary temperature 320 °C and vaporization temperature of auxiliary gas was 250 to 441 °C. The data analysis and quantification of drugs was performed using the Xcalibur Quan Browser software (Thermo Scientific).</p>	<p>Thermo Xcalibur software version 4.0.27 (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) used for data acquisition (Q-exactive plus tune version 2.11) as well as qualitative (Qual Browser) and quantification (Quan Browser) data analysis.</p>	<p>Drug analytes were identified using authentic standards.</p>

Study Title	Cross-modal Integration of Metabolomics and Cardiac Functionality Captures Dynamic Metabotoxic Effects of Doxorubicin in Engineered Heart Tissues
Study Description	<p>Functional stem cell-derived heart models offer new avenues for preclinical, animal-free physiological assessment of drug cardiotoxicity. Yet, comprehensive molecular profiling in these models remains limited, leaving key metabolic drivers of cardiotoxicity unexplored.</p><p>Here, we leveraged an innovative platform and a topology-guided integration framework to unveil the complex dose- and time-metabolic rewiring of the central carbon metabolism caused by doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity (DiC) in human heart tissue. Through cross-modal integration of cardiac functionality and metabolomics in 3D engineered heart tissues, we identified 20 metabolites linked to cardiac contraction and differentially affected by doxorubicin exposure. Nine of them, including carnitine esters and UDP-glucuronic acid, were never before implicated in DiC and represent promising candidates for DiC metabolic rescue.</p><p>By yielding high-resolution insights into complex biological mechanisms, our platform and mathematical framework enable metabolic and functional assessment of cardiotoxicity in functional heart models, paving the way for innovative advances in preclinical drug development.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>These metabolite extracts were obtained from 3D engineered heart tissues (3D-EHTs) generated from hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (commercial cell line GM25256, Coriell Institute), as described in the original article. After extraction, samples were stored at -80 degrees Celsius till analysis.</p>	<p>The detailed protocol for polar metabolite extraction is provided in the original article, under the&nbsp;<em>Experimental Methods</em>&nbsp;section.</p>	<p><em>Triethanolamine (TEA)-based LC-MS method</em></p><p>The metabolite extracts were dissolved in 100 μL of milliQ water prior to MS analysis (Figure 1b).</p><p>For the TEA-based method, 10 μl of extracts were loaded onto an Agilent 1290 ultra-high performance liquid chromatography column connected to an Agilent 6490A tandem quadrupole mass spectrometer (UHPLC-MS/MS), operated in dynamic multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM) mode as previously reported (PMID:&nbsp;37443799).</p><p>The chromatography settings and the list of transitions for the targeted compounds are deposited at FigShare.com and are publicly available at: https://figshare.com/s/a3ec827066d3c16e109b (DOI:10.6084/m9.figshare.27985364).</p><p><br></p><p><em>Tributylamine&nbsp;(TBA)-based LC-MS method</em></p><p>For the TBA-based method, we injected 20 μl were loaded onto an Agilent 1290 ultra-high performance liquid chromatography column connected to an Agilent 6490A tandem quadrupole mass spectrometer (UHPLC-MS/MS), operated in dynamic multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, using the acquisition method previously described (PMID:&nbsp;34939087). The chromatography settings and the list of transitions for the targeted compounds are deposited at FigShare.com and are publicly available at: https://figshare.com/s/a3ec827066d3c16e109b (DOI:10.6084/m9.figshare.27985364).</p><p><br></p><p><em>Acetic acid (AA)-based LC-MS analysis</em></p><p>The metabolic extract (20 μl) was transferred to a 96-well plate and diluted with 20 μl of stable isotope solution, containing 3µM U-13C6-Citrate (Toronto Research Chemicals), 6µM U-13C5 2ketoglutarate and 3µM of U-13C4 Succinate (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories). To mix and homogenize the diluted samples the plate was centrifuged for 3 minutes at 2000 rpm at 15 degrees Celsius, and shaken for 3 minutes at 100 rpm. Then, 1 µl of each sample was injected in a Waters I-Class Acquity chromatographic system fitted with a peek-lined Inert Sustain AQ-c18 (2.1x100 mm dp 3 μ) column connected to a Waters Xevo TQSμ mass spectrometer, and run using a 0.5% (v/v) acetic acid gradient, as previously described (PMID:&nbsp;20433152).</p><p>The chromatography settings and the list of transitions for the targeted compounds are deposited at FigShare.com and are publicly available at: https://figshare.com/s/a3ec827066d3c16e109b (DOI:10.6084/m9.figshare.27985364).</p>	<p>The details regarding the acquisition methods and MS settings can be found in the article, under the&nbsp;<em>Experimental Methods</em>&nbsp;section, and in the following references:</p><p>TEA-based method: PMID:&nbsp;37443799&nbsp;(DOI:&nbsp;10.3390/cells12131765)</p><p>TBA-based method: PMID:&nbsp;34939087&nbsp;(DOI:&nbsp;10.1093/glycob/cwab106)</p><p>AA-based method: PMID:&nbsp;30653465&nbsp;(DOI:&nbsp;10.1515/hsz-2018-0446)</p>	<p>Detailed information on data normalization based on dsDNA content is provided in the original article, under the <em>Experimental Methods</em> section.</p>	<p>In this study, three previously optimized targeted metabolomics methods were applied, and compound identification was based on fragmentation.</p><p><br></p><p>Transition lists used to identify compounds measured through TEA- and TBA-based methods are provided in Supplemental File 1, deposited in FigShare (https://figshare.com/s/a3ec827066d3c16e109b ; DOI:10.6084/m9.figshare.27985364).</p><p><br></p><p>The information on the compounds detected via AA-based emethod are reported in the article by Vriend et al., 2019 (PMID:&nbsp;30653465;&nbsp;DOI:&nbsp;10.1515/hsz-2018-0446).</p>

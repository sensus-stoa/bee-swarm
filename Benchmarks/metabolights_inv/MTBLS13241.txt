Study Title	Activated ATF6α is a hepatic tumor driver restricting immunosurveillance
Study Description	<p>Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the fastest growing cause of cancer-related mortality with limited therapies. While endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-stress and the unfolded protein response (UPR) are implicated in HCC, the involvement of the UPR-transducer activating transcription factor 6 alpha (ATF6α) remains unclear. In contrast to the well-characterized role of ATF6α-activation as an adaptive response to ER-stress, we demonstrate its hitherto unknown function as an ER-stress-inducing tumor-driver and metabolic master-regulator restricting cancer-immunosurveillance for HCC. ATF6α-activation in human HCC significantly correlated with an aggressive phenotype characterized by reduced patient-survival, enhanced tumor-progression, and local immunosuppression. Hepatocyte-specific ATF6α-activation in mice induced progressive hepatitis with ER-stress, immunosuppression, and hepatocyte-proliferation. Concomitantly, activated-ATF6α increased glycolysis and directly repressed gluconeogenic enzyme fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 1 (FBP1) by binding to gene regulatory elements. Restoring FBP1 expression prevented ATF6α-activation-related pathologies. Prolonged ATF6α-activation in hepatocytes triggered hepatocarcinogenesis, intratumoral T-cell infiltration, and nutrient-deprived immune-exhaustion. Immune-checkpoint blockade (ICB) efficiently restored immunosurveillance and dramatically reduced HCC. In line, HCC patients achieving complete response to immunotherapy displayed significantly increased ATF6α-activation compared to those with a weaker response. Targeting Atf6 via germline, hepatocyte-specific ablation, or therapeutic hepatocyte delivery of antisense-oligonucleotides dampened HCC in preclinical liver-cancer models. Thus, prolonged ATF6α-activation drives ER-stress, leading to glycolysis-dependent immunosuppression in liver cancer, sensitizing to ICB. Our findings suggest persistently activated ATF6α is a tumor-driver, a potential stratification-marker for ICB response and a therapeutic target in HCC.</p>

After ultra-sonication, 250μl of molecular biology purity grade water was added. Samples were centrifuged for phase separation at 12,000 g for 10min. The layers were then manually separated, aqueous phase was transferred to 1.5ml tubes and evaporated overnight in a vacuum concentrator (Speedvac SPD300, ThermoFisher Scientific).&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>Similarly, cell culture pellets collected in 1.2ml ultrapure methanol were transferred to 2ml AFA glass tubes and subjected to a 1-phase extraction procedure. 110μl of chloroform and ultrapure water were added, and the mixtures were subjected to ultra-sonication extraction. Clear supernatant was filled into 1.7mm Bruker SampleJet-compatible NMR spectroscopy tubes. We acquired NMR spectra on a 600 MHz (proton frequency) spectrometer (Avance III HD, Bruker BioSpin, Karlsruhe, Germany) with a 1.7mm room temperature microprobe at 298K.&nbsp;</p>	<p>A short proton ZG (zero go) experiment was recorded followed by a 7min 1D NOESY (Nuclear Overhauser effect) to assess offset frequency and optimize water suppression. CPMG (Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill) experiments were used for each polar extract sample to assign and quantify metabolites by suppressing residual background signals from remaining water and macromolecules (512 scans - 1h for liver samples, 1024 scans - 2h for cell culture).</p>	<p>For NMR data: Spectra were pre-processed with Bruker TopSpin 3.6.1 software.</p><p><br></p><p>For LC-MS data: Feature analysis was conducted in MSDial 4.90 for data generated in positive or negative ionization mode. The annotations are based on an in-house library further described in the following sub-chapter.</p><p>The MS1 tolerance was set to 0.01 Da, whereas the MS2 tolerance was set to 0.025&nbsp;Da. The retention time tolerance was defined as 0.4&nbsp;min. Retention time begin was set to 0.5&nbsp;min and ended at 8 min. MS1 mass range begin was defined to be 70 Da and ended at 1050 Da. The MS/MS mass range begins at 0 Da with a range to 2000 Da. The maximum charge number was set to 1.&nbsp;Cl and Br elements were disabled. Execution of retention time corrections was disabled.</p><p>For peak detection, a maximum peak amplitude of 10000 was set. A mass slice width of 0.1&nbsp;Da was applied. Data were smoothed using a linear weighted moving average algorithm with a smoothing level set to 3 scans. The minimum peak width was defined to be 5 scans, and the exclusion mass list was disabled.</p><p>For MS2 processing, a sigma window of 0.5 was applied. The MS/MS abundance cut-off was defined to be 0. The exclusion after precursor ion was enabled. The functions ‘Keep the isotopic ions until 0.5 Da’ and ‘Keep the isotopic ions w/o MS2Dec’ were disabled.</p><p>For identification, the MSP file of the corresponding library was inserted. The retention time tolerance was defined to be 0.4 min. The accurate mass tolerance (MS1) was set to 0.01 Da, whereas the accurate mass tolerance (MS2) was set to 0.05 Da. The retention time was used for filtering as well as a scoring parameter. An identification score cut-off of 80% was applied. No additional post identification was applied.</p><p>The alignment algorithm considered the following Adducts: For negative mode [M-H]-, [M-H2O-H]-, [M+Cl]- and [M+HAc-H]- were enabled; for positive mode [M+H]+, [M+NH4]+, [M+Na]+, [M+K]+, [M+ACN+H]+ and [M+H-H2O]+ were enabled. A mid-run quality control sample was chosen as reference file for the alignment with a retention time tolerance of 0.15 min. The MS1 tolerance was set to 0.015 Da, a retention time factor of 0.5 and a MS1 factor of 0.5 was applied. The peak count filter was set to 0 %, as well as N% detected in at least one group to 0 %. The function of removing features based on blank information was disabled, and gap filling by compulsion was enabled. The Isotope tracking was disabled. The Alignment results were exported from MSDial, containing the raw data matrix with signal areas, to a .txt file, which was further processed.</p><p>Only compounds with an m/z and RT match, a minimum intensity threshold, maximal missing values in 1 of 3 quality control samples, as well as in a maximal 25 % of the measured samples, and for a maximal relative standard deviation of 25 % were kept for further analysis. Depending on the sample, normalization to cell count, mass, or volume and/or internal standard was processed. The data was uploaded to Metaboanalyst 6.0 for further statistical analysis, including pareto scaling of the data.&nbsp;</p>	<p>For NMR data: Data were annotated and quantified using ChenomX NMR suite 8.5 software. The statistics were performed by MetaboAnalyst 5.0 online platform (www.metaboanalyst.ca). Briefly, we normalized the dataset by reference sample using probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN) and performed a parametric analysis of variance (ANOVA) with adjusted p-value (FDR) cut-off of 0.05 with Fishers LSD post-hoc analysis. For the heat maps we used Euclidean distance measure with Ward clustering algorithm.</p><p><br></p><p>For LC-MS data: The metabolite identification was realized by an in-house database containing the retention time, accurate positive and negative molecule ion masses, and fragment ions occurring with a relative abundance greater than 10&nbsp;% derived by authentic reference standards. The reference standards were part of the mass spectrometry metabolite library (MSMLS) supplied by IROA Technology (Nellysford, USA). Additionally, the database contains sum formula, ion mode, precursor adduct, and further compound identifications like INCHIKEY or SMILES.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p>	<p>For Chromatography, an Agilent 1290 Infinity II Bio LC System was used (manufacturer: Agilent Inc., Waldbronn, Germany). The separation was obtained by an AdvanceBio MS Spent Media column (150 x 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm). Mobile phase A consists of 10 mM ammonium acetate in water, and phase B of 10 mM ammonium acetate in acetonitrile/water 95/5. The preparation of the mobile phases includes a pH adjustment of the 200 mM ammonium acetate stock solution, which is further diluted in the final mobile phases by a factor of 1/20.</p><p>The flow rate was set to 450 µl/min, and a linear gradient was applied, defined as follows: 0 min, 100 % B; 0.5 min, 100 % B; 6.5 min, 50&nbsp;% B; 7 min, 50 % B, and the stop time for the LC was at 7.01 min. The LC method includes a post time of 0.7 min and a needle cleaning program during the pre-run of the next sample of approximately 1 min, resulting in a total time between injections of 8.7&nbsp;min. Column temperature is set to 70 °C, and an injection volume of 1 μL is used.</p>	<p>The LC system was coupled to a Q-Exactive Plus Orbitrap mass spectrometer using a HESI II ion source (manufacturer: Thermo Scientific, Bremen, Germany). For both positive and negative ionization modes the parameters were defined as follows: spray voltage 3.5&nbsp;kV, sheath gas flow 40 a.u., aux gas flow of 15 a.u. heated to 350 °C. The MS inlet was set to a sweep gas flow of 1 a.u., S-lense RF level of 40 a.u. and a capillary temperature of 300&nbsp;°C. The MS acquisition was performed in FullScan mode from 70 and 1050 m/z with a resolution of 35000 at 200 m/z. The method used 1 microscan, a default charge of 1, and a chromatographic peak width of 12 s. AGC target and maximal injection time were set to 1e6 and 50 ms, respectively. Data-dependent MS2 acquisition was based on an inclusion list with a resolution of 17500 m/z at 200 m/z, a minimum AGC target of 8e3 and AGC target of 2e5, and a maximal injection time of 100 ms. Fragment ions were generated with a stepped NCE of 30, 50, 70, and an isolation window of 1 m/z. Dynamic exclusion of 4 s and isotope exclusion were applied with a loop count of 10. For internal mass correction, lock masses were set for 391.2842 m/z in positive and 265.1476 m/z in negative mode.&nbsp;</p>

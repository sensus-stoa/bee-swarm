Study Title	Plasma metabolomic and lipidomic signatures characteristic of treatment non-response in rheumatoid arthritis
Study Description	<p _msttexthash='40138319' _msthash='1501'>Objectives: This study aimed to investigate plasma metabolomic and lipidomic profiles in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) to identify potential biomarkers for distinguishing treatment responses.</p><p _msttexthash='40138319' _msthash='1501'>Methods: Plasma samples were collected from 106 RA patients and 10 healthy controls, with 30 RA samples selected based on predefined inclusion criteria. Using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics analysis, a total of 2,279 metabolites and 2,987 lipids were systematically identified.</p><p _msttexthash='40138319' _msthash='1501'>Results: Through rigorous statistical evaluation (variable importance in the projection (VIP) &gt; 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) &lt; 0.05), 22 metabolites and lipids were found to be positively associated with RA risk based on logistic regression analysis. These were further refined into 12 core features using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. The 12 core features identified in the treatment non-response group included lipids Cer(d18:1/16:0), PS(16:0/20:0), and Palmitic acid; metabolites Mimosine and D-Xylose; drug-related metabolites Dihydralazine, Tridihexethyl, Acyclovir monophosphate, Indoline, and Melleolide; and pollution-related metabolites Norcotinine and 2-Chloro-1-(chloromethyl)ethyl carbamate. Machine learning models utilizing these features demonstrated robust discriminatory performance, achieving area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.90, validating the efficacy of these biomarkers in differentiating treatment-responsive from non-responsive RA patients. Additionally, the identified features showed significant correlations with high disease activity, enabling differentiation across various stages of disease progression. </p><p _msttexthash='40138319' _msthash='1501'>Conclusion: The findings reveal significant metabolic and lipidomic alterations in RA and highlight the identified biomarkers as effective indicators of treatment responses and potential therapeutic targets.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Peripheral blood samples were collected from 30 patients with RA and 10 HC. All RA patients fulfilled the diagnostic criteria established by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) or the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) guidelines. All RA participants were receiving treatment with at least one DMARD and fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: age ≥18 years and a disease duration of at least 6 weeks. Exclusion criteria for RA patients included: (1) a diagnosis of any autoimmune disease other than RA, (2) a history of severe chronic infections or the presence of any current infection, (3) diagnosis of cancer, (4) pregnancy or lactation. HC were required to meet the inclusion criteria of being ≥18 years old with normal results from recent liver and kidney function tests. The exclusion criteria for healthy controls were: (1) a diagnosis of any autoimmune disease, (2) a history of severe chronic infections or the presence of any current infection, (3) diagnosis of cancer, and (4) pregnancy or lactation. Venous blood samples were obtained from all participants in the morning prior to breakfast. Following collection, blood samples were centrifuged to isolate plasma, which was stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Details of the cohort can be found in Table 1 of the paper associated with this study.</p>	<p>Samples were slowly thawed at 4°C. An appropriate amount of sample was taken and mixed with 4 volumes of pre-cooled methanol/water solution (2:1, v/v), followed by vortexing for 30 seconds. Then, 8 volumes of MTBE (methyl tert-butyl ether) solution were added, vortexed for 30 seconds, and subjected to low-temperature ultrasonication for 30 minutes. After centrifugation at 14,000 g and 4°C for 10 minutes, the upper layer of the stratified liquid was transferred to a new EP tube, dried under vacuum at room temperature, and stored at -80°C.</p><p>Preparation of quality control (QC) samples: Equal volumes of all samples were pooled to prepare QC samples. The QC samples were processed in parallel using the above-described method, lyophilized, and stored at low temperature for later use.</p>	<p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='416'>Positive ion mode (POS):</p><p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='416'>Mobile phase A: 10 mM ammonium formate in acetonitrile:water (6:4, v/v) with 0.1% formic acid.</p><p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='416'>Mobile phase B: 10 mM ammonium formate in isopropanol:acetonitrile (9:1, v/v) with 0.1% formic acid.</p><p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='416'>Negative ion mode (NEG):</p><p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='416'>Mobile phase A: 10 mM ammonium formate in acetonitrile:water (6:4, v/v) with 0.1% formic acid.</p><p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='416'>Mobile phase B: 10 mM ammonium formate in isopropanol:acetonitrile (9:1, v/v) with 0.1% formic acid.</p><p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='416'>Samples were separated using a micro-liter flow ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system, Nexera UHPLC LC-30A. First, the chromatographic column was equilibrated with 70% mobile phase A. Subsequently, samples were delivered to an Acclaim C30 liquid chromatography column (Waters, Acclaim C30, 2.1×100 mm) via an autosampler, with a flow rate of 0.26 mL/min.</p><p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='416'>The elution gradient was as follows: starting with 30% mobile phase B, then increasing mobile phase B from 30% to 100% over the next 20 minutes and maintaining 100% mobile phase B for 5 minutes of elution. After that, it was adjusted back to 30% mobile phase B within 0.1 minutes, followed by a 2.9-minute washing period at 30% mobile phase B.</p>	<p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='505'>After separation by the analytical column, the samples were analyzed using a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer.</p><p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='505'>For full-scan MS (MS¹), the mass-to-charge (m/z) range for precursor ions was 200–2000, with a resolution of 120,000, an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1e6, and a maximum ion injection time (IT) of 100 ms.</p><p _msttexthash='32059222' _msthash='505'>For tandem MS (MS²), the resolution was 15,000, with an AGC target of 2e5 and a maximum IT of 80 ms. Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) was used for fragmentation, with normalized collision energies (NCE) set at 20, 40, and 60. The isolation window was 1.5 m/z, and the m/z range for product ions was 200–2000.</p>	<p>The raw mass spectrometry data (RAW files) generated by the Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer were first converted into open-format files using Thermo Fisher Xcalibur software to ensure compatibility with downstream analysis platforms. This conversion step retained key information such as retention time, m/z values, and ion intensities while standardizing the data structure.</p><p>Subsequently, the converted data were imported into Progenesis QI software for further preprocessing.</p>	<p>After data preprocessing in Progenesis QI, metabolic features were extracted. For each feature, the software performed database searching to match experimental data with reference metabolite information. A mass deviation tolerance of 5 ppm was set for accurate mass matching to ensure reliability.</p>

Study Title	Food Deprivation Enhances Disease Resistance: Underlying Mechanisms in Oysters Confronted with Pacific Oyster Mortality Syndrome
Study Description	<p>Variation in food availability can shape host susceptibility and pathogen virulence, thereby impacting disease outcomes. An ad libitum diet may enhance host physiology and immunity but it can also favor pathogen proliferation. Here, we investigated how food availability (ad libitum vs. starvation) affects interactions between the oyster Magallana gigas and the herpesvirus OsHV-1 µVar initiating the panzootic Pacific Oyster Mortality Syndrome (POMS). We found that starvation reduced oyster susceptibility to POMS. Through a comparative integrative-omics approach, we showed that (1) starvation induces metabolic rate depression, which may limit viral replication by reducing the availability of cellular energy that viruses can hijack, and (2) enhances autophagy and antiviral responses, thereby improving viral control. These results underscore the importance of nutritional status in shaping viral disease outcomes and may inform management strategies in aquaculture systems.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Animal</strong></p><p>All animal experiments used&nbsp;in this study did not require ethical approval from a human subject or animal welfare committee. However, they were conducted in strict accordance with the IFREMER’s guidelines for animal care and use.On 11th May 2020, two first-generation (G1) families of Magallana gigas (hereafter referred to as F11N and F14R) were produced through full-sib mating, using two G0 families. Parents of the G0 were wild oysters sampled in Dellec, and both G0 families (F11 and F14) were found highly susceptible to POMS as reported in 44. F11N and F14R were transferred from the hatchery (Bouin, France) to the Ifremer experimental station in Argenton (France), placed in tanks at 23°C, where they were fed ad libitum (1,500 µm3.µL-1) with a microalgae mixture composed of Tisochrysis lutea and Skeletonema costatum at equivalent biomass (50/50) 79. The ‘pathogen-free’ status was established for the F11N and F14R families by confirming the absence of OsHV-1 µVar DNA in tissue samples using qPCR, and by verifying the presence of Vibrio at low concentrations (a maximum of 9 Vibrio colony-forming units [CFU] per milligram of live oyster tissue), based on enumeration on TCBS agar as previously described80. We refer to these two families as “recipients”.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>When three-months old (09th August 2020), recipients were blindly divided into six 400-L tanks, each containing 277.7 g weight of F14R (N=400) and 157.2 g weight of F11N (N=383). While three tanks were maintained on an ad libitum food supply, the three others had their food supply stopped to induce the starved condition 30,31,33. A constant temperature of 23°C was maintained 16,81 (Figure 1A).</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>Donors were five-month-old “pathogen-free” oysters coming from two mass spawning with high (non-infectious environment, Rade de Brest, Dellec) and moderate (genetically diversified standardized oyster 81) susceptibility phenotype to POMS. Genetically susceptible diversified donors were used to maximize OsHV-1 µVar shedding.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Infection in mesocosm through cohabitation</strong></p><p>Recipients were acclimated to these environmental conditions for 16 days. Phytoplankton concentration was measured twice daily at the outlet of the tank to ensure a proper homogenization throughout the entire tank using an electronic particle counter (Beckman Coulter counter Multisizer 3 equipped with a 100-μm aperture tube). Each tank was equipped with temperature loggers (a WTW MULTIPARAMETRE MultiLine®&nbsp;3630 IDS; a Dotsman P750 with captor PT100 sensor; an iButton® DS1922L-F5 thermocrons®) to monitor temperature during the whole experiment. The pH, salinity and dissolved oxygen were also monitored with the WTW MULTIPARAMETRE MultiLine®&nbsp;3630 IDS (Table S10). During the 16 days of acclimation, water was renewed every day and oysters were rinsed to remove sedimented feces. To quantify oyster growth rates, individuals were weighted before and after the acclimation period. No mortalities were observed during the 16-days acclimation.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>Following the acclimation, a protocol of cohabitation between donor and recipient oysters was performed to mimic the natural infectious process of POMS disease.</p><p>Twenty hours prior the start of cohabitation, donor oysters were anaesthetized with hexahydrate MgCl2 (ACROS, 50 g·L−1, 200 g of oysters·L−1) for 2 hours 82 and 100 µL of a OsHV-1 µVar inoculum was injected into their adductor muscle using a 26-gauge needle inserted in a multi-dispensing hand pipette 83. This inoculum (2.2·10^5 genomic copies·µL-1) was prepared from OsHV-1 µVar-infected oyster tissue homogenates (oysters dead in Marenne Oléron bay in July 2020), following the protocol described by 83. Upon injection, donors remained at 23°C for 18 hours before cohabitation to ensure intense viral shedding (Figure 1A).</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>A weight ratio of 1:1 between donors and recipients was applied and donors were transferred into each tank containing acclimated recipients, marking the start of the cohabitation. To create an optimal environment for infectious process, flow rates were stopped during the first 125 hours post-cohabitation (hpc). A pool of 10 live recipients per tank and family (hereafter called “sample”) were blindly sampled at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 and 72 hpc (Figure 1A). Soft tissues were shucked from the shells and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen before storage at -80°C. We monitored mortality twice daily until no additional deaths were observed in all tanks for 48 hours. Dead recepients were promptly removed from tanks and disposed of.&nbsp;</p>	<p>I. Sample Extraction</p><p>Oyster tissue (20 mg) was promptly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen upon collection and transferred to 1.5 mL polypropylene tubes with screw caps (cat. No. 72.687, Sarstedt). 980µL of ice-cold methanol (cat. No. 0013684102BS, Biosolve), supplemented with 5 µL of 2-(N-Morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (Cat. No. M8250, Sigma-Aldrich) and 20 µL of mixed PUFAs ISTD was added to the tubes along with 0.5 g of glass beads. Samples were vortexed and homogenized using the Precellys 24 tissue homogenizer (speed 4 m/s, 2 cycles of 15 s each). Subsequently, the samples were agitated at 4°C for 1 hour, followed by removal of debris via centrifugation at 20,000 g (4°C) for 10 minutes. The resulting supernatant was transferred into 15 mL tubes.</p><p><br></p><p>II. Separation and Purification via SPE Column</p><p>Strata-X 33 mm SPE Columns (10 mg) (cat. 8B-S100-AAK, Phenomenex) was mounted on a SPE manifold for separation and purification. Purification was proceed as followed : </p><p>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Washing and conditioning.</p><p>a.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add 1 mL of methanol.</p><p>b.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add 1 mL of 0.1% formic acid in UPLC.</p><p>c.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Discard the wash solutions and proceed rapidly to step 13.</p><p>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;II.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sample loading.</p><p>a.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Gently agitate the sample before loading onto the SPE column.</p><p>b.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add the sample stepwise by loading 1 mL onto the SPE column.</p><p>c.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keep the filtrate.</p><p>&nbsp;III.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rinsing.</p><p>a.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add 1 mL of 0.1% formic acid in UPLC.</p><p>b.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add 1 mL of 15% ethanol in UPLC water, and let it run completely through the column (proceed quickly to step IV. below).</p><p>c.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Discard the filtrate.</p><p>&nbsp;IV.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Elution.</p><p>a.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add 500 mL of methanol.</p><p>b.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Collect the eluate in a glass tube.</p><p>c.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Repeat steps IV.a. and IV.b. for a total of 1 mL.</p><p>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;V.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Divide the filtrate from step II.c. in three distinct tubes to proceed with GM and NUCs analysis with one fraction, PUFAs analysis with another one and TCA analysis with the last one.</p><p>&nbsp;VI.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dry samples in a SpeedVac.</p><p><br></p>	<p>III. GM Analysis</p><p>Samples were reconstitute in 200 µL of UPLC water and transferred in LC-MS polypropylene vials. Chromatographic analysis was performed using UHPLC 1290 Infinity II with an HPLC Discovery® HS F5, 3 μm particle size, L × I.D. 15 cm × 2.1 mm (cat. No. 567503-U, Sigma-Aldrich). The mobile phase A consisted 0.1% formic acid (cat. No. 33015, Sigma-Aldrich) in UPLC water (cat. No. 0023214102BS, Biosolve), and mobile phase B consisted in 0.1% formic acid in UPLC Acetonitrile (cat. No. 0001207802BS, Biosolve). The gradient used was: 0% B, 0.00 minute; 0% B, 2.00 minutes; 25% B, 5.00 minutes; 35% B, 11.00 minutes; 95% B, 15.00 minutes; 95% B, 20.00 minutes; 0% B, 20.10 minutes; 0% B, 25.00 minutes. The column temperature was set at 40°C, flow rate 0.250 mL/min, and volume of injection 0.5 μL for each run.</p><p><br></p><p>IV. NUCs Analysis</p><p>Analysis was made on the same samples as the GM Analysis. Chromatographic analysis was performed using UHPLC 1290 Infinity II with an InfinityLab Poroshell HPH-C18, 2.7 μm particle size, L × I.D. 100 cm × 3.1 mm (cat. No. 695975-502, Agilent). The mobile phase A consisted 10mM ammonium acetate (cat. No. 5.33004, Sigma-Aldrich) , 2.5 μM medronic acid (cat. No. 5191-3940, Agilent), 0.1% formic acid in UPLC water (cat. No. 0023214102BS, Biosolve), and mobile phase B consisted in 90% UPLC methanol (cat. No. 0013684102BS, Biosolve), 10%&nbsp;UPLC water, 2.5 μM medronic acid, 0.1% formic acid. The gradient used was: 2% B, 0.00 minute; 2% B, 1.00 minutes; 42% B, 4.00 minutes; 100% B, 5.00 minutes; 100% B, 6.00 minutes; 2% B, 6.50 minutes; 0% B, 8.00 minutes. The column temperature was set at 30°C, flow rate 0.400 mL/min, and volume of injection 3 μL for each run.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p>V. PUFAs Analysis</p><p>Samples were reconstitute in 50 µL methanol then transferred into glass HPLC vial. Chromatographic analysis was performed using UHPLC 1290 Infinity II with a Kinetex C8, 2.6 μm particle size, L × I.D. 150 cm × 2.1 mm (cat. No. 00F-4497-AN, Phenomenex). The mobile phase A consisted 0.1% formic acid (cat. No. 33015, Sigma-Aldrich) in UPLC water (cat. No. 0023214102BS, Biosolve), and mobile phase B consisted in 0.1% formic acid in UPLC Acetonitrile (cat. No. 0001207802BS, Biosolve). The gradient used was: 10% B, 0.00 minute; 25% B, 5.00 minutes; 35% B, 10.00 minutes; 75% B, 20.00 minutes; 95% B, 20.10 minutes; 95% B, 28.00 minutes; 10% B, 28.10 minutes; 10% B, 30.00 minutes. The column temperature was set at 40°C, flow rate 0.250mL/min, and volume of injection 6 μL for each run.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>VI. TCA Analysis</p><p>Samples were reconstitute in 200 µL of UPLC methanol/UPLC Water (80:20) and 10 µL of d27-Myristic acid (cat. No. 400505, Agilent) at 75 µg/mL was added and transferred into brown crimp vial with insert. Samples were then dried again by speedvac. Derivatization were proceed by a PAL autosampler and was proceed as follow : 90 µL of Methoxyamine hydrochloride (cat. No. 89803, Sigma-Aldrich) at 20g/L were added then samples were incubate at 30°C for 90 minutes. 10 µL of MSTFA (cat. No. TS-48915, Thermo Scientific) were added then incubate at 37°C for 30 minutes. Chromatographic analysis was performed using a 8890 GC chromatographic system with a DB-MS +DG, 0.25 μm film thickness, L × I.D. 30 m × 0.250 mm (cat. No. 122-5532G, Agilent) column. 1 µl of the prepared sample was injected to the gas chromatograph inlet in the split mode 10:1 with the inlet temperature held at 250°C. Helium is used as the carrier gas (1.1 ml/min). The GC is set to an initial temperature of 60°C for 1 min, followed by a 60°C/min ramp to 120°C, 30°C/min ramp to 250°C, and a third ramp of 60°C/min to 325°C, followed by a final hold time of 5 min.</p>	<p>VII. GM Analysis</p><p>General metabolites profiling analysis was performed on a 6495 LC/TQ mass system and the Agilent MassHunter workstation (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) with the dynamic MRM scan mode. Source-parameter settings in the positive or negative modes were as follows. The capillary voltage was set at 4 kV for positive and negative mode, Gas temperature at 250°C, Gas flow at 12 L/min, Nebulizer at 35 psi, Sheath Gas Temperature at 400 °C and the Sheath Gas Flow at 12 L/min.</p><p><br></p><p>VIII. NUCs Analysis</p><p>Nucleotides metabolites profiling analysis was performed on a 6495 LC/TQ mass system and the Agilent MassHunter workstation (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) with the dynamic MRM scan mode. Source-parameter settings in the positive or negative modes were as follows. The capillary voltage was set at 3.5 kV for positive and negative mode, Gas temperature at 120°C, Gas flow at 11 L/min, Nebulizer at 40 psi, Sheath Gas Temperature at 400 °C and the Sheath Gas Flow at 12 L/min.</p><p><br></p><p>IX. PUFAs Analysis</p><p>PUFAs metabolites profiling analysis was performed on a 6495 LC/TQ mass system and the Agilent MassHunter workstation (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) with the dynamic MRM scan mode. Source-parameter settings in the positive or negative modes were as follows. The capillary voltage was set at 4 kV for positive and negative mode, Gas temperature at 250°C, Gas flow at 12 L/min, Nebulizer at 35 psi, Sheath Gas Temperature at 400 °C and the Sheath Gas Flow at 12 L/min.</p><p><br></p><p>X. TCA Analysis</p><p>Krebs cycle metabolites profiling analysis was performed on a 5977B MSD6495 mass system and the Agilent MassHunter workstation (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) with the dynamic MRM scan mode. Source temperature was set at 250°C and Electron energy at 70eV.</p>	<p>Preprocessing </p><p>For each metabolite detected from the targeted metabolomic analysis (GM, nucleotides, PUFAs, and the Krebs cycle), the measured values were expressed as peak area counts and normalized to their respective internal standards (ISTD). The data processing was performed using the {MetaboAnalystR} package (v4.0.0) with the R version v4.3.2. To minimize detection biases, a filtering step based on the interquartile range (IQR) was applied, removing the lowest 10% of the data. After filtering, quantile normalization was performed, followed by a logarithmic transformation (base 10). The normalized data were used for subsequent analyses.</p>	<p>In this study, authors performed targeted metabolomics, enabling to identify specific compounds based on their retention time and their mass to charge.</p>

Study Title	Untargeted metabolomics reveals metabolic mechanism of degrading chicken feathers by Acrophialophora levis
Study Description	<p>Chicken feathers constitute a major waste in poultry processing due to their resistance to degradation. Microbial degradation offers a promising solution for enhancing the utilization of feather waste. However, the metabolic mechanisms underlying microbial feather degradation remain poorly understood. In this study, <em>Acrophialophora levis</em> QHDZ1-2 was used as the research subject. Untargeted metabolomics was employed to analyze the metabolites produced by <em>A. levis</em> QHDZ1-2 cultured in feather meal medium compared to a control medium, aiming to elucidate its metabolic pathways involved in feather degradation. The results revealed that amino acids, peptides and analogues formed the most abundant group (82 metabolites). Notably, Glutathione disulfide was identified as a key metabolite associated with feather degradation. Carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates accounted for 40 metabolites. Carbohydrate-related metabolites, like cyclic GMP-AMP, participated in signaling during degradation. Fourteen metabolic pathways were significantly enriched. Among these, purine metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, and nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism appeared to constitute an upstream “reducing-power supply axis”, providing cofactors and reducing equivalents to support downstream redox reactions and thereby promote the breakdown of feather keratin. In summary, this study provides insights into the metabolic mechanism of <em>A. levis </em>QHDZ1-2 in chicken feather degradation through untargeted metabolomics. The findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the keratin-rich substrate degradation by filamentous fungi and offer a theoretical foundation for further applications in waste valorization.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><em>Acrophialophora levis</em> QHDZ1-2 was used in this study. The strain was preserved at the Fungal Resources Institute of Guizhou University (GZAC). For metabolomics, <em>A. levis</em> QHDZ1-2 was cultivated on two different solid media, the feather fermentation medium and the control medium. All media were sterilized at 121 °C for 30 min before use.</p><p><em>A. levis</em> QHDZ1-2 was inoculated separately onto the control medium (sucrose and sodium nitrate as carbon and nitrogen sources) and the feather fermentation medium (feather meal as both carbon and nitrogen sources). The cultures were incubated at 40 °C for 7 days. Mycelia were scraped from both solid media, with three independent biological replicates collected for each condition. For each replicate, approximately 300 mg of mycelia were harvested, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until metabolite analysis.</p>	<p>Approximately 100 μL of each sample were transferred into 1.5 mL EP tubes. Then 300 μL of methanol (LC–MS grade) and 20 μL of an internal standard solution were added, followed by vortex mixing for 30 s. The mixtures were sonicated for 10 min in an ice-water bath to enhance metabolite extraction. After sonication, the samples were incubated at −20 °C for 1 h to precipitate proteins. The samples were then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C.</p><p>After centrifugation, 200 μL of the supernatant from each tube was carefully transferred into LC–MS vials. To prepare a pooled quality control (QC) sample, 20 μL of supernatant from each biological sample was combined and mixed thoroughly. An aliquot of 200 μL from each individual sample was used for LC–MS analysis.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS UPLC system equipped with an Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm). The column was operated under reversed-phase conditions.</p><p>For both positive and negative ionization modes, the mobile phases consisted of:</p><p> • Mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water</p><p> • Mobile phase B: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile</p><p>The flow rate was set to 400 μL/min and the injection volume was 1 μL. The gradient elution program was as follows (time in minutes, %A/%B):</p><p> 0.00–0.25 min, 98% A / 2% B;</p><p> 0.25–10.00 min, linear gradient to 2% A / 98% B;</p><p> 10.00–13.00 min, 2% A / 98% B;</p><p> 13.10–15.00 min, re-equilibration at 98% A / 2% B.</p><p>The total run time for each injection was 15 min.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection was carried out on a Waters Xevo G2-XS QTof high-resolution mass spectrometer coupled to the Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS UPLC system. Data were acquired in electrospray ionization (ESI) mode in both positive and negative ionization.</p><p>The instrument was operated in MSe mode under the control of MassLynx V4.2 software (Waters). In each acquisition cycle, low- and high-collision-energy spectra were collected in parallel. The low collision energy was set to 2 V, whereas the high collision energy was ramped from 10 to 40 V. The scan frequency was 0.2 s per spectrum.</p><p>Typical ESI source parameters were as follows: capillary voltage 2000 V in positive-ion mode and −1500 V in negative-ion mode; cone voltage 30 V; source temperature 150 °C; desolvation gas temperature 500 °C; cone gas flow 50 L/h; desolvation gas flow 800 L/h.</p>	<p>Raw LC–MS data acquired with MassLynx V4.2 were imported into Progenesis QI software for preprocessing. The workflow included peak detection, peak alignment, deconvolution and integration to generate a data matrix of feature intensities for all detected metabolites across samples.</p><p>Total ion current (TIC) normalization was applied, in which the peak area of each metabolite in a given sample was divided by the total ion current of that sample to correct for systematic variation and make the metabolite intensities comparable between runs.</p>	<p>Putative metabolite identification was performed in Progenesis QI using the online METLIN database and an in-house spectral library provided by the service provider. Detected features were matched based on accurate mass and, where available, MS/MS fragmentation patterns. Additional annotation was carried out by comparing spectral information with entries in the KEGG, HMDB and LipidMaps databases. Theoretical fragment matching was used to support the assignments, and mass deviations for identified metabolites were within 100 ppm.</p>

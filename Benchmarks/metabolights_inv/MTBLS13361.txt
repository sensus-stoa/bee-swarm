Study Title	Untargeted Metabolomics Reveals Serum Metabolic Differences and Key Biomarkers Associated with Growth Traits in Male and Female Macrobrachium rosenbergii of Different Sizes
Study Description	<p>Macrobrachium rosenbergii is one of the most economically important crustaceans globally, but significant individual growth differences persist even under the same genetic background and rearing conditions, limiting the sustainability of its aquaculture industry. Metabolomics, as a powerful tool to characterize the terminal products of biological metabolism, can directly reflect the correlation between metabolic status and phenotypic traits. In this study, male and female M. rosenbergii from a single family were divided into three size groups (large, medium, and small) based on body weight: large males (ML, 64.2±1.48 g), medium males (MM, 40.8±0.90 g), small males (MS, 20.3±0.70 g); large females (FL, 61.4±1.46 g), medium females (FM, 43.6±0.85 g), small females (FS, 23.2±0.44 g). Serum samples were analyzed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based untargeted metabolomics to identify differential metabolites (DMs), enrich metabolic pathways, and explore correlations with phenotypic traits, intestinal microbiota, and gonadal differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) showed clear separation among different size groups, indicating distinct serum metabolic profiles. A total of 37 DMs were screened with VIP&nbsp;1 and P &lt; 0.05, including amino acids, carbohydrates, lipids, and hormones. Specifically, L-tyrosine, phosphorylcholine, succinic acid, and acetylcholine were highly abundant in large-sized males, while oleic acid, L-ribulose, sucrose, and 17α-estradiol were enriched in large-sized females. In contrast, 4-hydroxycinnamic acid, equol, mannose 6-phosphate, and gluconic acid were more abundant in small-sized females. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these DMs were significantly enriched in pathways related to growth and metabolism, such as 'amino acid biosynthesis', 'protein digestion and absorption', 'glycerophospholipid metabolism', and 'actin cytoskeleton regulation'. Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated that key DMs (e.g., acetylcholine, L-tyrosine, 17α-estradiol) were strongly positively correlated with growth-related phenotypic traits (body weight, carapace weight, body length, etc.). Additionally, significant correlations were observed between DMs and dominant intestinal microbiota (e.g., Lactococcus, Rhodobacter, Bacteroides) as well as gonadal DEGs (e.g., mhc, tpnc3, GHSR), constructing a multi-level regulatory network involved in M. rosenbergii growth. Our findings uncover the metabolic mechanisms underlying growth differences in M. rosenbergii and identify potential metabolic biomarkers for growth traits, providing valuable theoretical basis and technical support for molecular breeding to improve growth performance of this species.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Macrobrachium rosenbergii used in the experiment were from a single family of Jiangsu Shufeng Aquatic Seed Industry Co., Ltd. (consistent genetic background). After gender identification via male genital pores, the shrimp were divided into three size groups based on body weight: males (64.2±1.48 g, 40.8±0.90 g, 20.3±0.70 g) and females (61.4±1.46 g, 43.6±0.85 g, 23.2±0.44 g). Prior to sampling, phenotypic traits such as body weight and body length were measured. Hemolymph was aseptically extracted, allowed to coagulate on ice, and centrifuged at 6000 rpm and 4 for 15 min to separate serum. The serum was aliquoted into sterile tubes (0.2 mL per tube) and stored at -80 without repeated freeze-thaw cycles for subsequent LC-MS metabolomic analysis.</p>	<p>Metabolite extraction: Serum samples stored at -80 were thawed at 4 and vortexed for 1 minute to mix well; 400 μL methanol was added and vortexed for 1 minute, then centrifuged at 12000 rpm (cryogenic centrifugation) for 10 minutes. The supernatant was collected and concentrated to dryness, then re-dissolved with 150 μL of 4 ppm 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine (prepared with 80% methanol-water, stored at 4, used as internal standard). After re-centrifugation, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane, and the filtrate was used for LC-MS analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>Control samples: Internal standard (2-chloro-L-phenylalanine) was included; quality control (QC) samples were set, and substances with RSD &gt; 30% in QC samples were filtered out during data quality control; LC-MS grade methanol, acetonitrile, etc., were used as solvents to avoid solvent blank interference.</p>	<p>Chromatographic analysis was performed using a Vanquish liquid chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA)</p><p>Mobile phase settings: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile-0.1% formic acid in water (positive ion mode), and acetonitrile-5 mM ammonium formate in water (negative ion mode); flow rate was 0.25 mL/min, column temperature was 40, and injection volume was 2 μL</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted using an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA);the ionization modes included positive and negative ion modes, with data collected separately;</p>	<p>Data transformation workflow: First, Proteowizard software (v3.0.8789) was used to convert raw mass spectrometry files into mzXML format; then, the R language XCMS package was applied to perform peak detection, filtering, and alignment on the mzXML-format data, and the LOESS signal correction method was used for data calibration to reduce systematic errors; finally, during data quality control, substances with a relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt; 30% in quality control (QC) samples were filtered out to complete data cleaning and transformation, providing standardized data for subsequent metabolite identification and differential analysis.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification workflow: First, the raw mass spectrometry files were converted to mzXML format using Proteowizard software, then peak detection, filtering, alignment, and LOESS signal correction were performed using the R language XCMS package; metabolite annotation was conducted based on three public databases (HMDB, LipidMaps, KEGG) with the identification parameter set to ppm &lt; 30; during data quality control, substances with a relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt; 30% in quality control (QC) samples were filtered out to ensure identification reliability. Subsequent visualization and functional analysis of differential metabolites were carried out using the R language Ropls package (PLS-DA analysis), Pheatmap package (hierarchical clustering), as well as the MetaboAnalyst package and KEGG Mapper tool (pathway enrichment).</p>

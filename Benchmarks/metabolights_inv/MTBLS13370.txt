Study Title	Mongolian medicine Wulanwendusu-11 ameliorates myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury in association with the modulation of intestinal microbiota and associated metabolic pathways
Study Description	<p>Abstract</p><p>Objective: Wulanwendusu-11 (WLWDS-11), a traditional Mongolian medicinal formula, is commonly used for the treatment of Cardiovascular diseases. However, its therapeutic material basis and in vivo metabolic profile in the context of chronic myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) remain to be elucidated.</p><p>Methods: C57BL/6J mice were randomized into six experimental groups: MIRI model, sham surgery, and treatment groups for compound Danshen dripping pills (CDDP) plus three dosages of WLWDS-11 (denoted WLWDS-11-L, WLWDS-11-M, and WLWDS-11-H). General physiological indicators of mice in each group were observed, body weight, myocardial structure and pathological features were assessed by electrocardiogram, plasma cardiac enzyme levels. Immunohistochemical staining was used to detect the pathological changes in the heart. Immunofluorescence assay was used to detect the degree of apoptosis. qRT-PCR and Western blot were used to detect the expression of COX4I1, NDUFB8, SDHA, TFAM, RIPK1, RIPK3, MLKL and TNF-α. Metabolomics and metagenomics were used to analyze treatment effects on intestinal microbiota and metabolites.</p><p>Results: WLWDS-11 (especially in high dose) can significantly improve the cardiac function, reduce the area of myocardial infarction and weaken apoptosis and fibrosis in MIRI mice. Metabolomic profiling identified 1,632 qualitative secondary metabolites, 13 annotated secondary metabolites, 125 annotated tertiary metabolites, and 296 annotated quaternary metabolites. The top 10 differential metabolites included lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI) 18:2, phlorobenzophenone, LPI 20:4, and 2-arachidonoyl-1-stearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine. Pathway screening was conducted to identify the key pathways most closely associated with metabolic differences, which included arginine and proline metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, and phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis.&nbsp;</p><p>Metagenomic results showed that the sham and WLWDS-11-H groups were enriched for the RNA polymerase (KEGG pathway identifier: ko03020), alanine, aspartic acid, and glutamate metabolism (ko00250), and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (ko00970) pathways. Correlation hierarchical cluster analysis of differential flora and metabolites showed that Kosakonia, Constantimarinum, and Syncephalis genera were associated with the metabolites 1-myristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine and batatasin III, and had a strong correlation with 1-O-hexadecyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine. At the species level, three Helicobacter spp. were strongly associated with metabolites such as perfluorobutanoic acid, Ile-Leu, Leu-Ile, and nanaomycin E. Multiomics integration analysis showed that MIRI caused intestinal flora imbalance (such as Prevost enrichment) and serum metabolism disorder (such as accumulation of oxidized lipids and lysophospholipids, arginine/proline and unsaturated fatty acid metabolism disorder). The intervention of WLWDS-11 effectively reshaped the intestinal microbial community and made the metabolic spectrum return to normal. Correlation and network analysis confirmed the correlation between specific intestinal bacteria (such as Prevost, Kosakonia and Helicobacter) and host metabolites, and formed a flora-metabolite axis regulated by WLWDS-11. KEGG pathway analysis further confirmed the effects of the treatment on key pathways, including necrotizing apoptosis and oxidative phosphorylation. From the point of view of mechanism, WLWDS-11 reversed the mitochondrial dysfunction induced by MIRI by up-regulating the expressions of COX4I 1, NDUFB8, SDHA and TFAM. By inhibiting the RIPK 1/RIPK 3/MLKL pathway and TNF-α, necrotizing apoptosis and inflammatory response are inhibited. These results suggest that WLWDS-11 may protect MIRI's heart by regulating the metabolic pathway of flora. These findings identify WLWDS-11 as a potential candidate drug and provide a molecular mechanistic basis for the clinical treatment of MIRI. Conclusion: WLWDS-11 positively reshaped the gut microbial environment by suppressing pathogenic bacteria and promoting beneficial strains, thereby fostering eubiosis, attenuating cardiac pathology, and ultimately conferring cardioprotection.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Serum samples for untargeted metabolomics analysis were obtained from male C57BL/6J mice (6-8 weeks old). After a one-week acclimatization period, the mice were randomly assigned to sham, myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury model, and various treatment groups. All treatment groups received their respective drugs or vehicle via daily oral gavage for four weeks. The MIRI model was established 12 hours after the final administration by ligating the left anterior descending coronary artery for 30 minutes followed by reperfusion. The sham group underwent thoracotomy without ligation. Serum samples were collected on day 7 after reperfusion. Blood was drawn from the posterior-orbital venous plexus and centrifuged at room temperature at 15,000 × g for 20 minutes. The supernatant was then collected and subjected to a second centrifugation step at 4°C and 15,000 × g for another 20 minutes to obtain the final serum for analysis. The acquired serum samples were immediately aliquoted and stored at -80°C until metabolomic analysis. For the untargeted metabolomics study, serum from 6 randomly selected mice per group was used. For metabolite extraction, 50 μL of serum was mixed with 200 μL of extraction solution (methanol/acetonitrile = 1:1, containing internal standards) and processed using a Starlid workstation.</p>	<p>Prior to analysis, serum samples stored at -80°C were thawed on ice. Automated metabolite extraction was performed using a Starlid workstation. Precisely 50 μL of each serum sample was aliquoted and mixed with 200 μL of ice-cold extraction solution (a 1:1 v/v mixture of methanol and acetonitrile, containing a mixture of internal standards). The mixture was vortexed vigorously at 750 rpm for 5 minutes to ensure complete protein precipitation. This was followed by incubation at room temperature for 5 minutes. Finally, the mixture was filtered through a Manhage protein precipitation plate, and the resulting filtrate, containing the metabolites, was collected for subsequent instrumental analysis. To ensure the quality and reproducibility of the LC-MS data, several types of quality control and control samples were prepared and analyzed: Quality Control Sample: A pooled QC sample was generated by combining equal volumes of the filtrate from individual animal samples within each experimental group. This pooled QC sample was injected repeatedly throughout the analytical sequence to monitor instrument stability, assess retention time drift, and for data normalization. Process Blank: A solvent blank, consisting of the pure extraction solution (MeOH/MeCN=1:1 with internal standards) without serum, was processed identically to the real samples. This was used to identify background contamination originating from solvents, reagents, or the preparation process. Internal Standards: The internal standards included in the extraction solution were used to evaluate extraction efficiency and to correct for instrumental response variations during data pre-processing.</p>	<p>Metabolomic analysis was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry platform. The chromatography system was a Vanquish Flex UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data acquisition was controlled by Xcalibur 4.4 software. Metabolites were separated on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). The mobile phase consisted of A (25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide in water) and B (acetonitrile). A gradient elution program was employed at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The autosampler temperature was maintained at 4°C, and the injection volume was 2 μL.Mass spectrometric detection was conducted using an electrospray ionization source operating in positive/negative polarity switching mode to acquire both full-scan MS and MS/MS data. The key parameters were set as follows: sheath gas flow, 50 Arb; auxiliary gas flow, 15 Arb; capillary temperature, 320°C. The full MS scan resolution was set to 60,000, and the MS/MS scan resolution was 15,000. The spray voltage was set to +3.8 kV in positive mode and -3.4 kV in negative mode. Data-dependent acquisition used a stepped normalized collision energy of 20, 30, and 40 eV.</p>	<p>Untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry platform. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). Mass spectrometric detection was carried out using a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 120 quadrupole-Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometer equipped with an Electrospray Ionization source. Data acquisition was conducted in both positive and negative ion switching mode to achieve broad metabolome coverage. The key mass spectrometric parameters were set as follows: sheath gas flow, 50 Arb; auxiliary gas flow, 15 Arb; capillary temperature, 320 °C. The spray voltage was set to +3.8 kV in positive mode and -3.4 kV in negative mode. The full MS scan resolution was set to 60,000, and the MS/MS scan resolution was 15,000. Data-dependent acquisition used a stepped normalized collision energy of 20, 30, and 40 eV.</p>	<p>The raw LC-MS/MS data obtained in this study were processed and analyzed through a standardized bioinformatics pipeline, which integrated various professional software and statistical methods, aiming to extract reliable biological insights from the massive raw data. The specific workflow and methods are as follows: Raw data preprocessing was designed to filter noise, impute missing values, and perform normalization to enhance data quality and reliability. All steps were performed in the R language environment. Peak Extraction and Identification: 38,292 chromatographic peaks were extracted from the raw data. Metabolite identification was achieved by matching MS/MS spectra against Biotree's in-house Level 1 Metabolomics Database, which contains over 20,000 metabolite standards. Data Cleaning and Filtering: Deviation Value Filtering: The Relative Standard Deviation (RSD) of each peak was calculated, and unstable peaks with RSD &gt; 30% were removed. Missing Value Filtering: Peaks with&nbsp;50% missing rate in any single experimental group and&nbsp;50% total missing rate across all samples were retained. Missing Value Imputation: The remaining missing values were imputed using the half of the minimum value method. Data Normalization: Total Ion Current (TIC) normalization was applied to correct for overall response variations between samples. The analysis employed a strategy combining multivariate and univariate statistics to comprehensively and reliably identify differential metabolites. Software: SIMCA (V18.0.1, Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB). Methods: Principal Component Analysis (PCA): An unsupervised model was built after logarithmic transformation (LOG) and centering (CTR) of the data to observe the overall sample distribution trend and identify outliers. Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA): A supervised discriminant model was constructed. Model quality was assessed by 7-fold cross-validation, and its robustness was verified via a 200-times permutation test to prevent overfitting.</p>	<p>This study employed a systematic analytical pipeline to extract metabolite features from raw mass spectrometry data and perform identification at different confidence levels along with biological functional annotation. Chromatography System: Ultra High Performance Liquid Chromatography system equipped with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). Mass Spectrometry System: Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 120 quadrupole-Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometer equipped with an Electrospray Ionization source. Data Acquisition Mode: Data-Dependent Acquisition was performed in positive/negative ion switching mode. The full MS scan resolution was 60,000, and the MS/MS scan resolution was 15,000, using stepped normalized collision energy. Feature Detection and Extraction: Process: Chromatographic peaks were automatically detected and extracted from the raw LC-MS data files. Information for each feature included mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), retention time (RT), and peak area. Initial Output: A total of 38,292 features were extracted from all samples (18 experimental + 3 QC samples). Metabolite annotation was based on high-accuracy MS1 and MS/MS spectra, following a strategy of different confidence levels. Public Databases Used: KEGG: Used for pathway analysis and general metabolite annotation. HMDB: Used for detailed metabolite annotation. PubChem: Used for extensive compound structure and identifier information.</p>

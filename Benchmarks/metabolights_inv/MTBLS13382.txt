Study Title	Expanding the clinical use of DBS: through a metabolomic and a lipidomic evaluation
Study Description	<p>Personalized medicine aims to tailor healthcare to individual characteristics, relying on precise biological data and patient monitoring. Metabolomics and lipidomics play a key role in this approach, providing real-time insights into metabolic states and enabling accurate diagnosis and treatment optimization. However, traditional blood sampling methods are invasive, costly, and logistically complex. Dried Blood Spots (DBS) offer a promising alternative by enabling simple, minimally invasive self-sampling that facilitates remote patient monitoring and enhances clinical research participation. Already used for neonatal screening, DBS are now gaining attention for broader clinical applications, including metabolic and disease monitoring.</p><p>This study aimed to optimize pre-analytical conditions for DBS use in metabolomic and lipidomic analyses—evaluating factors such as extraction solvent, punch number, and injection volume—and to compare the biological information obtained from DBS with that from serum. Results demonstrate the potential of DBS as a reliable and practical tool for personalized medicine and clinical research, particularly in decentralized or resource-limited settings.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p> DBS and serum samples were collected at the same time, from 4 healthy patients. All samples were obtained in accordance with French legislation (Loi Jardé). In French hospitals with a research mission, the use of sample leftovers for research purposes is based on the principle of no opposition. According to Loi Jardé, no requirement of Ethical Committee or Institutional Review Board was required because no new sampling was required. This law also stipulates that patients must be fully informed of this information/practice and patients must have the possibility, at any time, of objecting to the use of their samples in research. The analyses were always performed in accordance with confidentiality rules. Data were coded without mention of first and last names, and the results were produced in a way that does not allow patients to be identified. Patients did not invoke their right to have a right of access, rectification, portability and limitation of the processing of data and/or biological samples.</p>	<p><strong>I. Extractions</strong></p><p><strong>I.1. DBS</strong></p><p> Based on the optimisation workflow, for metabolomics analysis, 2 punches (3.0 mm diameter disc) were extracted with 200 µL of ACN/MeOH/H2O (4:4:2) [32,33], vigorously vortexed for 1 min, then allowed to planar agitate for 1 h at strength 8 on 10 (Titramax 1000, Heidolph, Entraigues-sur-la-Sorgue, France) and then centrifuged (10 000 g, 10 min, 4°C). 150 µL of supernatant are aliquoted and dried in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf in a SpeedVac® during 1 h at 40 °C. The dried extracts were redisolved with 75 µL of solvent, depending on the analysis: ACN/H2O (20:80) for hydrophobic analysis or ACN/H2O (80:20) for hydrophilic analysis. After being vigorously vortexed during 1 min and then allowed to planar agitation for 30 min at strength 8 on 10 for homogenization, all aliquots were transferred in 96-well plates. </p><p> For lipidomics analysis, 2 punches were added with 1 mL of internal standard solution, then extracted with IPA (isopropyl alcohol). After being vigorously vortexed during 1 min, then allowed to planar agitate (1 h, strength 8/10, obscurity). (Titramax 1000, Heidolph, Entraigues-sur-la-Sorgue, France), the solutions were centrifuged (10 000 g, 10 min, 4 °C). 500 µL of supernatant were aliquoted and dried in a 1.5 ml Eppendorf in a Savant SPD111V SpeedVac Concentrator (ThermoFisher, Saint-Herblain, France) for 2 h at 40 ° C. The dried extracts were redisolved with 100 µL of ACN/IPA/H2O (6:3:1). Before being aliquoted in a 96-well plate, samples have been homogenised (1 min vortex then planar agitation for 1 h at strength 8 on 10).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>I.2. Serum</strong></p><p> For metabolomics purposes, 50 µL of serum were extracted with 150 µL of cold MeOH [34], vigorously vortexed during 1 min, rest 20 min at -20 °C then allowed to planar agitation for 1 h at strength 8 on 10 (Titramax 1000, Heidolph, Entraigues-sur-la-Sorgue, France). After centrifugation (10 000 g, 10 min, 4 °C), 150 µL of supernatant were aliquoted and dried in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf in a Savant SPD111V SpeedVac Concentrator (ThermoFisher, Saint-Herblain, France) during 1 h at 40 °C. The dried extracts were redisolved with 100 µL of solvent, depending on the analysis: ACN/H2O (20:80) for hydrophobic analysis or ACN/H2O (80:20) for hydrophilic analysis Before being aliquoted in a 96-well plate, samples have been homogenised (1 min vortex then planar agitation for 1 h at strength 8 on 10).For lipidomics purposes, 50 µL of serum were extracted following Folch’ procedure [30] with 1 mL of internal standard solution. After being vigorously vortexed during 1 min, rest 20 min at -20 °C then allowed to planar agitate (1 h, strength 8/10). After centrifugation (10 000 g, 10 min, 4 °C), 900 µL of aqueous supernatants were aliquoted and dried in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf in a Savant SPD111V SpeedVac Concentrator (ThermoFisher, Saint-Herblain, France) during 1 h at 40 °C. The dried extracts were redisolved with 100 µL of solvent ACN/IPA/H2O (6:3:1). Quantitative Analysis of SCFA (Short Chain Fatty Acids)</strong></p><p>2 punches were extracted with 200 µL of ACN/MeOH/H2O (4:4:2) and 50 µL of serum were extracted with 150 µL of cold methanol. After being vigorously vortexed during 1 min and then allowed to planar agitation for 1 h at strength 8 on 10 (Titramax 1000, Heidolph, Entraigues-sur-la-Sorgue, France) and then centrifuged (10 000 g, 10 min, 4 °C). 50 µL of supernatant is derived by 3-nitrophenylhydrazine in a 500 µL 96-well plates. </p><p> The derivatization process was adapted from the method described by Liebisch et al. [32]. 50 µL of sample (or solutions for calibration curve points), 50 µL of an aqueous internal standard mixture containing acetic-13C2-acid (for acetic acid and propionic acid), butanoic-d7-acid (for butyric acid and isobutyric acid) and valeric-d9-acid (for valeric acid and isovaleric acid), all standards at 1 nM, were added to samples. Then, 50 µL of a 2.5 % pyridine solution in methanol, 50 µL of EDC solution (n-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-n′-ethylcarbodiimide) in methanol at 105 mM and 50 µL of an aqueous/methanol solution (1:1) of 3-NPH (3-nitrophenylhydrazine) at 175 mM were added. The derivatization solution acted for 1 h at 4 °C. The reaction was quenched by adding 250 µL of acidified water (1 % formic acid). The multistep gradient (followed by 2 min of equilibration time) was programmed as follows: 0.0–0.1 min, flow : 0.6mL/min, 10% B; 0.10–9.25 min, flow : 0.6 mL/min, 10-35% B; 9.25–11.50 min, flow : 0.60-0.65 mL/min, 35-85% B; 11.5–11.8 min, flow : 0.65-0.80 mL/min, 85-100% B; 11.8–12.0 min, flow : 0.8-1.0 mL/min, 100% B; 12.0–12.1 min, flow : 1.0 mL/min, 100% B; 12.1–12.4 min, flow : 1.00-0.85 mL/min, 100% B; 12.40–12.45 min, flow : 0.85 mL/min, 100-55% B; 12.45–12.50 min, flow : 0.85-0.80 mL/min, 55-10% B; 12.50–12.60 min, flow :&nbsp;0.88-0.70 mL/min, 10% B; 12.60–12.70 min, flow : 0.7-0.6 mL/min, 10% B; 12.7–15.0 min, flow : 0.6 mL/min, 10% B. The injection volume is 5µL for both matrices.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>3. Semi-quantitive lipidomics</strong></p><p>The analysis was performed using Ultra-Performance Convergence Chromatography (UPC2). A 1 µL aliquot of lipid extract was injected into an ACQUITY UPC2 Torus DEA column (100 × 3.0 mm i.d., particle size: sub-1.7 µm, Waters) at 40°C (Le Faouder et al., Metabolites, DOI: 10.3390/metabo11050305). Mobile phases with a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min included SCCO2 for phase A and MeOH/H2O (98:2 v/v) with 20 mM ammonium acetate for phase B (modifier). The gradient program began with 1% of B solvent; from 0.5 to 6 min, this increased to 40%, then to 65% from 6 to 6.10 min. B was maintained at 65% for 3 minutes before returning to initial conditions for 3 minutes using an active back pressure regulator (ABPR) set at 1,500 psi. From 6 to 9 min, the flow rate was decreased to 1.1 mL/min. The make-up flow was MeOH/H2O (95:5 v/v) at 0.1 mL/min.</p>	<p><strong>MS Parameters</strong></p><p class='ql-align-center'><em>Also available on Protocols.io : https://dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.81wgbwkyqgpk/v2 </em></p><p class='ql-align-center'><br></p><p><strong>1. Untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics</strong></p><p> Analyses were performed on a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) equipped with a HESI source (head electrospray ionisation). Xcalibur 2.2 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) controlled the system.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>1.1. Metabolomic - Reversed-Phase </strong> </p><p> The HESI source (head electrospray ionisation) was used for both chromatography systems with a spray voltage of 3.5 kV, a capillary temperature of 325 °C, a heater temperature of 350 °C, a sheath gas flow of 50 arbitrary units (AU), an auxiliary gas flow of 13 AU, a sweep gas flow of 3 AU, and a tube lens S-Lens RF level 50, operated in positive (ESI+) and negative (ESI-) electrospray ionization modes (one run for each mode). Detection was performed with a full-scan acquisition at 70,000 resolution (m/z = 200), which ranged from 58.0 to 870.0 m/z, with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 105 charges and a maximum injection time (IT) of 250 ms.</p><p> </p><p><strong>1.2. Metabolomics - HILIC </strong></p><p> The HESI source (head electrospray ionisation) was used for both chromatography systems with a spray voltage of 3.5 kV, a capillary temperature of 325 °C, a heater temperature of 350 °C, a sheath gas flow of 50 arbitrary units (AU), an auxiliary gas flow of 13 AU, a sweep gas flow of 3 AU, and a tube lens S-Lens RF level 50, operated in positive (ESI+) electrospray ionization modes. Detection was performed with a full-scan acquisition at 70,000 resolution (m/z = 200), which ranged from 58.0 to 870.0 m/z, with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 105 charges and a maximum injection time (IT) of 250 ms.</p><p> </p><p><strong>1.3. Lipidomic - Reversed-Phase </strong></p><p> The HESI source (head electrospray ionisation) was used for both chromatography systems with a spray voltage of 3.5 kV, a capillary temperature of 325 °C, a heater temperature of 350 °C, a sheath gas flow of 50 arbitrary units (AU), an auxiliary gas flow of 13 AU, a sweep gas flow of 3 AU, and a tube lens S-Lens RF level 50, operated in positive (ESI+) and negative (ESI-) electrospray ionization modes (one run for each mode). Detection was performed with a full-scan acquisition at 70,000 resolution (m/z = 200), which ranged from 150.0 to 1500.0 m/z, with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 105 charges and a maximum injection time (IT) of 250 ms.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>2. Quantitative metabolomics</strong></p><p> Analyses were performed on a triple quadrupole Xevo TQ-XS mass spectrometer (Waters, Guyancourt, France) operated in MRM mode in positive and/or negative electrospray ionization modes (Zspray; ESI). The acquired data were processed using the MassLynx TargetLynx module (Waters, Guyancourt, France).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>3. Semi-quantitive lipidomics</strong></p><p> Super Fluid Critical (SFC)-lipidomic analyses were carried out with a Xevo G2-XS (Q-TOF) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The ionization source was an ESI, used in both positive and negative modes, in separated acquisition, allowing to analyse Diacylglycerol (DG), Triacylglycerol (TG), Cholesteryl Ester (CE), Acylcarnitine (CAR), Ceramide (CER), Hexosylceramide (HexCer); Dihexosylceramide (Hex2Cer), Lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), Lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE), Phosphatidylcholine (PC), Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE),&nbsp;Sphingomyelin (SM), Fatty Acids (FA), Phosphatidylglycerol (PG), Phosphatidylinositol (PI), Phosphatidylserine (PS). Lipid classes were detected in both positive and negative ionization modes in separate runs at a resolution of 16,000 (at m/z 400). </p><p class='ql-align-center'><em>More information available here : https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17184000 </em></p>	<p>Untargeted metabolomics, untargeted lipidomics and semi-quantitative lipidomics files have been converted in anther format.</p><p><br></p><ol><li>Untargeted metabolomics &amp; lipidomics, acquired with Q-Exactive, have been converted in mzMLformat with MSconvert for analysis with Workflow4Metabolomics</li><li>Semi-quantitative lipidomics file have been converted using DataConnect to be analyzed with MZmine</li></ol><p><br></p><p>Quantitative metabolomics files have not been converted.</p>	<p><strong>1.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Softwares</strong></p><p> MassLynx (Waters, Guyancourt, France) was used for the absolute quantification of metabolites, while MZmine (mzio GmbH) [36] was used for the relative quantification of lipids. For exploratory analyses, features were considered for further analysis only if they were detected in all injections and their CV were less than 30 % after signal normalization. The identification of metabolites was performed based on MS² spectra and retention time compared with the in-house injected database of &gt; 600 compounds from IROA [37] (MSMLS, FAMLS, BACSMLS &amp; OAMLS from Merck) . Concerning the identification of lipids, the experimental MS2 spectra were analysed with LipidSearch 5.0 (OPTON-30879, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Levels of confidence in the identification are classified in LipidSearch as (A) “ all specific ions are assigned for the head groups and substituents”, (B) “all specific ions are assigned for the head groups and some specific ions are assigned for substituents” or “some specific ions are assigned for the head groups and all specific ions are assigned for substituents”, and (C) “some specific ions are missing for the head groups and substituents”. Compounds assigned as below D-level were not considered. Once the compounds were identified using MS², the retention times and mass of the parent ion were used to identify them in all the samples injected in MS1. In the case of lipidomic analysis, an external QC (a pool sample of several biological samples such as serum, egg yolk, feces and brain tissue extract) was injected multiple times and analyzed to evaluate the reproducibility of the analysis and to calibrate the retention time of the lipids from the database. </p><p> To decipher which pathways are described using each sample types, we compared metabolic pathway enrichment by loading identified metabolites into MetaboAnalyst for DBS and serum. Enrichment is calculated by comparing the number of metabolites observed in a sample (hits) for a specific metabolic pathway compared to the total number of metabolites expected in that pathway (TOT.), and then comparing this to the total identified metabolites in the sample. All pathways found significantly enriched in serum are also enriched in DBS, with DBS providing access to an even greater number of enriched pathways.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>2.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Data Analysis</strong></p><p> The evaluation criteria to determine the best extraction methods of DBS were the number of features detected (with an intensity &gt; 104), the total signal intensity produced by the features, the number of identified compounds and the average CV of these identified compounds. Once the optimum extractant was determined (for metabolomics and lipidomics), the number of punches and the optimum injection volume were investigated. The evaluation criteria were the number of identified compounds and their global CV. </p><p> To compare the information provided by both sample types, the numbers of identified compounds was evaluated through Venn’s diagram, the variability and the level of confidence in the identification of identified compounds and the biological pathways described through enrichment pathways on MetaboAnalyst.</p>

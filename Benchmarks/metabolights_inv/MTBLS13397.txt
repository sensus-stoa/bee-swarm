Study Title	Serum metabolomics identifies novel prognostic biomarkers in amanita poisoning
Study Description	<p><strong>Background:</strong> Amanita poisoning causes 90-95% of global mushroom-related deaths, yet early prognostic biomarkers for Amanita poisoning are lacking.</p><p><strong>Methods:</strong> 33 patients with Amanita poisoning were recruited and categorized into survival and death group. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to investigate the independent mortality risk factors for Amanita poisoning patients. Untargeted serum metabolomics was performed to screen the differentially expressed metabolites. The quality control (QC) samples were used to evaluate the stability and reproducibility of ultra performance liquid chromatography quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS) analytical system. The prognosis predictive metabolic biomarkers were identified by ROC curve analysis. Correlations between metabolic biomarkers and biochemical indicators were analyzed by Spearman’s correlation analysis.</p><p><strong>Results:</strong> Significant differences were observed between the survival and death groups in clinical manifestations—such as gastrointestinal bleeding, dizziness, headache, delirious coma, infection and shortness of breath—and in biochemical indicators, including alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT). Metabolomic analysis identified 80 differentially expressed metabolites involved primarily in amino acid and unsaturated fatty acid metabolism. ROC analysis (AUC &gt; 0.9) screened nine potential metabolic biomarkers for predicting clinical outcomes: 9,10-Epoxyoctadecenoic acid, PI(16:0/18:2(9Z,12Z)), N-Acetyl-L-aspartic acid, PI(20:3(5Z,8Z,11Z)/18:0), Propionylcarnitine, Proline betaine, 4'-Methyl-(-)-epigallocatechin 3-(4-methyl-gallate), PG(18:1(11Z)/22:6(4Z,7Z,10Z,13Z,16Z,19Z)) and L-Proline. Notably, correlation analysis revealed that 9,10-Epoxyoctadecenoic acid was positively correlated with AST and APTT, whereas 4'-Methyl-(-)-epigallocatechin 3-(4-methyl-gallate), N-acetyl-L-aspartic acid, PI(16:0/18:2(9Z,12Z)), PI(20:3(5Z,8Z,11Z)/18:0) and Propionylcarnitine showed negative correlations with various liver and coagulation parameters.</p><p><strong>Conclusions:</strong> Serum metabolomics has identified metabolic biomarkers capable of predicting mortality in&nbsp;Amanita&nbsp;poisoning, with significant correlation to liver and coagulation injury. These biomarkers may facilitate early risk stratification and guide targeted therapeutic interventions. Limitations include small sample size and single-center retrospective design, which may restrict result generalizability.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Blood samples were collected from all patients upon their admission to the emergency ward, within 24 h after the suspected&nbsp;Amanita&nbsp;poisoning event. The samples were drawn into EDTA-containing tubes and allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min for clotting. Subsequently, they were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to isolate the serum. The supernatant (serum) was aliquoted and stored at -80 °C until further analysis. The stability of the serum samples during freeze-thaw cycles and long-term storage was monitored by regularly testing the protein content.</p>	<p>For metabolite extraction, a 100 μL aliquot of each thawed serum sample was mixed with 20 μL of an internal standard solution (L-2-chlorophenylalanine, 0.3 mg/mL in methanol). The mixture was vortexed for 10 s. Subsequently, 300 μL of a precooled methanol/acetonitrile mixture (2:1, v/v) was added, followed by vortexing for 1 min. The solution was then subjected to ultrasonic extraction in an ice-water bath for 10 min, stored at -20 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. A 300 μL volume of the supernatant was collected, and 150 μL of it was transferred to an LC-MS injection vial for analysis. To ensure data quality, a quality control (QC) sample was prepared by pooling equal volumes of the extraction liquid from all samples. One QC sample was analyzed after every 5 experimental samples to monitor the stability and repeatability of the instrument.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on an Ultimate 3000 LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using an Acclaim RSLC120-C18 column (2.1 mm x 100 mm, 2.2 μm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) a 0.1% ammonium formate aqueous solution (containing 2 mmol/L ammonium formate) and (B) acetonitrile. The flow rate was constant at 0.2 mL/min with an injection volume of 10 μL. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0.0-2.0 min, 2% B; 2.0-12.0 min, 2-50% B; 12.0-20.0 min, 50-90% B; 20.0-30.0 min, 90% B; 30.0-60.0 min, 90-2% B.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric analysis was carried out on an ESI-QTOF-MSMS (Impact II, Bruker, Germany) operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. The instrument parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage, 4.5 kV (positive) and 3.5 kV (negative); dry gas flow, 8 L/min; dry gas temperature, 200 °C; nebulizer pressure, 2.0 bar; and fragmentor voltage, 500 V. The mass spectrometer was operated in full-scan data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode with a mass scanning range from&nbsp;20 to 1000 m/z.</p>	<p>The raw data files were processed using Metaboscape 3.0 software (Bruker) for noise reduction, peak detection, peak extraction, alignment and normalization. The resulting data matrix, containing compound names, chemical formulas, molecular weights, retention times, sample names and group information, was exported. This processed data was then imported into MetaboAnalyst 6.0 for multivariate statistical analysis, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA). The variable importance in projection (VIP) value from the OPLS-DA model was used to rank the contribution of metabolites to group separation.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed by comparing the acquired MS data against standard databases. The accurate mass (MS1) and retention time of each feature were matched against the Bruker Company library and the freely available Human Metabolome Database (HMDB,&nbsp;https://hmdb.ca). Due to the untargeted nature of the acquisition, annotations were primarily based on accurate mass and retention time matching, as confirmatory MS/MS fragment ions were not consistently available for all features in the data-dependent acquisition mode.</p>

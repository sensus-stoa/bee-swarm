Study Title	Habitual Coffee Intake Shapes the Gut Microbiome and Modifies Host Physiology and Cognition: double-blind, randomised, parallel design
Study Description	<p>Coffee influences multiple physiological processes, including gut function, stress, cognition, and the microbiome. However, the mechanisms underlying these effects remain poorly understood. In this study, we examined coffee’s impact on the microbiota–gut–brain axis—a bidirectional communication pathway between the gut microbiome and the brain—and assessed whether these effects occur independently of caffeine in healthy participants. Our primary outcome was microbiota composition and function, whereas the secondary outcome was gut microbial metabolites and coffee-related metabolites (NCT05927038 and NCT05927103). Significant group differences emerged in faecal microbiome composition, with coffee drinkers showing increased relative abundance of Cryptobacterium and Eggerthella species, alongside reduced levels of the metabolite’s indole-3-propionic acid, indole-3-carboxyaldehyde, and the neurotransmitter γ-aminobutyric acid. Behaviourally, coffee drinkers exhibited greater impulsivity and emotional reactivity, whereas non-coffee drinkers demonstrated better memory performance. Some alterations in the faecal metabolome were reversible following coffee abstinence, and reintroduction triggered acute microbiome changes independent of caffeine. An integrated model identified nine key metabolites—including theophylline, caffeine, and selected phenolic acids—strongly linked to microbial species and cognitive measures. These findings reveal previously unrecognised effects of coffee on the microbiota–gut–brain axis, suggesting that microbiome profiles could potentially predict coffee consumption patterns and highlighting a close association between coffee intake and gut microbial composition.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Biological samples from human adults were collected as part of the study on the day of Visit 2 (V2), Visit 3 (V3) and Visit 4 (V4). </p><p>In total, four matrices were collected (saliva, blood, urine and stool) of which stool was analysed by metabolomics and described in this submission. </p><p><br></p><p><strong>&nbsp;Stool</strong></p><p>Participants were asked to collect the first freshly voided stool samples of the day in plastic containers containing an AnaeroGen sachet (Oxoid AGS AnaeroGen Compact, Fischer Scientific, Dublin). Stool samples were refrigerated using ice packs until delivery to the laboratory, where they were aliquoted and stored at −80°C. Stool samples were collected the day of the visits as well as during the extra timepoints during the coffee washout and coffee intervention.</p>	<p>Stool sample were extracted using four times the initial sample weight (wt/vol) of MilliQ water, vortexing until a suspension was reached and centrifugation to separate particulate matter. The supernatant was filtered by centrifugation (Pall AcroPrepTM, 0.2 um, 4.000 g, 4°C, 15 min).</p><p><br></p><p>For reverse phase analysis, sample extracts were diluted eleven times in mobile phase eluent A (10 mM ammonium formate + 0.1% formic acid in ultrapure water) and fortified with stable isotope (deuterium and 13C) labelled standards before analysis.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>For GC-MS-based short-chain fatty acid analysis, performed by MS-Omics (Vedbæk, Denmark)[1], stool extract supernatants were acidified with hydrochloric acid (0.5 M) and fortified with deuterium-labelled standards.</p><p><br></p><p>Ref:</p><p>[1] https://cmbio.io/contact-us</p>	<p>Semi-polar metabolite profiling was performed by MS-Omics (part of cmbio, Vedbæk, Denmark). The semi-polar analysis in randomized order via reverse phase UHPLC was performed using an adapted version of the protocol previously described[1]. A UHPLC system (VH-A10-A, VH-P10-A, ThermoFisher Scientific) equipped with an Acquity Premier&nbsp;HSS T3 (2.1x150 mm, 1.7um, Waters) column, at 30°C and a flow of 0.3 ml/min on an injection volume of 5 uL was applied.&nbsp;Eluent A consisted of 10 mM ammonium formate in water with 0.1% formic acid, and eluent B consisted of 10 mM ammonium formate in methanol with 0.1% formic acid.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>Short chain fatty acid analysis of Acetic acid, Formic acid, Propanoic acid, 2-Methylpropanoic&nbsp;acid, Butanoic acid, 3-Methylbutanoic acid, Pentanoic acid, 4-Methylpentanoic acid, Hexanoic&nbsp;acid, and Heptanoic acid was performed in a targeted manner on a high-polarity&nbsp;</p><p>column (ZebronTM ZB-FFAP, GC Cap. Column 30 m x 0.25 mm x 0.25 μm) installed in a GC&nbsp;(7890B, Agilent) coupled with a quadrupole MS (5977B MSD, Agilent). SCFAs were injected by 0.5 uL into a 180°C liner, at a split ratio of 1:50 and constant flow of 4 ml/min. A temperature gradient from 80°C to 190°C over 8 minutes (at a rate of 15°C/min), followed by a rapid ramp from 190°C to 260°C (100°C/min) was used to separate SCFAs.</p><p><br></p><p>Ref:</p><p>[1] Doneanu CE, Chen W, Mazzeo JR. UPLC/MS Monitoring of Water-Soluble Vitamin Bs in Cell Culture Media in Minutes. Water Appl. Note. 2011</p>	<p>For semi-polar metabolite analysis, the Orbitrap Exploris 240 MS (Thermo Scientific) operated with following parameters: full MS scan, a resolution of 60,000, AGC target Standard, maximum injection time mode auto, and a scan range of 65 to 975 m/z. Data-dependent MS/MS (dd-MS²) parameters included a TopN of 5, resolution of 30,000, AGC target of Standard, Auto maximum injection time, isolation window of 0.4 m/z, and stepped collision energy (NCE) of 20, 40, 60. Dynamic exclusion was set to 2.5s after 1 time to prevent repeated fragmentation of the same ion, with an intensity threshold of 1.0e3 for ion selection, and an apex trigger with a trigger window of 30%. Isotope exclusion was enabled to focus on the most abundant isotopes, and lock masses were used to ensure calibration stability throughout the runs. An electrospray ionization interface was used as a source. Analysis was performed in positive and negative ionization mode in switching (alternating) polarity.</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p>Short-chain fatty acid analysis was performed in a targeted manner using a quadrupole MS (5977B MSD, Agilent) in SIM mode equipped with EI source and a 1 min solvent delay. The transfer line was heated to 250°C and ion source temperature was set to 230°C.</p>	<p>LC-MS metabolomics data processing was performed untargeted using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Scientific) and Skyline 22.2 (MacCoss Lab Software) for peak picking and feature grouping.</p><p><br></p><p>Raw GC-MS data&nbsp;were processed and quantified in Skyline 22.2[1] and the&nbsp;PARADISe software (v2.6)[2] developed by MS-Omics and collaborators.</p><p><br></p><p>Ref:</p><p>[1] Adams KJ, Pratt B, Bose N, Dubois LG, St John-Williams L, Perrott KM, Ky K, Kapahi P, Sharma V, MacCoss MJ, Moseley MA, Colton CA, MacLean BX, Schilling B, Thompson JW; Alzheimer’s Disease Metabolomics Consortium. Skyline for Small Molecules: A Unifying Software Package for Quantitative Metabolomics. J Proteome Res. 2020 Apr 3;19(4):1447-1458. doi: 10.1021/acs.jproteome.9b00640. Epub 2020 Mar 26. PMID: 31984744; PMCID: PMC7127945.</p><p>[2] Lea G. Johnsen, Peter B. Skou, Bekzod Khakimov, Rasmus Bro, Gas chromatography – mass spectrometry data processing made easy, Journal of Chromatography A, Volume 1503, 2017, Pages 57-64, ISSN 0021-9673, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chroma.2017.04.052.</p>	<p>An MS-Omics in-house annotation and curation pipeline written in MATLAB (2022b, MathWorks) was applied. Identification of compounds was performed at four levels: Level 1: identification by retention times (compared against in-house authentic standards, with an accepted deviation of 0.2 min), accurate mass (with an accepted deviation of 3 ppm), and MS/MS spectra (with a match factor above 60). Level 2a: identification by retention times (compared against in-house authentic standards, with an accepted deviation of 0.2 min), accurate mass (with an accepted deviation of 3 ppm). Level 2b: identification by accurate mass (with an accepted deviation of 3 ppm), and MS/MS spectra from in-house authentic standards and mzCloud (ThermoScientific) at a match factor &gt;60. Level 3: identification by accurate mass alone (with an accepted deviation of 3ppm), based on the Human metabolome database&nbsp;(v 5.0)[1].</p><p>&nbsp;In the case of known isotopes with indistinguishable retention time and fragmentation behaviour, co-eluting peaks were manually integrated and reported as compound groups (e.g. hexoses or sugar alcohols).</p><p><br></p><p>Ref:</p><p>[1] Wishart DS, Guo AC, Oler E, et al.,&nbsp;HMDB 5.0: the Human Metabolome Database for 2022.&nbsp;Nucleic Acids Res. 2022.

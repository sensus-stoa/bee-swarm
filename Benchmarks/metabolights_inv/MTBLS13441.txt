Study Title	Integrated Transcriptomic and Metabolomic Analyses Reveal the Antibacterial Mechanism of Isocorydine against Mycobacterium bovis
Study Description	<p>Mycobacterium bovis, the causative agent of bovine tuberculosis, poses a significant zoonotic threat. This study defines the antibacterial activity and mechanism of the natural alkaloid isocorydine (ICD) against M. bovis using an integrated multi-omics approach. Phenotypic analyses demonstrated that ICD (MIC=400 μg/mL) disrupts cell wall and membrane integrity, causing ion leakage, extracellular pH alteration, lipid depletion, and severe ultrastructural damage. Transcriptomic profiling under sub-inhibitory ICD stress revealed 66 differentially expressed genes, with significant upregulation of efflux pumps (ABC transporters, MMPL, SMR) and their associated TetR family transcriptional regulators, indicating a targeted bacterial counter-response. Concurrently, metabolomic analysis identified 1,158 differential metabolites, depicting a state of profound metabolic dysregulation characterized by the depletion of central carbon metabolites (e.g., sugars, succinate) and the accumulation of fatty acids and precursors (e.g., oleic acid). Integrated pathway analysis highlighted critical disruptions in starch/sucrose metabolism, ABC transporters, and fatty acid biosynthesis. Our findings establish that ICD exerts its bactericidal effect primarily by targeting the cell envelope, causing irreparable physical damage and triggering a catastrophic metabolic crisis that overwhelms the bacterium's compensatory mechanisms. This work elucidates a multi-targeted antibacterial mechanism and positions ICD as a promising phytochemical lead for combating mycobacterial infections.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Mycobacterium bovis CVCC68002 cells were cultured in 7H9 medium supplemented with 10% OADC at 37°C with shaking (180 rpm). Following dilution to OD600 0.01, treatment groups were exposed to 200 μg/mL isocorydine (1/2 MIC) while controls received vehicle only (DMSO). After 12 hours of incubation, cells from six biological replicates per group were collected by centrifugation (6,000 g, 5 min, 4°C), washed three times with ice-cold PBS (pH 7.0), flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C until metabolite extraction.</p>	<p>Bacterial cells were harvested and washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by centrifugation (5,000 g, 5 min, 4°C) to completely remove the supernatant. Metabolites were then extracted by adding 1 mL of a pre-cooled methanol:acetonitrile:water solution (2:2:1, v/v/v) to the cell pellet. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and subjected to cell disruption. For complete lysis of the mycobacterial cells, mechanical bead beating was employed in conjunction with ice-bath ultrasonication for 20 min. The resulting extract was passed through a 0.22 μm organic phase filter membrane and incubated at −20°C for 1 h to precipitate proteins. After centrifugation at 13,000 rpm (4°C, 15 min), the supernatant was carefully collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane again, and stored at −80°C until LC-MS analysis. For quality control, a pooled QC sample was prepared by combining equal volumes of each individual extract. Solvent blanks were processed in parallel to monitor background contamination. The reproducibility of the extraction and analysis procedure was confirmed by the consistent total ion chromatograms and tight clustering of the QC samples in subsequent multivariate statistical analysis.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC system equipped with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (1.7 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm) maintained at 25°C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water containing 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonia, and (B) acetonitrile. Elution was carried out at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min with an injection volume of 2 µL using the following gradient program: 95% B (0–0.5 min), linearly decreased to 65% B (0.5–7 min), further to 40% B (7–8 min), held at 40% B (8–9 min), returned to 95% B (9–9.1 min), and re-equilibrated at 95% B until 12 min. Throughout the analysis, samples were kept at 4°C in the autosampler.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric analyses were conducted using a liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) platform. Metabolite profiling was performed on an Agilent 6550 Q-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with a Jet Stream electrospray ionization (ESI) source, operating in both positive and negative ionization modes across an m/z range of 50–1200. Key source parameters included: gas temperature, 250 °C; drying gas flow, 16 L/min; nebulizer, 20 psig; and capillary voltage, 3000 V. High-resolution MS/MS spectra for metabolite identification were subsequently acquired on an AB SCIEX TripleTOF 6600 system using data-dependent acquisition (DDA). For the TripleTOF system, ion source conditions were set to 650 °C with an ion spray voltage floating (ISVF) of ±5000 V, and MS/MS scans were triggered on the top 10 precursor ions using a collision energy of 35 ± 15 eV. The full m/z range was analyzed in segmented intervals (50–300, 290–600, 590–900, 890–1200) to enhance secondary spectral coverage. Mass calibration was performed prior to sequence acquisition to ensure measurement accuracy throughout the analysis.</p>	<p>Raw MS data files were converted from the vendor format to the open mzXML format using ProteoWizard software. Subsequent data processing, including peak picking, retention time alignment, and peak area integration, was performed using the XCMS package. Metabolite identification was carried out by matching the accurate mass (mass error &lt; 25 ppm) and MS/MS fragmentation patterns against a custom-built in-house database. Prior to statistical analysis, the processed data matrix was normalized by total peak area and scaled using Pareto scaling.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed based on high-resolution MS and MS/MS spectra acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode on the TripleTOF 6600 system. Putative identifications were assigned by matching the accurate precursor mass (mass tolerance &lt; 25 ppm) and the corresponding MS/MS fragmentation patterns against a custom, in-house metabolite spectral database, utilizing both metabolite (MetDDA) and lipid (LipDDA) specific search methods.</p>

Study Title	Unravelling age-driven molecular transitions underlying Pacific Oyster Mortality Syndrome permissiveness in Magallana gigas
Study Description	<p>Ageing is a progressive and irreversible biological process characterized by the deterioration of physiological functions and increased vulnerability to mortality. Although extensively studied in vertebrates, ageing in long-lived invertebrates remains comparatively unexplored. While ageing typically leads to greater susceptibility to infectious diseases, a striking and unexpected reversal was identified in oysters : older oysters exhibit markedly increased tolerance to the Pacific Oyster Mortality Syndrome (POMS), a panzootic disease primarily driven by the OsHV-1 herpesvirus and responsible for severe losses in global aquaculture. To investigate this counterintuitive pattern, we challenged oysters aged 4, 16, and 28 months from four biparental families and conducted an integrative multi-omics analysis; including epigenomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics; on the two families showing the strongest age-related increase in survival. Our results reveal that ageing in Magallana gigas is characterized by coordinated epigenetic, transcriptional, and metabolic reprogramming that reduces host permissiveness to POMS. We show that the epigenetic remodeling of key immune regulators (e.g., Toll-like receptors, MyD88) aligns with transcriptional rewiring of NF-κB and ubiquitin pathways, producing a finely tuned innate immune state marked by enhanced antiviral activity but reduced antibacterial responsiveness. We also identify age-related repression of mTOR signaling, likely promoting autophagy and improving viral control. These regulatory changes are tightly linked to metabolic adjustments; including reduced TCA cycle flux, remodeled nitrogen metabolism, and altered glutathione dynamics which collectively support a stress-tolerant, energy-conserving phenotype. Together, our findings reveal a fundamental evolutionary trade-off: juveniles prioritize growth at the cost of viral susceptibility, whereas adults invest in cellular maintenance and antiviral preparedness.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Production of full-sibling families </strong></p><p>Four biparental oyster (H2D, F14R, F11N, and F14V) families (Magallana gigas) were produced at the Ifremer hatchery in Argenton (France) in 2020.&nbsp;The Spat (F1) were transferred and maintained under biosecured conditions at the Ifremer nursery (Bouin, France). Further details about hatchery and nursery steps are&nbsp;described in (de Lorgeril et al., 2018, 2020). At 4-, 16-, and 28-month-old, the offspring were relocated to the Ifremer facilities in Argenton. Oysters were kept at 23°C and fed ad libitum (50/50 mixture of Tisochrysis lutea and Skeletonema costatum, 1,500 µm³/µL) for 15 days for acclimation (Rico-Villa et al., 2006). The pathogen-free status was confirmed by testing for the absence of OsHV-1 µVar DNA and ensuring that Vibrio sp. concentrations were below ten colony-forming units per milligram of tissue, as described in (Petton et al., 2015).</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p><strong>Production of the viral suspension for the experimental infection</strong></p><p>A viral suspension of Ostreid herpesvirus 1 µVar (OsHV-1 µVar) was prepared following the protocol from a mix of several variants described by (Schikorski et al., 2011). From moribund oysters the tissues were dissected, pooled, and homogenized in sterile artificial seawater using a blender under chilled conditions. The homogenate was subjected to sequential centrifugation steps to remove cellular debris (3,000 × g for 10 min followed by 10,000 g for 20 min at 4°C). The resulting supernatant was filtered through 0.45 µm and 0.22 µm pore-size filters to eliminate bacteria and larger particles, yielding a clarified viral suspension. The quantification of genomics units (viral load) of OsHV-1 µVar was performed by real-time quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) using a Stratagene Mx3005P Real-Time thermocycler. The qPCR reaction volume (20 μL) was: 5 μL DNA (5 ng/µL), 2 µL of each primer at the final concentration of 550 nM (Eurogentec SA), 1 µL of distilled water, and 10 µL of Brilliant III Ultra-Fast SYBR®Green PCR Master Mix (Agilent). The virus-specific primer pairs targeted a region of the OsHV-1 µVar genome predicted to a gene that encodes a DNA polymerase catalytic subunit (open reading frame, ORF) 100AY509253: Forward: 5’-ATTGATGATGTGGATAATCTGTG-3’ and Reverse: 5’-GGTAAATACCATTGGTCTTGTTCC-3’ (Webb et al., 2007; Pepin, 2013). The amplification qPCR program consisted of 3 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 20 s, with a melting temperature curve of the amplicon to verify the specificity of the amplification. Quantification of viral DNA copies was estimated by comparing the observed Cq values to a standard curve of the dimer primer amplification product cloned into the pCR4-TOPO vector. The final concentration was adjusted to 2.2 × 10^5 genome copies/µL and stored at –80°C.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p><strong>Experimental infection </strong></p><p>To investigate the effects of ageing on oysters' permissiveness to POMS, the four families (called recipients) were exposed to the disease through a cohabitation experiment with OsHV-1 µVar-injected donor oysters at ages 4-, 16-, and 28 months (see below), mimicking the natural transmission of POMS (Figure 1A and Table S1).</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>Donor oysters came from two stocks of “pathogen-free” oysters to maximize the production of OsHV-1 µVar during cohabitation: one stock, called Dellec, showing a highly susceptible to POMS (&gt; 90%) (de Lorgeril et al., 2018), and one stock, called NSI, with an expected 50–60% mortality (Petton et al., 2013). Twenty hours before the onset of cohabitation, 4-month-old donor oysters were anaesthetized with hexahydrate MgCl2 (ACROS, 50 g/L) for 2 hours (Suquet et al., 2009). Then, 100 µL of OsHV-1 µVar suspension was injected into their adductor muscle with a 26-gauge needle using a multi-dispensing pipette (Schikorski et al., 2011). The injected donor oysters were maintained at 23°C in tanks (Volume = 400 L) for 18 hours, an optimal temperature for the disease (Petton et al., 2021) Temperature, pH, salinity, and dissolved oxygen were monitored twice a day with a WTW Multiparameter MultiLine 3630 IDS and a Dotsman P750, equipped with a PT100 sensor and an iButton DS1922L-F5 thermochron.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>Cohabitation infection was performed by placing the injected donor and recipient oysters together at a 1:1 biomass ratio. The 4-, 16-, and 28-month-old oysters were acclimated in three, six, and fifteen tanks, respectively, to maintain a constant biomass per liter, as the individual weight increased throughout the age. To increase the transmission of OsHV-1 µVar to the recipient oysters, water renewal was halted for the first 125 hours post-cohabitation (hpc). At the end of this period, all surviving donor oysters were removed from the tanks. Once cohabitation began, six live recipient oysters were collected at 0 (basal conditions), 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours hpc for each family in each experiment. All tissues were removed from the shells, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80°C. Mortality was monitored twice daily, and dead oysters were removed as soon as they were observed. The experiment ended when no deaths were recorded in any of the tanks for a continuous 48 hours. The infection experiments were conducted over three consecutive years, each dedicated to a specific age group (4-, 16-, and 28-month-old recipient oysters), under the same conditions and within the same facility with the same donor oysters. To validate the mortality across the three cohabitation experiments, an internal control consisting of 4-month-old NSI recipient oysters was included in each trial.&nbsp;</p>	<p><strong>Sample Extraction</strong></p><p>Oyster tissue (20 mg) was promptly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen upon collection and transferred to 1.5 mL polypropylene tubes with screw caps (cat. No. 72.687, Sarstedt). 980µL of ice-cold methanol (cat. No. 0013684102BS, Biosolve), supplemented with 5 µL of 2-(N-Morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (Cat. No. M8250, Sigma-Aldrich) and 20 µL of mixed PUFAs ISTD was added to the tubes along with 0.5 g of glass beads. Samples were vortexed and homogenized using the Precellys 24 tissue homogenizer (speed 4 m/s, 2 cycles of 15 s each). Subsequently, the samples were agitated at 4°C for 1 hour, followed by removal of debris via centrifugation at 20,000 g (4°C) for 10 minutes. The resulting supernatant was transferred into 15 mL tubes.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Separation and Purification via SPE Column</strong></p><p>Strata-X 33 mm SPE Columns (10 mg) (cat. 8B-S100-AAK, Phenomenex) was mounted on a SPE manifold for separation and purification. Purification was proceed as followed :</p><p>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Washing and conditioning.</p><p>a.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add 1 mL of methanol.</p><p>b.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add 1 mL of 0.1% formic acid in UPLC.</p><p>c.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Discard the wash solutions and proceed rapidly to step 13.</p><p>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;II.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sample loading.</p><p>a.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Gently agitate the sample before loading onto the SPE column.</p><p>b.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add the sample stepwise by loading 1 mL onto the SPE column.</p><p>c.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keep the filtrate.</p><p>&nbsp;III.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rinsing.</p><p>a.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add 1 mL of 0.1% formic acid in UPLC.</p><p>b.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add 1 mL of 15% ethanol in UPLC water, and let it run completely through the column (proceed quickly to step IV. below).</p><p>c.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Discard the filtrate.</p><p>&nbsp;IV.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Elution.</p><p>a.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add 500 mL of methanol.</p><p>b.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Collect the eluate in a glass tube.</p><p>c.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Repeat steps IV.a. and IV.b. for a total of 1 mL.</p><p>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;V.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Divide the filtrate from step II.c. in two distinct tubes to proceed with GM and NUCs analysis with one fraction and TCA analysis with the last one.</p><p>&nbsp;VI.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dry samples in a SpeedVac.</p>	<p><strong>GM Analysis</strong></p><p><br></p><p>Samples were reconstitute in 200 µL of UPLC water and transferred in LC-MS polypropylene vials. Chromatographic analysis was performed using UHPLC 1290 Infinity II with an HPLC Discovery® HS F5, 3 μm particle size, L × I.D. 15 cm × 2.1 mm (cat. No. 567503-U, Sigma-Aldrich). The mobile phase A consisted 0.1% formic acid (cat. No. 33015, Sigma-Aldrich) in UPLC water (cat. No. 0023214102BS, Biosolve), and mobile phase B consisted in 0.1% formic acid in UPLC Acetonitrile (cat. No. 0001207802BS, Biosolve). The gradient used was: 0% B, 0.00 minute; 0% B, 2.00 minutes; 25% B, 5.00 minutes; 35% B, 11.00 minutes; 95% B, 15.00 minutes; 95% B, 20.00 minutes; 0% B, 20.10 minutes; 0% B, 25.00 minutes. The column temperature was set at 40°C, flow rate 0.250 mL/min, and volume of injection 0.5 μL for each run.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NUCs Analysis</strong></p><p><br></p><p>Analysis was made on the same samples as the GM Analysis. Chromatographic analysis was performed using UHPLC 1290 Infinity II with an InfinityLab Poroshell HPH-C18, 2.7 μm particle size, L × I.D. 100 cm × 3.1 mm (cat. No. 695975-502, Agilent). The mobile phase A consisted 10mM ammonium acetate (cat. No. 5.33004, Sigma-Aldrich) , 2.5 μM medronic acid (cat. No. 5191-3940, Agilent), 0.1% formic acid in UPLC water (cat. No. 0023214102BS, Biosolve), and mobile phase B consisted in 90% UPLC methanol (cat. No. 0013684102BS, Biosolve), 10% UPLC water, 2.5 μM medronic acid, 0.1% formic acid. The gradient used was: 2% B, 0.00 minute; 2% B, 1.00 minutes; 42% B, 4.00 minutes; 100% B, 5.00 minutes; 100% B, 6.00 minutes; 2% B, 6.50 minutes; 0% B, 8.00 minutes. The column temperature was set at 30°C, flow rate 0.400 mL/min, and volume of injection 3 μL for each run.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>TCA Analysis</strong></p><p><br></p><p>Samples were reconstitute in 200 µL of UPLC methanol/UPLC Water (80:20) and 10 µL of d27-Myristic acid (cat. No. 400505, Agilent) at 75 µg/mL was added and transferred into brown crimp vial with insert. Samples were then dried again by speedvac. Derivatization were proceed by a PAL autosampler and was proceed as follow : 90 µL of Methoxyamine hydrochloride (cat. No. 89803, Sigma-Aldrich) at 20g/L were added then samples were incubate at 30°C for 90 minutes. 10 µL of MSTFA (cat. No. TS-48915, Thermo Scientific) were added then incubate at 37°C for 30 minutes. Chromatographic analysis was performed using a 8890 GC chromatographic system with a DB-MS +DG, 0.25 μm film thickness, L × I.D. 30 m × 0.250 mm (cat. No. 122-5532G, Agilent) column. 1 µl of the prepared sample was injected to the gas chromatograph inlet in the split mode 10:1 with the inlet temperature held at 250°C. Helium is used as the carrier gas (1.1 ml/min). The GC is set to an initial temperature of 60°C for 1 min, followed by a 60°C/min ramp to 120°C, 30°C/min ramp to 250°C, and a third ramp of 60°C/min to 325°C, followed by a final hold time of 5 min.</p>	<p><strong>GM Analysis</strong></p><p>General metabolites profiling analysis was performed on a 6495 LC/TQ mass system and the Agilent MassHunter workstation (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) with the dynamic MRM scan mode. Source-parameter settings in the positive or negative modes were as follows. The capillary voltage was set at 4 kV for positive and negative mode, Gas temperature at 250°C, Gas flow at 12 L/min, Nebulizer at 35 psi, Sheath Gas Temperature at 400 °C and the Sheath Gas Flow at 12 L/min.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NUCs Analysis</strong></p><p>Nucleotides metabolites profiling analysis was performed on a 6495 LC/TQ mass system and the Agilent MassHunter workstation (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) with the dynamic MRM scan mode. Source-parameter settings in the positive or negative modes were as follows. The capillary voltage was set at 3.5 kV for positive and negative mode, Gas temperature at 120°C, Gas flow at 11 L/min, Nebulizer at 40 psi, Sheath Gas Temperature at 400 °C and the Sheath Gas Flow at 12 L/min.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>TCA Analysis</strong></p><p>Krebs cycle metabolites profiling analysis was performed on a 5977B MSD6495 mass system and the Agilent MassHunter workstation (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) with the dynamic MRM scan mode. Source temperature was set at 250°C and Electron energy at 70eV.</p>	<p><strong>Preprocessing</strong></p><p>The peak area counts were normalized based on their respective internal standard control compounds for each detected metabolite from the GM, Nucleotides, and Krebs cycle. The 'MetaboAnalystR' package (v.4.0.0) (Chong &amp; Xia, 2018; Chong et al., 2019; Pang et al., 2020) was used to filter compounds based on an interquartile range (IQR) cutoff of &lt; 25, removing 10% of the data. Subsequently, quantile normalization was applied, followed by a logarithmic transformation.</p>	<p>In this study, authors performed targeted metabolomics, enabling to identify specific compounds based on their retention time and their mass to charge.</p>

Study Title	Enterohepatic Axis Reprogramming via S1PR2 Inhibition Activates Compensatory Microbiota-Bile Acid Crosstalk to Ameliorate Colitis
Study Description	<p> Studies have revealed that gut microbiota dysbiosis and bile acid metabolism play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease, and there exists a reciprocal interaction between gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism. S1PR2, a G protein-coupled receptor, has been demonstrated to exert pro-inflammatory effects in various diseases. Our findings indicate that the S1PR2 inhibitor JTE-013 ameliorates intestinal inflammation in mice with DSS-induced colitis. However, the precise mechanisms involved remain unclear. Therefore, this study investigates whether JTE-013 ameliorates murine colitis through modulation of the gut microbiota-bile acid metabolism axis.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Intestinal contents&nbsp;from mouse&nbsp;were collected and stored at -80 °C.</p>	<p>An appropriate amount of sample was placed in a 2 mL centrifuge tube, and 400 μL of methanol (-20°C) was accurately added. The mixture was vortexed for 60 s, followed by the addition of two steel beads. The tube was then placed in a tissue grinder and ground at 55 Hz for 1 min. This procedure was repeated at least twice. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to ultrasonication at room temperature for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min. A 300 μL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 600 μL of water and vortexed for 30 s. An appropriate amount of the supernatant was then diluted fivefold with 30% methanol. The diluted supernatant was passed through a 0.22 μm filter membrane, and the filtrate was transferred to a detection vial for analysis.</p>	<p>Separation was performed using an ACQUITY UPLC® BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, USA) with an injection volume of 5 μL and a column temperature of 40°C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.01% formic acid in water and (B) acetonitrile. The gradient elution program was set as follows: 0–4 min, 25% B; 4–9 min, 25–30% B; 9–14 min, 30–36% B; 14–18 min, 36–38% B; 18–24 min, 38–50% B; 24–32 min, 50–75% B; 32–33 min, 75–90% B; 33–35.5 min, 90–25% B. The flow rate was maintained at 0.25 mL/min.</p>	<p>An electrospray ionization (ESI) source was operated in negative ion mode. The ion source temperature was set at 500 °C, and the ion source voltage was -4500 V. The collision gas pressure was 6 psi, the curtain gas pressure was 30 psi, and both the nebulizing gas and auxiliary gas were set at 50 psi. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) was used for detection.</p>	<p>The original files were processed by spectra and database search, and qualitative and quantitative results of metabolites were obtained. Then the quality control analysis of the data is carried out to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data results. Then, multivariate statistical analysis of metabolites was performed to reveal the differences between different groups of metabolites. Hierarchical clustering and metabolite correlation analysis are used to reveal relationships between samples and between metabolites and metabolites. Finally, the biological significance of metabolites was explained through functional analysis such as metabolic pathways.</p>	<p>In this study, targeted metabolomics was employed for the absolute quantification of bile acids. Metabolite identification was primarily based on comparison with commercial reference standards, achieving Level 1 identification confidence. The specific procedures were as follows: First, chromatographic separation was performed using an ACQUITY UPLC® BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) under a set gradient program to distinguish bile acid isomers. Subsequently, data acquisition was carried out using an electrospray ionization source in negative ion mode with multiple reaction monitoring. Metabolite identification was based on the following criteria: retention time matching with reference standards, accurate precursor ion mass (mass deviation &lt; 5 ppm), and characteristic MS/MS fragment ions.</p><p>The following tools and databases were used in this analysis:</p><p>Software: The instrument's built-in data acquisition software was used, and XCMS was utilized for data preprocessing and feature extraction.</p><p>Databases: Metabolite annotation primarily referenced the HMDB and KEGG databases, while the LIPID MAPS database was used to verify the classification information of bile acids.</p><p>Identification Level: All bile acids verified by reference standards were labeled as Level 1 identification.</p>

Study Title	Investigating the Impact of Acinetobacter lwoffii-ECT1/E6E7 Co-culture Metabolites on Cervical Cell Proliferation and Apoptosis via Untargeted Metabolomics.
Study Description	<p>This study employed untargeted metabolomics detection combined with bioinformatics analysis to investigate the metabolites from the co-culture of Acinetobacter lwoffiiand cervical precancerous cells ECT1/E6E7. A. lwoffiiwas co-cultured with ECT1/E6E7 cells at an MOI of 100. The culture supernatants from six groups were collected: ECT1/E6E7 cells cultured alone for 18h (E_18h) and 30h (E_30h), A. lwoffiicultured alone for 18h (L_18h) and 30h (L_30h), and the co-culture of ECT1/E6E7 cells with A. lwoffiifor 18h (E_L_18h) and 30h (E_L_30h). Broad untargeted metabolomics technology was used to detect the differential metabolites in the co-culture. The untargeted metabolomics results showed that the most significant differential metabolites at the intersection of the L group, E group, and E_L group at 18h and 30h of co-culture were gluconic acid and p-Cresol, respectively.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>1. ECT1/E6E7 cells: Purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). These are HPV16/E6E7 gene-positive human cervical epithelial cells and are used as a model for studying SIL (squamous intraepithelial lesions).</p><p>2. Acinetobacter lwoffii: Purchased from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC). It was stored in a -80°C glycerol environment. Prior to co-culture, it was purified, isolated, and proliferated in MRS solid/liquid medium, and cultured under aerobic conditions at 37°C with shaking at 190 rpm. After co-culture, both were routinely cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and no antibiotics, placed in a 37°C cell culture incubator (MOI=100).</p><p>3. The culture supernatants from six groups were collected separately: ECT1/E6E7 cells cultured alone for 18h (E_18h) and 30h (E_30h), A. lwoffii cultured alone for 18h (L_18h) and 30h (L_30h), and the co-culture of ECT1/E6E7 cells with A. lwoffii for 18h (E_L_18h) and 30h (E_L_30h).</p>	<p>1. Thaw the experimental samples at 4°C. After thawing, vortex the samples for 1 min to ensure thorough mixing.</p><p>2. Precisely pipette an appropriate amount of sample into a 2 mL centrifuge tube.</p><p>3. Add 400 µL of methanol solution and vortex for 1 min.</p><p>4. Centrifuge at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min. Take all the supernatant, transfer it to a 2 mL centrifuge tube, and concentrate to dryness.</p><p>5. Accurately add 150 µL of a solution of 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine (4 ppm) prepared in 80% methanol-water to reconstitute the sample. Take the supernatant, filter it through a 0.22 µm membrane, and transfer the filtrate to a detection vial.</p><p>6. Take 20 µL from each sample to be tested and mix them to form a QC (Quality Control) sample. (QC is used to correct biases in the mixed sample analysis results and errors caused by the analytical instrument itself. If the number of experimental samples exceeds 20, QC injection detection will be performed; if the number is 20 or fewer, QC injection detection is not arranged.)</p><p>7. Use the remaining samples to be tested for LC-MS detection.</p>	<p>1.&nbsp;Instrument: Thermo Vanquish ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).</p><p>2.&nbsp;Column: ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column, 1.8 µm particle size, 2.1*100 mm dimensions (Waters, Milford, MA, USA).</p><p>3.&nbsp;Chromatographic Separation Conditions: Column temperature: 40 °C.Flow rate: 0.30 mL/min. Mobile phase: For positive ion mode: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B2) and 0.1% formic acid in water (A2).For negative ion mode: acetonitrile (B3) and 5 mM ammonium formate in water (A3).Injection volume: 2 µL.</p><p>4.&nbsp;Mobile Phase Gradient Elution Program:Positive Ion Mode: 0–1 min: 10% B2.1–5 min: 10%&nbsp;98% B2 (linear gradient). 5–6.5 min: 98% B2 (isocratic hold).6.5–6.6 min: 98%&nbsp;10% B2 (linear gradient).6.6–8 min: 10% B2 (isocratic hold). Negative Ion Mode:0–1 min: 10% B3. 1–5 min: 10%&nbsp;98% B3 (linear gradient).5–6.5 min: 98% B3 (isocratic hold). 6.5–6.6 min: 98%&nbsp;10% B3 (linear gradient).&nbsp;6.6–8 min: 10% B3 (isocratic hold).</p>	<p>1.&nbsp;Instrument: Thermo Q Exactive Focus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).</p><p>2.&nbsp;Mass Spectrometry Conditions: Ion source: Electrospray ionization (ESI), with data acquired in both positive and negative ion modes.Spray voltage: 3.50 kV for positive ion mode; -2.50 kV for negative ion mode.Sheath gas flow rate: 40 arbitrary units (arb).Auxiliary gas flow rate: 10 arb. Capillary temperature: 325 °C. Full scan (MS1): Resolution 70,000; scan range m/z 100–1000. Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS): Performed using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a collision energy of 30 eV. MS/MS resolution: 17,500. Top 3 most intense ions from the MS1 scan were selected for fragmentation.Dynamic exclusion was enabled to avoid unnecessary MS/MS acquisition of repeatedly detected ions.</p>	<p>The raw mass spectrometry data files were converted to the mzXML format using the MS Convert tool within the ProteoWizard software package (v3.0.8789). Peak detection (peaks identification), peak filtering (peaks filtration), and peak alignment (peaks alignment) were performed using the R XCMS package. The main parameters were: bw=2, ppm=15, peakwidth=c(5, 30), mzwid=0.015, mzdiff=0.01, method='centWave'. This process generated a data matrix containing information such as mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), retention time (rt), and peak intensity (area). The data was then exported to Excel for subsequent analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>To enable comparison between data of different magnitudes, quantitative values were corrected using total peak area normalization (Sum peak normalization). Based on the data from Quality Control (QC) samples, the systemic stability throughout the experimental process was monitored, and sample correlation analysis was performed. Furthermore, quality assurance (QA) was conducted on the basis of the QC samples to remove features (peaks) with poor reproducibility in the QC samples. Specifically, features with a relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt; 30% in the QC samples were filtered out.</p>	<p>Multivariate statistical analysis was performed on the data using the R language ropls package, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA), and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA). The resulting score plots, loading plots, and S-plots revealed differences in metabolic composition among different samples. The absence of model overfitting was confirmed by cross-permutation testing. Model cross-validation primarily referred to parameters such as R2X, R2Y, and Q2. Values of R2 and Q2 closer to 1 indicate a better model. Through OPLS-DA analysis, orthogonal variables unrelated to the classification variable in the metabolites can be filtered out, and non-orthogonal and orthogonal variables are analyzed separately, thereby obtaining more reliable information on the degree of inter-group differences in metabolites and their correlation with experimental groups. The Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) value indicates the importance of a metabolite for sample classification discrimination.</p><p><br></p><p>Hierarchical cluster analysis of metabolite relative quantitative values was performed by scaling the dataset using the R language pheatmap package. Metabolites were identified and annotated by querying databases such as the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) (http://www.hmdb.ca), MassBank (http://www.massbank.jp/), LipidMaps (http://www.lipidmaps.org), and mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org). The distribution of differential metabolites across different sample groups was visualized using Volcano Plots. The similarity in the composition of differential metabolites among different groups was displayed using Upset Venn diagrams. The correlation between metabolites was analyzed by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient or Spearman's rank correlation coefficient for each pair of metabolites across different groups. A positive correlation indicates the same trend, while a negative correlation indicates opposite trends. The R language cor() function was used to calculate correlation coefficients, and the cor.test() function was used for statistical testing of metabolite association analysis, with a p-value &lt; 0.05 considered statistically significant. The relative abundance of metabolites at the same level across each sample was assessed using z-score (standard score) normalization. Metabolic pathway enrichment and topological analysis were conducted using the MetaboAnalyst database (www.metaboanalyst.ca) to identify potential metabolic pathways. Subsequently, the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) database was utilized for the analysis and annotation of metabolic pathways involving the identified metabolites.</p>

Study Title	Enhancing sensory quality of heat-not-burn tobacco via bioaugmentation with Cytobacillus oceanisediminis C4 and Amylase
Study Description	<p>Tobacco fermentation is a crucial process for improving tobacco quality by reducing undesirable substances. In this study, an enzyme–microbe combined fermentation strategy was established and evaluated. Cytobacillus oceanisediminis C4 combined with amylase pretreatment was applied in the fermentation of tobacco powder (TP) to improve the quality of tobacco, yielding an enhanced sensory score of 84.50, compared with 82.00 in the untreated TP. Compared with the control, the reducing sugar content increased significantly, whereas starch and protein contents decreased markedly after combined fermentation. Furthermore, total aromatic components of TP increased by 34.77%, along with noticeable changes in TP surface structure. Bacterial community structure analysis revealed a noteworthy shift at the genus level, with the relative abundance of Bacillus and Pseudomonas escalating from 0.30% and 1.15% to 74.67% and 4.40%, respectively. Additionally, fungal community structure analysis revealed that the relative abundance of Monascus surged from 1.69% to 87.59%. Metabolomic analysis demonstrated that fermentation reprogrammed the metabolic profile toward flavor-active and aroma-precursor compounds, characterized by increased levels of amino acids and phenolic acids and decreased levels of lipids and flavonoids. These coordinated changes contributed to enhanced aroma, reduced irritation, and overall improvement in tobacco quality. Overall, this study established an enzyme–microbe combined fermentation process, demonstrated the effectiveness of the process in improving Heat-not-Burn tobacco products, and provided multi-omics insights into the enzyme–microbe combined fermentation process.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>First-cured tobacco samples (KRK26) were collected in 2020 from Yanshan County, Wenshan Prefecture, Yunnan Province, China, and used as experimental materials. The tobacco leaves were crushed at low temperature, passed through a 200-mesh sieve, and then stored at room temperature under light-protected conditions.</p>	<p>The biological samples were first vacuum freeze-dried using a lyophilizer (Scientz-100F), then ground into a fine powder with a mixer mill (MM 400, Retsch) at 30 Hz for 1.5 min. Subsequently, 50 mg of the powder was accurately weighed using an analytical balance (MS105DΜ) and extracted with 1000 μL of 70% methanol containing internal standards; for samples weighing less than 50 mg, the extraction solvent was added proportionally at a ratio of 1000 μL per 50 mg sample. After extraction, 500 μL of petroleum ether was added, followed by vortexing for 5 min, standing for phase separation, and centrifugation at 12,000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C. The entire lower aqueous phase was then collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane into brown autosampler vials with glass inserts and stored at 20 °C until LC–MS/MS analysis. The experimental group consisted of fermented tobacco powder, while the control group was prepared without the addition of either enzymes or bacterial suspension.</p>	<p>Data acquisition was performed using an ExionLC AD ultra-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with a tandem mass spectrometer (UPLC–MS/MS). Metabolites were separated on an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) using a mobile phase consisting of water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B) at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min, a column temperature of 40 °C, and an injection volume of 2 μL. The gradient elution started at 5% B, increased linearly to 95% B over 9 min, was held for 1 min, then returned to 5% B and equilibrated to 14 min.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection was conducted with an electrospray ionization source operated at 550 °C, with ion spray voltages of 5500 V (positive) and 4500 V (negative). Data were acquired in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode using a triple quadrupole mass analyzer with nitrogen as the collision gas, and declustering potentials and collision energies were optimized for each MRM transition.</p>	<p>The raw LC–MS/MS data acquired by the instrument were first exported and initially processed using vendor-provided SCIEX software to convert the raw mass spectrometry files into standardized formats suitable for downstream analysis. The data were then processed through a metabolomics analysis pipeline that included peak detection and extraction, noise removal, retention time alignment across samples, and signal correction to reduce the effects of instrumental drift and systematic variation. During preprocessing, missing values were appropriately imputed (KNN method), and data transformations such as log transformation, mean centering, and unit variance (UV) scaling were applied as required. The processed datasets were subsequently used for metabolite identification and quantification and imported into the R environment, where statistical analyses—including principal component analysis, hierarchical clustering, differential metabolite screening, and functional annotation—were conducted using packages such as prcomp, MetaboAnalystR, and ComplexHeatmap.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed based on a self-built MWDB (MetWare Database) using MS/MS spectral information. During data processing, isotopic signals, redundant adduct ions, and duplicate fragment ions originating from larger precursor molecules were removed. Metabolite quantification was carried out using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode on a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. In MRM mode, the quadrupole first selects the target precursor ion to exclude interfering ions, after which the precursor ion undergoes collision-induced dissociation to generate fragment ions; a specific characteristic fragment ion is then selectively monitored, thereby minimizing interference and improving quantification accuracy and reproducibility. Following acquisition of metabolomic data from different samples, chromatographic peak areas of all detected metabolites were integrated, and peak areas of the same metabolite across different samples were normalized to ensure consistency .</p>

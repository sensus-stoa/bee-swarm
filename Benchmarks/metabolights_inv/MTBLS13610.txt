Study Title	Clinacanthus nutans fractions exert anti-nasopharyngeal carcinoma effects: integrating untargeted metabolomics, network pharmacology, molecular docking and experimental validation
Study Description	<p>Background: Clinacanthus nutans (C. nutans), a Southeast Asian medicinal herb, has ethnopharmacological potential against nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). This study aimed to elucidate the bioactive compounds, potential molecular targets, and regulatory pathways responsible for the anticancer activity of the Clinacanthus nutans leaves ethanol extract (E-CNL) fractions against NPC.</p><p>Methods: HPLC-Q-TOF/MS/MS and GC-MS analyses, combined with chemometric modeling, were employed to comprehensively profile the phytochemical composition of E-CNL fractions. NPC-related targets were retrieved from GEO, GeneCards, and DisGeNET, and integrated into a PPI network constructed via Cytoscape. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to characterize key targets and pathways. Molecular docking (AutoDockTools, PyMOL) was used to assess compound–target binding. Pharmacological validation was conducted using NPC cell lines (HK-1, C666-1, and CNE2).</p><p>Results: Sixty-one compounds and 794 potential targets were identified with 175 overlapping NPC-associated targets. PPI analysis highlighted ten hub genes, including TP53, STAT3, BCL2, CASP3, BCL2L1, MTOR, TNF, AKT1, ALB, and HIF1A. GO terms indicated roles in oxidative stress response, kinase regulation, phosphorylation, and MAPK cascade modulation, while KEGG analysis revealed significant associations with the PI3K–Akt, p53, TNF, FoxO, and VEGF signaling pathways. Molecular docking revealed strong affinities of the key compounds with PIK3CA, AKT1, TP53, and CDK1. In vitro assays confirmed that E-CNL fractions significantly inhibited NPC cell proliferation and migration, supporting their potential therapeutic relevance.</p><p>Conclusion: E-CNL fractions exert their anti-NPC effects through multicomponent, multi-target, and multi-pathway mechanisms. These findings provide a scientific foundation for the further exploration of E-CNL fractions as promising treatment agents for NPC.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>1. Plant-material collection</p><p>Dried CNL were obtained from Herbagus Sdn Bhd (Bertam, Penang, Malaysia). Plant specimens were kept in the Herbarium Unit, School of Biological Sciences, Universiti Sains Malaysia, Penang, Malaysia (Voucher no. SK 1980/11). An ultra-centrifugal mill ZM200 (Retsch, Haan, Germany) was used to finely grind the dried CNL into a powder. The resulting powder was sealed in a plastic bag and stored at 4°C until further use.</p><p>2. E-CNL preparation</p><p>CNL powder was evenly distributed in ethanol, maintaining a consistent plant powder-to-solvent ratio (weight/volume = 1:6 g/mL). The mixture was ultrasonically extracted for 30 min at 35°C by using a KQ-600KDE high-power CNC ultrasonic cleaner (Kunshanshumei, China). The ultrasonic frequency was set to 40 kHz, with a power supply of 480 W. The extraction procedure was repeated thrice. The extracts were then filtered, vacuum-evaporated, freeze-dried, and stored at 20°C until further analysis.</p><p>3. E-CNL fractions preparation</p><p>The resulting E-CNL was sequentially fractionated thrice with n-hexane, dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, methanol, and water. The combined extracts obtained from thrice successive solvent extractions were pooled, concentrated, and dried. This process yielded the corresponding n-hexane, dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, methanol, and water fractions of the E-CNL, which are denoted as H-CNLs, DCM-CNLs, EA-CNLs, M-CNLs, and W-CNLs, respectively.</p>	<p>Phase separation was conducted to obtain polar and nonpolar metabolites following a modified extraction protocol based on the method of Matyash[19,20]. Briefly, 2 mg E-CNL fractions (H-CNLs, DCM-CNLs, EA-CNLs, M-CNLs, and W-CNLs) was dissolved in 600 μL of pre-chilled methanol and vortexed for 1 min at room temperature to ensure complete dissolution. Subsequently, 2.0 mL of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) was added, and the mixture was gently agitated at room temperature for 1 h. Then, 600 μL of water was added and the mixture was phase separated, followed by a 10 min resting period and centrifugation at 1000 × g for another 10 min at 4 °C to achieve complete phase separation. The both upper organic layer (nonpolar fraction) and the lower aqueous layer (polar fraction) were cautiously transferred into separate 2 mL polypropylene tubes.</p><p>Each fraction was then filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 5 min to remove particulates before injection. The resulting supernatants were transferred into glass vials for LC–MS/MS and GC–MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared with pooling equal aliquots from all samples to ensure analytical reproducibility across the metabolomics workflow. All samples (including QC) injection according to the sequence that randomized order generated via an randomization tool (https://www.random.org/lists) to minimize systematic bias. Relative standard deviation (RSD) analysis of detected feature peak areas in QC injections was performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/home.xhtml).</p>	<p>1. LC-MS/MS analysis</p><p>An Agilent 1200 Infinity Series ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to a micrOTOF-Q III High Definition Mass Spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) was used to analyze the E-CNL fractions samples. Chromatography separation was performed in a reversed-phase SB C18 column (4.6 mm × 50 mm, 1.8 μm; Agilent ZORBAX, USA) kept at 40 °C. The mobile-phase flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, the sample injection volume was 10 μL. All samples were analyzed in positive and negative ESI modes.</p><p>For the lipidomic analysis of the upper phase, mobile phase A comprised acetonitrile/water (60:40, v/v), whereas mobile phase B comprised isopropanol/acetonitrile (90:10, v/v), both with 0.1% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate. In the positive-ion mode, the gradient elution proceeded as follows: 0-5.0 min 15%-50% B; 5.0-11.0 min 50%-55% B; 11.0-18.0 min 55%-97%B; 18.0-27.0 min 97%-100% B; 27.0-28.0 min 100% B; 28.0-29.0 min 100%-15% B; 29.0-30.0 min 15% B. In the negative-ion mode, the gradient elution proceeded as follows: 0-1.0 min 25% B; 1.0-2.0 min 25%-40% B; 2.0-5.0 min 40%-65% B; 5.0-9.5 min 65%-97% B; 9.5-14.0 min 97% B; 14.0-15.0 min 97%-25% B; 15.0-16.0 min 25% B. For the metabolomic analysis of lower phase, water with 0.1 % formic acid was mobile phase A, and acetonitrile was mobile phase B. The gradient elution of positive-ion mode followed this pattern: 0-6.0 min 10% B; 6.0-10.0 min 10%-70% B; 10.0-13.0 min 70%-97% B; 13.0-20.0 min 97% B; 20.0-21.0 min 97%-10% B; 21.0-22.0 min 10% B. The gradient elution of negative-ion mode followed this pattern: 0-6.0 min 15% B; 6.0-10.0 min 15%-70% B; 10.0-13.0 min 70%-97% B; 13.0-20.0 min 97% B; 20.0-21.0 min 97%-15% B; 21.0-22.0 min 15% B.</p><p>2. GC-MS analysis</p><p>GC–MS was performed on an Agilent Technologies 7890A GC System equipped with a 5975C inert XL EI/CI MSD featuring a Triple-Axis Detector (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). Instrument control and raw data acquisition were managed through the GC–MSD Workstation software. Separation occurred on an HP-5MS column with 5% phenyl methyl siloxane (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent Technologies, MA, USA).&nbsp;</p><p>A 2 μL aliquot of sample was introduced in splitless mode. Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The initial column temperature was set at 70 °C for 4 min and then increased to 190 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min. Subsequently, the temperature was increased to 250 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min and then further raised to 300 °C at 4 °C/min, where it was ultimately held for 5 min. The entire analysis process lasted 33.5 min, with the solvent delay set at 3 min.</p>	<p>1. LC-MS/MS analysis</p><p>The mass spectrometry (MS) parameters were optimized as follows: the capillary voltage was set to 4500 V for ESI (+) and 2500 V for ESI (-). Ion transfer times were adjusted to 70 μs for ESI (+) and 100 μs for ESI (-). The nebulizer gas pressure was maintained at 8.0 bar, with a drying gas flow rate of 8.0 L/min and a temperature of 200 °C. Nitrogen served as both the drying and nebulizing gas. Spectral data were recorded in full-scan mode over an m/z range of 50–1000 Da. Prior to each analytical run, internal mass calibration was automatically performed using an injected calibrant. Data acquisition and instrument control were managed via MicrOTOF control software.</p><p>2. GC-MS analysis</p><p>The injector was held at 250 °C, while the auxiliary temperature was maintained at 280 °C. Mass spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 60–800. The electron ionization (EI) source and quadrupole were operated at 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively.</p>	<p>1. LC-MS/MS analysis</p><p>The collection of LC–MS/MS raw data was conducted using Bruker DataAnalysis software (version 4.4). The raw spectra were initially saved in Bruker’s proprietary .d format and subsequently converted to .abf format through the Abf Converter tool following acceptance of the CompassXtract license agreement.&nbsp;</p><p>2. GC-MS analysis</p><p>Raw chromatographic data were processed using the MassHunter Workstation software (Agilent Technologies, California, USA), where spectral deconvolution and peak extraction were performed to generate the initial data matrix (Supplementary Table S5).</p>	<p>1. LC-MS/MS analysis</p><p>The converted data (.abf format) were further analyzed in MS-DIAL software (RIKEN, version 4.92) for both metabolomics and lipidomics workflow analysis. The identification and annotation of compounds were accomplished by matching the acquired mass spectra against the default MS reference databases. The aligned data matrix (Supplementary Tables S1–S4) was exported from MS-DIAL for subsequent statistical analyses.</p><p>2. GC-MS analysis</p><p>Compounds annotation and identification were accomplished by matching acquired spectra with reference patterns from the NIST05 mass spectral library.</p>

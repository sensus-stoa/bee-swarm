Study Title	Dietary germ oil-derived phytosterols ameliorate hyperlipidemia by suppressing bile salt hydrolase-producing gut microbiota to enrich taurohyodeoxycholic acid_Bile acid profile of the ileal content
Study Description	<p>Dyslipidemia is a major cardiovascular risk factor. While dietary phytosterols are known to lower cholesterol, the mechanisms involving the gut-liver axis are not fully understood. By integrating human clinical trials and mechanistic animal models, we demonstrate that phytosterols improve lipid profiles by modulating the gut microbiota-bile acid-farnesoid X receptor (FXR) axis. Phytosterol supplementation suppresses the abundance of bile salt hydrolase-active bacteria, such as Lactobacillus, leading to reduced intestinal enzymatic activity and the accumulation of conjugated bile acids. These bile acids act as intestinal FXR antagonists, downregulating the ileal fibroblast growth factor 15 (FGF15) signaling pathway. This suppression relieves feedback inhibition on hepatic bile acid synthesis, thereby accelerating cholesterol catabolism. Fecal microbiota transplantation validates that these metabolic benefits are gut microbiota dependent. Together, these findings link dietary phytosterols to host lipid metabolism through gut microbial bile acid regulation, providing a mechanistic framework for individualized, food-based strategies to manage dyslipidemia.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Following isolation from the rat ileum, the intestinal content was stored at -80°C. For analysis, aliquots were taken and kept on dry ice prior to and during the assay.</p>	<p>A 25 mg aliquot of each individual sample was precisely weighed and transferred to an Eppendorf tube&nbsp;(Table S0).&nbsp;After the addition of 1000 μL of extract solution (precooled at -40&nbsp;℃, acetonitrile-methanol-water = 2:2:1, containing internal standard), the samples were vortexed for 30 s, homogenized at 35 Hz for 4 min, and sonicated for 5 min in ice-water bath. The homogenate and sonicate circle was repeated for three times, followed by incubation at -40&nbsp;℃&nbsp;for 1 h and centrifugation at 12000 rpm and 4&nbsp;℃&nbsp;for 15 min.&nbsp;The resulting supernatants were transferred to LC-MS vials for UHPLC-MS/MS analysis.</p>	<p>The UHPLC separation was carried out using an UHPLC System (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC&nbsp;BEH C18&nbsp;column (150 * 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters). The mobile phase A was 5 mmol/L ammonium acetate in water, and the mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The column temperature was set at 45&nbsp;℃. The auto-sampler temperature was set at 4&nbsp;℃&nbsp;and the injection volume was 1 μL.</p>	<p>A&nbsp;Orbitrap Exploris 120&nbsp;mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was applied for assay development. Typical ion source parameters were:&nbsp;spray voltage = +3500/-3200 V, sheath gas (N2) flow rate = 40, aux gas (N2) flow rate = 15, sweep gas (N2) flow rate = 0, aux gas (N2) temperature = 350&nbsp;℃,&nbsp;capillary temperature = 320&nbsp;℃.</p>	<p>The&nbsp;parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) parameters&nbsp;for each of the targeted analytes were optimized, by injecting the standard solutions of the individual analytes, into the API source of the mass spectrometer. Since most of the analytes did not show product ion acceptable for quantification, the precursor ion in high resolution was selected for quantification.</p>	<p>The&nbsp;parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) parameters&nbsp;for each of the targeted analytes were optimized, by injecting the standard solutions of the individual analytes, into the API source of the mass spectrometer.

Study Title	Dietary lactoferrin supplementation enhances immune function, improves intestinal integrity and reshapes gut microbiota in kittens
Study Description	<p> Background:&nbsp;Lactoferrin is a natural iron-binding glycoprotein with antimicrobial, immunomodulatory and barrier-protective activities, and it is widely applied in human nutritional supplements and infant food additives, but its application potential in pet food remains undetermined.&nbsp;This study investigated the influence of dietary&nbsp;lactoferrin on immune functions, intestinal&nbsp;barrier integrity and gut microbiota in kittens.</p><p> Results:&nbsp;A total of 24 healthy kittens aged 5-10 months were divided into three groups (n = 8) and fed with a commercial basal diet (CON&nbsp;group) and the basal diet supplemented with 500 or 1000 mg/kg lactoferrin (L-LF group or H-LF&nbsp;group), respectively, for 42 days. Blood was collected from individual&nbsp;kittens on days&nbsp;21 and 42,&nbsp;and feces of each animal were collected on day 42.&nbsp;Compared with the CON&nbsp;group, the L-LF&nbsp;and H-LF groups increased immunoglobulin A levels&nbsp;on day 21 (P&nbsp;&lt; 0.05). The H-LF group showed&nbsp;lower serum IL-6 level on day 21, and both L-LF and H-LF groups&nbsp;reduced&nbsp;IL-1β concentration&nbsp;on day 42 (P&nbsp;&lt; 0.05), in contrast with the CON group. Additionally, serum levels of lipopolysaccharide and zonulin were decreased in the&nbsp;H-LF group relative to the CON group on day 42 (P&nbsp;&lt; 0.05). Untargeted metabolomics analysis revealed that dietary lactoferrin altered serum metabolite profiles and increased&nbsp;levels of sphingosine 1-phosphate, proline betaine, L-argininium(1+), and carnitine. The L-LF and&nbsp;H-LF groups&nbsp;were enriched in 5 and 6 differential metabolic pathways, respectively. Metagenomic analysis of fecal samples indicated that both the L-LF and H-LF groups&nbsp;had a higher relative abundance of Bacteroidota&nbsp;than the CON group (P&nbsp;&lt; 0.05). The H-LF group also increased the relative abundance of Prevotellaceae, Prevotella, Bifidobacterium&nbsp;and Segatella&nbsp;copri&nbsp;in the feces of kittens (P&nbsp;&lt; 0.05).&nbsp;Moreover, the abundance of&nbsp;Escherichia coli&nbsp;in both L-LF and H-LF groups declined compared with the CON group (P&nbsp;&lt; 0.05).&nbsp;Furthermore, KEGG pathways&nbsp;of secondary metabolite biosynthesis, nucleotide sugar biosynthesis, and sphingolipid metabolism were significantly enriched in kittens fed with lactoferrin.</p><p><strong>Conclusions: </strong>Dietary lactoferrin improved the immune function, strengthened intestinal barrier integrity&nbsp;and remodeled gut microbiota in kittens,&nbsp;highlighting its potential as a functional additive in kitten food.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>On the 42nd day of the experiment, after 12 hours of fasting, blood samples were collected from the forelimb veins of kittens and placed in vacuum blood collection tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Subsequently, these samples were centrifuged at 3000 revolutions per minute (rpm) at 4 °C for 10 minutes to obtain serum for untargeted metabolomics analysis.</p>	<p>100 μL of serum was pipetted into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, and 400 μL of extraction solution (acetonitrile: methanol = 1:1, v/v) containing four internal standards (L-2-chlorophenylalanine at 0.02 mg/mL) was then added. After vortex mixing for 30 seconds, low-temperature ultrasonic extraction was performed for 30 minutes at 5°C and 40 KHz, and then the samples were stored at -20°C for 30 minutes. Subsequently, samples were centrifuged at 13,000 × g and 4°C for 15 minutes, and the supernatants were transferred and dried with nitrogen. The residue was reconstituted with 100 μL of reconstitution solution (acetonitrile:methanol = 1:1, v/v), followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction at 5°C and 40 KHz for 5 minutes. The samples were then centrifuged at 13,000 × g and 4°C for 10 minutes, and the resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to an injection vial with an inner liner for subsequent instrumental analysis.</p>	<p>The chromatographic column was ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA). Mobile phase A consisted of 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), and mobile phase B consisted of 47.5% acetonitrile + 47.5% isopropanol + 5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid). The injection volume was 3 μL, and the column temperature was 40°C.</p>	<p>The samples were ionized by electrospray ionization, and mass spectrometry signals were collected in both positive and negative ion scanning modes, with a mass scan range of 70-1050 m/z.</p><p><br></p>	<p>After LC-MS analysis, the raw data were imported into the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak detection, peak integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment. A data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and peak intensity was finally obtained. Meanwhile, the MS and MS/MS spectral information was matched against the public metabolomic databases HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca), Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and the self-built database of Majorbio to acquire metabolite information.</p><p>The database-searched data matrix was uploaded to the Majorbio Cloud Platform (cloud.majorbio.com) for further analysis.</p>	<p>The identification of significantly differential metabolites in untargeted metabolomics was based on the variable importance in projection (VIP) values derived from the orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model and the P-values determined by the student’s t-test. Metabolites with VIP &gt; 1 and P &lt; 0.05 were defined as significantly differential metabolites.</p>

Study Title	Circadian disruption exacerbates MASH in mice by reducing intestinal Akkermansia muciniphila abundance via the FXR-CYP7A1-bile acid axis_Targeting Bile Acids_Untargeted Serum Metabolomics
Study Description	<p>Circadian disruption has emerged as a significant public health issue and is increasingly recognized as a contributor to the progression of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), though the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, we established a mouse model combining continuous light exposure and a western diet to investigate how circadian disruption exacerbates metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). Our results demonstrated that circadian disturbance aggravated hepatic inflammation, lipid accumulation, and intestinal barrier impairment. Through integrated 16S rRNA sequencing and metabolomic analyses, we identified a marked reduction in Akkermansia muciniphila abundance and an elevation in CDCA-3S. Supplementation with A. muciniphila, taurine, or the FXR agonist obeticholic acid restored microbial and metabolic homeostasis, subsequently alleviating MASH pathology. Mechanistically, our data suggest these beneficial effects may involve the FXR–CYP7A1 signaling pathway. Our findings indicate that circadian disruption aggravates MASH potentially through reducing A. muciniphila abundance in a process associated with the FXR–CYP7A1–bile acid axis , and highlight the potential of microbial and metabolic interventions as novel therapeutic strategies for individuals with sleep-related circadian disorders.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The LC analysis was performed on a Vanquish UHPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).</p><p>Chromatography was carried out with an ACQUITY UPLC ® HSS T3 (2.1×100 mm, 1.8 µm)</p><p>(Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The column maintained at 40 ℃. The flow rate and injection volume</p><p>were set at 0.3 mL/min and 2 μL, respectively. For LC-ESI (+)-MS analysis, the mobile phases</p><p>consisted of (B2) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (v/v) and (A2) 0.1% formic acid in water (v/v).</p><p>Separation was conducted under the following gradient: 0~1 min, 10% B2; 1~5 min, 10%~98% B2;</p><p>5~6.5 min, 98% B2; 6.5~6.6 min, 98%~10% B2; 6.6~8 min, 10% B2. For LC-ESI (-)-MS analysis,</p><p>the analytes was carried out with (B3) acetonitrile and (A3) ammonium formate (5mM).</p><p>Separation was conducted under the following gradient: 0~1 min, 10% B3; 1~5 min, 10%~98% B3;</p><p>5~6.5 min, 98% B3; 6.5~6.6 min, 98%~10% B3; 6.6~8 min, 10% B3</p>	<p>The pretreatment of LC/MS raw data was performed by&nbsp;Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA)&nbsp;software, and a three-dimensional data matrix in CSV format was exported. The information in this three-dimensional matrix included: sample information, metabolite name and mass spectral response intensity. Internal standard peaks, as well as any known false positive peaks (including noise, column bleed, and derivatized reagent peaks), were removed from the data matrix, deredundant and peak pooled.&nbsp;</p>	<p>1. Thaw the experimental sample at 4°C, vortex the sample for 1 min after thawing, and mix evenly;</p><p>2. Accurately transfer an appropriate amount of sample into a 2 mL centrifuge tube;</p><p>3. Add 400 μL methanol and vortex for 1 min;</p><p>4. Centrifuge for 10 min at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C,take all the supernatant transfer it to a new 2 mL centrifuge tube, concentrate and dry it;</p><p>5. Add 150 μL of 2-chloro-l-phenylalanine (4 ppm) solution prepared with 80% methanol water to redissolve the sample, filter the supernatant by 0.22 μm membrane and transfer into the detection bottle for LC-MS detection.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection of metabolites was performed on Orbitrap Exploris 120 (Thermo</p><p>Fisher Scientific, USA) with ESI ion source. Simultaneous MS1 and MS/MS (Full MS-ddMS2 mode,</p><p>data-dependent MS/MS) acquisition was used. The parameters were as follows: sheath gas</p><p>pressure, 40 arb; aux gas flow, 10 arb; spray voltage, 3.50 kV and -2.50 kV for ESI(+) and ESI(-),</p><p>respectively; capillary temperature, 325 ℃; MS1 range, m/z 100-1000; MS1 resolving power,</p><p>60000 FWHM; number of data dependant scans per cycle, 4; MS/MS resolving power, 15000 FWHM; normalized collision energy, 30%; dynamic exclusion time, automatic[2]</p>	<p>At the same time, the metabolites were identified by searching database, and the main databases were the&nbsp;HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/), Metlin ( https://metlin.scripps.edu/)&nbsp;.&nbsp;</p>	<p>Mouse Portal Vein Blood Collection</p><p>Following adequate anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of 3.5% chloral hydrate, the mouse was placed in a supine position and immobilized on a surgical board. The abdominal surgical area was shaved and disinfected. An incision was made along the midline to open the skin and peritoneum, carefully entering the abdominal cavity. Using sterile cotton swabs, the intestines were gently retracted to the animal's left side to fully expose the portal vein-mesenteric venous system. Under direct visualization, a 1 mL sterile syringe with the needle bevel facing upwards was used. The needle was carefully inserted into the main portal vein at an angle of approximately 30 degrees, near the confluence of the splenic and superior mesenteric veins. The syringe plunger was withdrawn slowly and steadily to collect the outflowing blood. Throughout the procedure, rapid aspiration or the introduction of air bubbles was meticulously avoided to prevent hemolysis or disruption of blood flow. Upon completion of collection, the needle was promptly withdrawn, and light pressure was applied to the puncture site with a sterile dry cotton swab for hemostasis. The collected blood sample was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant (serum/plasma) was aliquoted and immediately stored in a -80°C ultra-low temperature freezer.</p>

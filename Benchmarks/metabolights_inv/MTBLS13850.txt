Study Title	Polystyrene nanoparticles promote endometrial cancer development through the ACSS2-mediated reprogramming of arachidonic acid metabolism
Study Description	<p>Microplastics and nanoplastics are commonly found in our everyday environments. So far, microplastics and nanoplastics have been detected in various tissues and bodily fluids, including hair, sputum, digestive tissue, lungs, blood, and placental tissue. Although some studies indicate that microplastics and nanoplastics can induce tumor development, their impact on endometrial cancer (EC) remains unclear. In this study, we examined the effect of polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NPs) on EC development and explored the underlying pathogenic mechanisms. We observed the uptake and accumulation of PS-NPs in HEC-1B cells and EC organoids. Using cellular assays and mouse models, we demonstrated that PS-NP exposure can accelerate EC progression in vitro and in vivo. Next, through transcriptomic sequencing and targeted metabolomic sequencing, we found that adenosine 5'-pyrophosphate activated protein kinase (AMPK) can activate ACSS2, allowing its entry into the nucleus and increasing the histone acetylation of the PLA2G3 gene. This induces PLA2G3 mRMA upregulation, increases arachidonic acid(AA) production, and ultimately leads to the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in EC cells. The relevant molecular markers in this study can provide new strategies for early warning and targeted intervention, reducing the potential impact of PS-NPs on EC.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Lipid separation was performed on an ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system, specifically the SCIEX ExionLC AD. The analysis used a Thermo Accucore C30 reverse-phase column (2.6 μm particle size, 2.1 mm internal diameter × 100 mm length). The mobile phase consisted of two solvents: solvent A was a mixture of acetonitrile and water (60:40, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid and 10 mmol/L ammonium formate; solvent B was a mixture of acetonitrile and isopropanol (10:90, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid and 10 mmol/L ammonium formate. A gradient elution program was applied at a constant flow rate of 0.35 mL/min as follows: 0 min, 80% A; 2.0 min, 70% A; 4.0 min, 40% A; 9.0 min, 15% A; 14.0 min, 10% A; 15.5 min, 5% A; 17.3 min, 5% A; 17.5 min, 80% A; 20.0 min, 80% A. The column temperature was maintained at 45°C, and the injection volume was 2 μL.</p>	<p>The raw mass spectrometric data were processed using Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). Initially, chromatographic peaks for each lipid in every sample were integrated to obtain peak areas. Quantification was performed using the internal standard (IS) method, where the peak area ratio of each analyte to its corresponding IS was calculated. These ratios were then normalized against the protein concentration of each sample (determined by BCA assay) to account for biomass differences, yielding lipid content expressed as pmol/mg protein. For subsequent statistical and multivariate analyses (e.g., PCA, OPLS-DA), the normalized abundance data were subjected to Unit Variance (UV) Scaling (also known as Auto Scaling or Z-score normalization), transforming the data to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one for each lipid across samples. In some modeling steps, Mean-Centering was also applied. All statistical analyses and data transformations were performed using R software (version 3.5.1 or above) with specific packages including</p>	<p>Lipid extraction was performed using a methyl tert-butyl ether-based method. Briefly, cell pellets were extracted with 500 μL of MTBE/methanol mixture containing internal standards. After vortexing and sonication, phase separation was induced by adding 100 μL of water. The upper organic layer was collected, evaporated to dryness under nitrogen, and reconstituted in 200 μL of solvent for LC-MS/MS analysis. For quality control, a pooled QC sample was prepared by combining equal aliquots from all experimental samples and was injected at regular intervals (every 10 samples) throughout the analytical sequence</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection was performed on a SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ triple quadrupole-linear ion trap mass spectrometer, which was coupled online to the UPLC system. Ions were generated using an Electrospray Ionization (ESI) source operated in both positive and negative ionization modes with rapid polarity switching. The ion source parameters were set as follows: ion source temperature (TEM): 500 °C; ion spray voltage (IS): +5500 V (positive) and -4500 V (negative); nebulizer gas (GS1): 45 psi; turbo gas (GS2): 55 psi; curtain gas (CUR): 35 psi. Data were acquired in the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode for targeted quantification. For each lipid analyte, optimized compound-specific parameters, including the declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE), were applied. The instrument was controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software.</p>	<p>Lipid identification was performed using a targeted approach based on an in-house MS/MS spectral library (MWDB, MetWare Database). Primary identification relied on matching two key parameters: 1) the precursor ion (m/z) and 2) the characteristic product ion(s) acquired in Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) mode with the library entries. For major lipid species, the retention time (RT) was also compared with that of available authentic standards for additional confirmation. Following initial identification, lipids were annotated and functionally characterized using public metabolite databases, primarily the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), to obtain compound names, classification, and pathway information. The workflow was supported by data processing in Analyst 1.6.3 software and subsequent bioinformatics analysis using R software with relevant packages.</p>	<p>The sample source is human endometrial cancer cell line HEC-1B cell pellets. They were divided into control group (6 replicates) and experimental group (6 replicates). The collected and treated cell pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of ultrapure water containing protease inhibitors, etc. 50 μL of suspension was then taken for lipid extraction and the remaining 50 μL was used for protein concentration determination. After extraction and concentration, the samples used for lipid analysis are stored at -80°C until they are analyzed on the machine.</p>

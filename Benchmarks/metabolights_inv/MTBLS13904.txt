Study Title	Comprehensive metabolomics reveals defence-driven metabolic reprogramming via withanoside biosynthesis under insect herbivory in Withania somnifera
Study Description	<p>Plants exhibit dynamic physiological and biochemical responses to herbivory that often involve extensive metabolic reprogramming. Withania somnifera (Ashwagandha), a globally important medicinal plant, is susceptible to herbivory by the pest Henosepilachna vigintioctopunctata, resulting in significant yield losses. Understanding herbivory-induced metabolic responses is essential for elucidating plant-insect interactions and identifying potential defense-associated metabolites. In this study, we investigated the metabolic responses of W. somnifera leaves to H. vigintioctopunctata infestation using comprehensive metabolomics approach combining liquid chromatography-high-resolution accurate mass spectrometry (LC-HRAMS) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Our findings revealed that herbivore triggers a significant metabolic shift by upregulating secondary metabolites, withanosides and phenylpropanoids. More importantly, hormones such as IAA and salicylic acid are involved in the signaling of defense mechanisms in W. somnifera. Furthermore, primary metabolites from carbohydrate metabolism were significantly affected. In addition, elemental analysis using ICP-OES revealed the accumulation of micronutrients due to pest infestation in W. somnifera. Notably, we also found plant defense metabolites specifically withanolides and withanosides within the herbivore pests themselves. The accumulation of withanosides in response to pest infestation was further confirmed through gene expression analysis. Additionally, feeding on Withania leaf extract containing withanolides adversely affected beetle physiology and survival. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that herbivory induces substantial metabolic plasticity in W. somnifera, particularly involving withanolide glycosylation and phenylpropanoid metabolism. This study provides a valuable framework for understanding herbivory-associated metabolic responses and identifies candidate pathways for future functional and comparative studies.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>4.2 Plant material, insect collection, plant growth conditions, infestation setup, and sample collection</strong></p><p><strong>4.2.1 Collection and cultivation of Withania somnifera NMITLI-118 seeds</strong></p><p>Seeds of Withania somnifera (NMITLI-118) were collected from National Gene Bank of CSIR-CIMAP, Lucknow. The variety used for the present study, NMITLI-118 is a widely grown cultivar of Withania somnifera known for its high withanolides content suitable for herbal medicinal market. Plants were grown in the experimental field for performing actual infestation studies.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>4.2.2 Collection of insects (Henosepilachna vigintioctopunctata)</strong></p><p>Adult H. vigintioctopunctata beetles were collected from the fields of Withania somnifera. After collection, the insects were maintained under controlled laboratory conditions (25 ± 2°C, 60–70% RH, 12:12 h light-dark cycle) on an artificial diet consisting of 5% sucrose until the experiments were conducted.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>4.2.3 Plant growth in CSIR-CIMAP experimental field</strong></p><p>W. somnifera plants were grown in an isolated experimental field of CSIR-CIMAP (located at 26.5ºN latitude and 80.5ºE longitude, 120 m above mean sea level). The field soil comprised of sandy loam and vermicompost. Plants were grown for 4-months in standard growth conditions with temperature range from 23-27ºC (day) and 18-22ºC (night). The mature W. somnifera plants were then selected for infestation experiments. A total of 20 plants were selected for the infestation experiments (10 control and 10 infestation group). Both control (healthy) and infestation group plants (n = 10 each) were enclosed individually in large transparent polythene cages (50 × 70 cm) fitted with fine mesh vent to allow air exchange while preventing insect escape. This setup ensured comparable microclimatic conditions between control and infested groups. For infestation, 10 adult Henosepilachna vigintioctopunctata beetles (starved for 3 h prior to release) were introduced into each enclosed plant, while control plants were similarly bagged without beetles. Beetle activity and visible feeding damage were monitored daily to ensure consistent infestation levels. After a 15-day infestation period, fully expanded mature leaves were sampled from both control and infested plants at the same time of day for physiological and metabolomic analyses. The samples were immediately flash -frozen in liquid nitrogen (L-N2) and stored at -80ºC until analysis. Each plant was treated as an independent biological replicate. The adult beetles infesting on 4 months old plant leaves for 5-days were collected and immediately flash-frozen in liquid N2 and stored at -80ºC until further analysis.</p>	<p><strong>4.7.2 Chromatographic parameters for GC-MS analysis</strong></p><p>GC-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 8890 gas chromatograph coupled with an Agilent 7010C (Triple Quadrupole) mass spectrometer. The Agilent HP-5MS column, having dimensions of 30 M × 250 µm × 0.25 µm was used for achieving the peak separation. Helium in the split ratio of 6:1 and a flow rate of 0.9 mL/min was used as the carrier gas. Injection volume was set to 1 uL. The total run time of the method was 24 minutes. The running condition for the samples was 70ºC for 2 minutes as an initial hold and a heating ramp rate of 12.5ºC/minute until the temperature reached 295ºC, and finally, with a ramp rate of 25ºC/minute, the temperature reached 320ºC, with 3 minutes as a final hold. The instrument method included a solvent delay of 6.8 minutes. MSD transfer line temperature was set at 280ºC.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>4.8.2 Chromatographic parameters for LC-MS analysis</strong></p><p>The UPLC-MS analysis was performed in Vanquish Easy LC coupled with Orbitrap ExplorisTM 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA USA). The UPLC separation was performed using a Waters Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm × 150 mm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) maintained at 40ºC. Gradient elution was achieved using two solvent systems composed of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile (B) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/minute. The 30 minutes UPLC gradient elution program was as follows: (i) 0-75% solvent (B) from 0 to 20 minutes (ii) 75% solvent (B) from 20-25 minutes (iii) 5% solvent (B) from 26 to 30 minutes of total run time. The injection volume was 2 µL.</p>	<p><strong>4.9 Metabolomics data processing and analysis</strong></p><p><br></p><p>GC-MS and LC-MS based metabolomics data matrices were further analyzed using multivariate data analysis (MVDA) tools. The final data matrices were first normalized and Pareto-scaled for removing the unwanted systematic errors due to instrument variability or matrix affects. The scaled and normalized data was further processed for multivariate analysis using the R platform. Principal component analysis was performed for reducing the dimensionality of the complete dataset and making it easier to interpret. For identification of differentially regulated metabolites, PLS-DA was performed (Westerhuis et al., 2008). To assess the quality and cross-validate the PLS-DA model, R2 and Q2 values were employed. The R2 value was used to evaluate the model's fit to the original data, while the Q2 value measured the consistency between the original data and the cross-validated predicted data. In addition to this, the PLS-DA model was further validated through permutation tests (Westerhuis et al., 2008). The variable importance in projection (VIP) scores generated by the PLS-DA model were used to identify the most significantly altered metabolites, particularly those with VIP values greater than 1 (Xia et al., 2021). Univariate analysis was performed using student’s t-test for comparing the targeted metabolites across both groups.</p>	<p><strong>4.7.1 Sample preparation for GC-MS</strong></p><p>For metabolite profiling using GC-MS, leaf samples were first freeze-dried in a lyophilizer (Christ Alpha 1-2 LD Plus) and then ground to powder using a pestle and mortar. Extraction process was performed as per the protocol reported in our previous study (Verma et al., 2025). 100 mg of powdered samples were extracted with 1.2 mL of 70% methanol with 10 ppm uniform 13C6-glucose as an internal standard. The extraction process included 10 minutes of vortex at 2200 RPM in a multitube vortexer followed by sonication for 15 minutes at room temperature in an ultrasonic water bath subsequently followed by vigorous vortexing for 1 hour at 2200 RPM. Samples were then centrifuged for 20 minutes at 13000 RPM at 20ºC. The resultant supernatant was collected and the pellets were resuspended with the extraction solution and the above process was repeated for double extraction. The extracted samples were collected, and concentrated using a vacuum concentrator (Concentrator Plus-Centrifuge Concentrator, Eppendorf SE Hamburg/Germany). The lyophilized samples were then derivatized by adding 60 μL of 20 mg/mL methoxyamine hydrochloride in pyridine solution in each sample followed by vortexing at 2200 RPM. Then, the samples were simultaneously incubated and shaken at 65ºC for 1 hour at 800 RPM in a thermomixer (ThermoMixer C, Eppendorf SE Hamburg/Germany). After thermo-mixing, 120 μL of MSTFA (N-Methyl-N-(Trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide) was added in each sample and the samples were again simultaneously incubated and shaken at 70ºC for 1 hour at 800 RPM. The derivatized samples were then centrifuged at 12000 RPM for 20 minutes at room temperature. The supernatant was collected and transferred to a GC-MS vial with proper labelling. The samples were then proceeded for GC-MS analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>4.8.1 Sample preparation for LC-MS analysis</strong></p><p>For UPLC-MS based metabolite profiling, leaf samples that were freeze-dried in a lyophilizer (Christ Alpha 1-2 LDPlus) and grounded to powder using pestle and mortar were utilized. 100mg of powdered samples were extracted with 1.0 mL of 100% ethanol with 10ppm of uniform 13C6-glucose as an internal standard. The triple extraction process included vortexing for 15 minutes at 2200 RPM in a multitube vortexer. The samples were then heated in a water bath for 1 hour at 55ºC. The heating was followed by vortexing for 20 minutes at 2200 RPM subsequently followed by sonication of the samples in an ultrasonic water bath for 15 minutes at max frequency at room temperature. Then the samples were centrifuged at 13000 RPM for 15 minutes at 20ºC. 850 μL of supernatant was collected and the pellets were resuspended with 1.0 mL of extraction solvent and the above process was repeated 2 more times for triple extraction. Now the supernatant was collected from triple extraction, transferred in a 1.5 mL MCT and the solution was lyophilized using a vacuum concentrator. For the lyophilization process, in a single time, not more than 500 μL of solution was taken in the MCT to avoid any sample loss. Lyophilized samples were stored at -20ºC until LC-MS analysis.</p><p>The lyophilized samples were reconstituted with 1 mL of 100%MeOH (MS grade). The reconstitution process included vortexing for 10 minutes at 2200 RPM in a multitube vortexer followed by sonication for 10 minutes at max frequency at room temperature. Then the samples were vortexed again for 10 minutes at 2200 RPM and centrifuged at 13000 RPM for 20 minutes at 20ºC. Now the supernatant from each sample was transferred in a fresh set of 1.5 mL MCT. For dilution, 400 μL of each sample was taken in a new set of 1.5 mL MCT and 600 μL of reconstitution solution was added to each of them and then samples were vortexed for 5 minutes. Prior to injection, UPLC-MS samples were filtered with a 0.22 μM nylon syringe filter. Samples were now transferred in an LC-MS vial with a proper label and stored at 4ºC until analysis.</p>	<p><strong>4.7.2 MS Instrument parameters for GC-MS analysis</strong></p><p>GC-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 8890 gas chromatograph coupled with an Agilent 7010C (Triple Quadrupole) mass spectrometer. Mass spectrometric analysis was conducted using electron ionization (EI) at 70 eV with a mass range of 40-500 and a scan time of 200 ms. The Agilent MassHunter workstation was used for the data acquisition. The metabolomics data was acquired in the .D file format, which was further processed and analyzed for metabolite identification and comparative metabolomics.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>4.8.2 MS Instrument parameters for LC-MS analysis</strong></p><p>The UPLC-MS analysis was performed in Vanquish Easy LC coupled with Orbitrap ExplorisTM 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA USA). Mass spectrometric analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer through an ESI interface. Analysis was performed in both positive and negative ionization modes. MS analysis included the full scan and data-dependent acquisition (DDA) MS2 scan. Full scan parameters involved the orbitrap resolution of 60000 with a scan range (m/z) of 100-1000 in profile mode. DD-MS2 parameters involved the Orbitrap resolution of 30000 with normalized HCD collision energies of 30, 40, and 50 eV. Thermo Scientific Xcalibur™ Data Acquisition and Interpretation Software was used for method setup and data acquisition. Waters Progenesis QI was used for the metabolomics data analysis in DDA mode. Quantification of withanolides was carried out by analyzing analytical standards under the same instrumental conditions and comparing their extracted peak areas with those of the compounds.</p>	<p><strong>4.9 Metabolites Identification for LC-MS and GC-MS metabolomics data</strong></p><p><br></p><p>LC-MS data processing was performed by first converting the thermo (.raw) data files to (.mzML) which were processed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Massachusetts, USA). For accurate metabolite annotation, MS/MS identification was performed using the AraCyc, KEGG, HMDB, and PubChem databases, with identification achieved up to level 2. Withanolides were identified using analytical standards of available withanolides and with the help of MS/MS identification aided with the order of chromatographic separation established in previous studies. The final data matrix was obtained based on the annotated metabolite and respective peak areas across all the samples.</p><p>GC-MS data files (.D) from the Agilent system were acquired using MassHunter workstation. Data matrix was obtained by batch processing the complete data files in Quantitative analysis software (©Agilent Technologies, Version 12.0, Build 12.0.893.1) after identification of metabolite features using RT and m/z. The identification of metabolites was performed using AMDIS by deconvoluting the data resulting in clean GC-MS mass spectra. The data was then compared by RI library database of known compounds which was further aided with NIST-MS database for accurate identification of metabolites. The reference indices were obtained by analyzing the homologous alkane series (C7-C30) in the same method.</p>

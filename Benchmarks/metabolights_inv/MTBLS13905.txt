Study Title	Integrating Network Pharmacology, Microbiomics, and Metabolomics to Uncover the Therapeutic Effect of Liubao Tea on Osteoarthritis
Study Description	<p>Background: Osteoarthritis (OA) is a debilitating joint disorder with no effective disease-modifying drugs. Liubao tea, a traditional Chinese dark tea, possesses various bioactivities, including anti-inflammatory and gut microbiota-regulating effects. However, its potential therapeutic role and mechanism of action in OA remain unexplored.</p><p>Methods: Ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) was used to analyze the chemical components of Liubao tea. Network pharmacology was employed to identify the active components and their potential targets in OA treatment. A destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM)-induced mouse OA model was established and treated with low/high-dose Liubao tea extract. Micro-CT, histological staining (H&amp;E, Safranin O-Fast Green), and ELISA were performed to evaluate joint structure, cartilage damage, and inflammatory cytokine levels. 16S rRNA sequencing, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), and untargeted serum metabolomics were conducted to explore gut microbiota and metabolic changes. Additionally, Brequinar, a de novo pyrimidine synthesis inhibitor, was used to verify the role of pyrimidine metabolism.</p><p>Results: UPLC-MS/MS and network pharmacology analyses identified 1,989 metabolites in Liubao tea, including 273 bioactive components (e.g., flavonoids, lignans) that targeted 602 OA-related genes. Liubao tea treatment significantly ameliorated DMM-induced OA progression, as evidenced by improved subchondral bone microarchitecture (increased BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Th; decreased Tb.Sp), reduced cartilage erosion (lowered modified Mankin and OARSI scores), and suppressed systemic inflammation (decreased IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α levels). Liubao tea remodeled gut microbiota homeostasis (increased α-diversity and altered bacterial taxa), and FMT from Liubao tea-treated mice recapitulated its anti-OA effects. Metabolomic analysis revealed that Liubao tea significantly downregulated the pyrimidine metabolism pathway, and Brequinar treatment mimicked its therapeutic benefits, confirming the role of pyrimidine metabolism suppression in OA alleviation.</p><p>Conclusion: Liubao tea attenuates OA progression by modulating gut microbiota composition and inhibiting the pyrimidine metabolism pathway, highlighting its potential as a novel natural therapeutic strategy for osteoarthritis.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system coupled with a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved using a&nbsp;Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm)&nbsp;. The column temperature was maintained at 40°C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water with 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile. The gradient elution program was set as follows: 0–1.5 min (5% B); 1.5–4.5 min (25% B); 4.5–7 min (30% B); 7–11 min (55% B); 11–12 min (85% B); 12–13.5 min (95% B); 13.5–14 min (5% B). The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 1 μL.</p>	<p>The raw UPLC-Q-TOF MS data were processed using Progenesis QI software (Waters, USA) for peak alignment, denoising, and normalization. The three-dimensional matrix containing information on sample names, peak retention times, and normalized peak intensities was exported for multivariate statistical analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using SIMCA-P 14.1 software (Umetrics, Sweden). The model quality was evaluated using 7-fold cross-validation and permutation testing. The stability of the system was validated by the tight clustering of QC samples in PCA score plots and by the high correlation (R² &gt; 0.9) of QC samples.</p>	<p>For metabolite extraction, 100 μL of mouse serum was aliquoted and mixed with 400 μL of pre-cooled methanol (-20°C) to precipitate proteins. The mixture was vortexed vigorously at 6000 rpm for 3 min and then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to a new 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen gas at room temperature. The dried residue was reconstituted in 100 μL of methanol, vortexed, and centrifuged again at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. A 10 μL aliquot of the final supernatant was used for UPLC-Q-TOF MS analysis. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing equal volumes of all test samples to condition the system and monitor instrument stability throughout the run.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry was performed on a&nbsp;quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-TOF)&nbsp;mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, operating in both positive and negative ion modes. The full scan mass range was set from&nbsp;m/z 100 to 1000. The key MS parameters were configured as follows: spray voltage, 3.5 kV (positive) and 3.2 kV (negative); sheath gas flow rate, 40 arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 5 arb; ion transfer tube temperature, 320°C; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350°C. For MS/MS fragmentation, the collision energy was set to 20–40 eV.</p>	<p>Differential metabolites were screened based on a Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) value &gt; 1.0 from the OPLS-DA model and a Student's t-test p-value &lt; 0.05. Metabolites were identified by matching their accurate mass (mass error &lt; 10 ppm), retention time, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), MetLin, and an in-house standard library from Shanghai Applied Protein Technology. All identifications in this study met confidence Level 2 or higher. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the KEGG database and MetPA.</p>	<p>Serum samples were collected from the mice in each experimental group (CG, MG, TG; n=6 per group) at the experimental endpoint. The blood was centrifuged to obtain serum, which was then immediately frozen and stored at -80°C until analysis.</p>

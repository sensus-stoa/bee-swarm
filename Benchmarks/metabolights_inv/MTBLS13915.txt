Study Identifier	MTBLS13915
Study Title	Integrated plant metabolic and microbiome signatures distinguish phytoprotection mechanisms underpinning single- vs dual-pathosystem soil suppressiveness
Study Description	<p>The goal of this study is to compare the metabolic response of plants grown in soils suppressive or conducive to wheat crown rot (caused by<em> Fusarium graminearum</em>) and inoculated or not with <em>F. graminearum</em>. This dataset contains LC-QTOF-MS-based untargeted metabolomics data from the roots of wheat plants. The wheat plants were grown in disease-suppressive and disease-conducive soils from France and Switzerland.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>A set of 8 soils was used for this experiment, including 4 French soils (sampled near Seyssel and Albens in the Savoie region) and 4 Swiss soils (sampled in the Morens region in Cantons Vaud and Fribourg, western Switzerland). The Morens soils include MS16, MS7, MC112 and MC10, and the Savoie soils include Ysa5, Asa2, Ymo4 and Amo1. Sites and soil sampling have been described (Catry et al., 2026). The wheat pathogen used for inoculation was&nbsp;<em>F. graminearum</em>&nbsp;strain Fg1 (Besset-Manzoni et al., 2019).</p><p>The greenhouse experiment followed the one described in Catry et al. (2026), in which&nbsp;<em>Nicotiana glutinosa</em>&nbsp;seedlings had been grown in each soil and inoculated or not with a&nbsp;<em>T. basicola</em>&nbsp;spore suspension. After that experiment, soils from the pots were grouped according to their original source, thoroughly mixed and sieved to remove tobacco roots. The prepared soils were then returned to pots and sown with three&nbsp;<em>Triticum aestivum</em>&nbsp;(cv. Recital) seeds. Seeds were inoculated with 100 μL of a&nbsp;<em>F. graminearum</em>&nbsp;spore suspension (2 ×&nbsp;10^5&nbsp;spores per seed) or received 100 μL of sterile distilled water (10 pots per soil × inoculation condition). Plants were grown under controlled greenhouse conditions (22 ± 2 °C day / 18 ± 2 °C night, 16 h light / 8 h dark, relative humidity 60–70%).&nbsp;For each treatment, 8 biological replicates (each consisting of one pot with three plants) were used, and 8 of them were processed for root metabolomics. Sampling was done at 8 weeks after sowing. The plants were unearthed, and loosely-adhering soil was gently removed with sterile tweezers.&nbsp;The roots&nbsp;were separated from the shoots, placed in sterile water, and tightly-adhering soil was detached by vortexing.</p>	<p>Two µL of each extract were analyzed on an Accurate-Mass Q-TOF 6546 instrument coupled with an LC 1290 Infinity II system (Agilent Technologies). The separation was carried out at 40&nbsp;°C using a 120 EC-C18 column (3.0 × 100&nbsp;mm × 2.7&nbsp;μm; Agilent Poroshell) with water, with 0.4% formic acid (v/v) as eluent A and acetonitrile as eluent B. The elution gradient (0.7&nbsp;mL/min) was run with eluent B from 1 to 57% in 8.5&nbsp;min, then 93% in 7&nbsp;min and finally a plateau at 100% from 16.5 to 18.5&nbsp;min. Mass analyses were made in positive mode with the nebulization gas (nitrogen) at a flow of 12 L/min and 40 PSG pressure, and with a capillary tension of 3500&nbsp;V.</p>	<p>Agilent’s data files were converted to open-format files using ProteoWizard's msConvert. Data were then processed with the Galaxy platform ‘Workflow4metabolomics’.</p>	<p>Wheat roots were freeze-dried using an Alpha 1–4 LSC (Martin Christ, Osterode am Harz, Germany) and then ground (Tissue Lyser II; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The powder obtained was extracted twice with methanol at a rate of 1.5&nbsp;mL per 100&nbsp;mg. Extracts were dried and dissolved at 10&nbsp;g/L in methanol.</p>	<p>Spectral data (UV and MS) were used to annotate discriminating molecules based on metabolome variables associated with the differences between suppressive and conducive soils, identified via OPLS-DAs (R package 'ropls'). From MS/MS fragmentation spectra, in both positive and negative modes, of pseudomolecular ions and adducts, the Sirius software (Dührkop et al., 2019) generated molecular formulas and information on their putative structures, by comparison with various databases (i.e., Natural Products, Coconut, KEGG, PlantCyc, Pubchem). These annotations were then checked by comparing them to the existing literature (e.g. with SciFinder).</p>

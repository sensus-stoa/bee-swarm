Study Title	Effects of Magnesium Potassium Sulfate on Tibetan Sheep Quality and Its Volatile and Non-Volatile Metabolic Substancesate the publication title
Study Description	<p>Preliminary research conducted by the team has identified that potassium and magnesium may influence the quality of Tibetan lamb meat, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study aims to examine the impact of dietary supplementation with magnesium potassium sulfate (PMS) on the carcass quality, meat quality, nutritional composition, and flavor metabolites of Tibetan sheep, while also elucidating potential mechanisms through a metabolomics approach. The findings indicate that the inclusion of 0.3% PMS in the diet significantly enhances the carcass quality of Tibetan sheep (P&lt;0.05), evidenced by increases in abdominal wall thickness and backfat thickness by 13.13% and 7.41%, respectively, compared to the control group. Furthermore, the addition of 0.25% PMS significantly elevates pH at 24 hours, a*, and intramuscular fat content, while significantly reducing pH at 45 minutes and b* (P&lt;0.05). Moreover, the supplementation of 0.25% PMS results in significantly higher concentrations of linoleic acid, linolenic acid, EPA, and DHA compared to the control group (P&lt;0.05). The findings regarding volatile compounds demonstrated that the application of PMS facilitated the production of fruity and nutty flavor compounds, including 3-methylpentanoic acid methyl ester and heptanoate methyl ester, while suppressing the accumulation of malodorous substances such as 1-hydrazino-2-propanol. Concurrently, the non-volatile compound analysis revealed that PMS primarily enhances fatty acid biosynthesis by activating the adipocyte cytokine signaling pathway. Additionally, it modulates the sphingolipid signaling pathway to synergistically regulate meat quality, thereby enhancing the flavor profile of Tibetan sheep.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC system. Samples were separated using a liquid chromatography method suitable for non-volatile metabolite profiling. The mobile phases, gradient elution program, flow rate, column temperature, autosampler temperature, and injection volume were set according to the established LC-MS metabolomics method used in this study.</p>	<p>The raw data was converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard (metabolite structure identification was performed using MetDDAA and LipDDA methods), followed by peak alignment</p>	<p>For metabolite extraction, 50 mg of frozen longissimus dorsi muscle sample was mixed with a pre-chilled methanol/acetonitrile/water solution at a ratio of 2:2:1 (v/v/v). The mixture was vortexed, sonicated for 30 min, and then incubated at -20°C for 10 min. The sample was centrifuged at 14,000 g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and dried under vacuum. For LC-MS analysis, the dried extract was reconstituted in 100 μL of acetonitrile/water solution at a ratio of 1:1 (v/v), vortexed, and centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to autosampler vials for analysis. Quality control samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots from all study samples and processed using the same extraction procedure.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using an AB Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer. The instrument was operated with an electrospray ionization source for untargeted metabolomic profiling. Data were acquired in positive and/or negative ion mode according to the LC-MS acquisition method. The m/z scan range and source parameters, including ion spray voltage, source temperature, gas settings, declustering potential, and collision energy, were set according to the established metabolomics method used in this study.</p>	<p>Perform retention time correction before peak area extraction using XCMS. After normalizing the total peak intensity, the processed data was imported into SIMCA-P (version 14.1, Umetrics) software to screen for differential metabolites and perform KEGG pathway analysis.</p>	<p>In this experiment, 120 healthy, similarly conditioned, 2-month-old weaned Tibetan lambs were selected and randomly assigned to four groups, with 30 lambs in each group. Group K served as the control group and was fed a basal diet consisting of concentrate supplement, silage oat hay, and green-dried oat hay. The concentrate supplement was mainly composed of corn, soybean meal, and premix. The experimental groups S1, S2, and S3 were fed the basal diet supplemented with PMS at concentrations of 0.2%, 0.25%, and 0.3% in the concentrate supplement, respectively. All lambs were housed in a fully enclosed barn with separate pens and an exercise yard under sheltered, sunny, dry, and well-ventilated conditions. Lambs were fed twice daily at 8:30 AM and 4:30 PM and had ad libitum access to feed and water. Longissimus dorsi muscle samples were collected after slaughter, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C until metabolomic analysis. Quality control samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots from all study samples.</p>

Study Title	Maize Recruits Beneficial Microorganisms via Rhizosphere Metabolites as Signals to Construct a Functional Network for Saline-Alkaline Stress Resistance
Study Description	<p>Carbonate-type saline-alkaline stress severely constrains maize production; however, the synergistic response mechanisms between rhizosphere microorganisms and metabolites remain unclear. This study focused on maize fields in the carbonate chernozem region of the Songnen Plain in Northeast China. Through field experiments and the integration of soil chemical factor analysis, microbial high-throughput sequencing (16S rRNA and ITS), and non-targeted metabolomics (LC-MS), we systematically investigated the response mechanisms of the rhizosphere micro-ecosystem under saline-alkaline stress. The results indicated that saline-alkaline stress significantly increased soil pH and electrical conductivity (EC), and led to decreases in soil organic matter (SOM), total nitrogen (TN), and total phosphorus (TP) contents. However, the rhizosphere zone exhibited a certain buffering capacity, maintaining a higher cation exchange capacity (CEC). Microbial community analysis revealed that bacterial alpha diversity increased under stress. In contrast, fungal diversity significantly decreased, and the community structure shifted towards a pathogen-dominated community, primarily within Ascomycota, especially the genus Fusarium. Co-occurrence network analysis further revealed that saline-alkaline conditions enhanced the complexity and connectivity of bacterial networks but led to the contraction and structural simplification of fungal networks. Metabolite analysis showed that saline-alkaline stress induced significant reprogramming of the rhizosphere metabolic profile. Organophosphorus compounds, nucleotides, and their analogs were significantly enriched, while defensive secondary metabolites such as Cajanol specifically accumulated in the saline-alkaline rhizosphere. Pathway analysis indicated the activation of stress resistance and oxidative stress mitigation-related pathways, including Betalain biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, and arginine metabolism. Multi-omics integration analysis identified soil EC and total potassium (TK) as key environmental factors driving the differentiation of microbial and metabolite communities. Key differential metabolites showed significant positive correlations with saline-alkaline-enriched microbial taxa (e.g., Sphingomonas), revealing a metabolite-mediated microbial recruitment mechanism. This study, through multi-omics analysis, discovered that the maize rhizosphere, under saline-alkaline stress, undergoes metabolic reprogramming (e.g., enriching defensive metabolites like Cajanol) to directionally recruit beneficial bacteria such as Sphingomonas and maintains higher bacterial network complexity, while also leading to the pathologization of the fungal community. Our study reveals that maize recruits beneficial microbes via rhizosphere metabolic reprogramming, providing a mechanistic basis for microbiome-assisted saline-alkaline soil remediation.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system equipped with an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 µm particle size; Waters, USA). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water: acetonitrile (2:98, v/v) (Solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (Solvent B). The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 5 µL.</p>	<p>Raw LC/MS data were processed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA). This processing generated a three-dimensional data matrix in CSV format, containing sample information, metabolite names, and mass spectral response intensities. Internal standard peaks and known false positive peaks (including noise, column bleed, and derivatized reagent peaks) were removed from the data matrix. The data were then deduplicated and peaks were pooled.</p>	<p>For metabolite extraction, 100 mg of rhizosphere soil was accurately weighed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube. A volume of 1000 µL of extraction solvent [methanol/water (4:1, v/v)] containing 20 µg/mL 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine (internal standard) was added. The sample was ground for 6 minutes (50 Hz, -10 °C) using a grinder, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 30 minutes (40 KHz, 5 °C). After centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4 °C, the supernatant was transferred to an injection vial for analysis. To ensure stability and accuracy, quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes of extracts from all rhizosphere samples. These QC samples were processed and tested identically to the analytical samples and were inserted every 12 analytical samples during the instrument run.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted on a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). (Note: Specific parameters such as ion source type, ionization mode (positive/negative), m/z range, and source parameters like temperature, voltage, and gas flows were not provided in the text. These details should be added based on the actual experimental settings, if available.) The following default or typical parameters for this platform are suggested as placeholders: Heated Electrospray Ionization (HESI) source; Ionization Mode: positive and negative ion modes; m/z range: typically 70-1050 m/z; Sheath gas flow rate, Aux gas flow rate, Sweep gas flow rate, Spray voltage, Capillary temperature, and Aux gas heater temperature should be specified.</p>	<p>Metabolites were identified by searching the processed data against several databases, including the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, http://www.hmdb.ca/), METLIN (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and the self-compiled Majorbio Database (MJDB) from Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The identification was performed using Progenesis QI software. The resulting data matrix was then uploaded to the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com) for further data preprocessing and analysis. During preprocessing on the cloud platform, features were filtered (retained if present in 80% of any sample set), missing values were filled with the minimum value, and data were normalized by sum and log10 transformed. QC variables with a relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt; 30% were excluded.</p>	<p>Soil sampling was conducted on October 9, 2025, at the experimental base of the Qiqihar Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Heilongjiang Province (47°35′ N, 123°92′ E). Rhizosphere and bulk soil samples were colleted from both saline-alkaline and conventional plots of maize fields, designated as SR (saline-alkaline rhizosphere), SB (saline-alkaline bulk), CR (conventional rhizosphere), and CB (conventional bulk), respectively. Using a sterilized shovel, soil sub-samples from six plants per replicate plot were collected along an S-shaped pattern. Rhizosphere soil (approximately 2 mm adhering to roots) was collected after shaking off loosely attached soil. Simultaneously, bulk soil was collected from the same plot at approximately 20 cm from the plant base at the same soil depth. The six plant samples from each plot were randomly divided into two groups (3 plants each). Rhizosphere soil and bulk soil within the same group were separately homogenized to form one composite sample. Therefore, each plot yielded two composite rhizosphere soil samples and two composite bulk soil samples, resulting in a total of 24 soil samples for the entire experiment. Samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C. Each sample was divided into two portions. One portion was stored at -80°C for soil microbial and metabolite extraction; the remaining soil was air-dried for determination of soil chemical properties.</p>

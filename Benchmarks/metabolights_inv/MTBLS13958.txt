Study Title	Kidney protective effect of fermented celery polysaccharides and Eubacterium rectale in diabetic rats
Study Description	<p>This study aimed to investigate the protective effects of non-fermented celery polysaccharides (NFCP), fermented celery polysaccharides (FCP), Eubacterium rectale (ER), and their combined intervention (ER+FCP) on Type 2 diabetes (T2D)-related kidney injury in a rat model, using untargeted urinary metabolomics and kidney function indices. We employed ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC–QTOF/MS) to analyze urinary metabolite profiles. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated significant metabolic disturbances in the diabetic group (DM), while all intervention groups showed a shift towards a healthier metabolic profile, with ER and ER+FCP exhibiting the most pronounced improvements. Metabolic alterations in the intervention groups were further validated by differential metabolite analysis, with FCP inducing a larger number of significant changes compared to NFCP. The combined intervention (ER+FCP) showed synergistic effects, enhancing lipid metabolism, inflammation resolution, and the regulation of bile acids, all contributing to kidney protection. Inflammatory markers, oxidative stress, and histological analysis confirmed the efficacy of these interventions in mitigating kidney injury. Our findings highlight the potential of fermented celery polysaccharides and ER to provide enhanced protection against diabetic kidney disease through metabolic modulation and synergistic interactions.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Urine samples were collected from male Wistar rats (7 weeks old, 200 ± 20 g) with Type 2 diabetes induced by a high-fat diet (HFD) and streptozotocin (STZ) injection. The rats were randomly assigned to different experimental groups: Non-Fermented Celery Polysaccharides (NFCP), Fermented Celery Polysaccharides (FCP), Eubacterium rectale (ER), and ER + FCP. All rats were treated for 5 weeks. Urine was collected at baseline and at the end of the treatment period (after 5 weeks) for metabolomic analysis. Prior to collection, rats were housed in clean cages and fasted overnight. Urine was collected into pre-chilled containers and stored at -80°C until further analysis</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on a Nexera X2 UPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The column used was an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) maintained at 40 °C for resolution.</p><p>The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and water, with a gradient elution method applied. The flow rate was set to 0.4 mL/min, and the injection volume was 5 μL. The chromatographic system was hyphenated to a Triple TOF 5600 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with an electrospray interface, operating in both positive and negative ionization modes.</p>	<p>Raw mass spectrometry data were processed using Progenesis QI v3.0 (Nonlinear Dynamics / Waters) including peak detection, alignment, deconvolution, and normalization. Extracted features were log-transformed and Pareto-scaled to reduce the influence of high-abundance metabolites and improve comparability across samples. The resulting dataset was then used for subsequent metabolomic analysis, including principal component analysis (PCA) and identification of differential metabolites.</p>	<p>Urine samples were isolated using a protein-precipitation procedure. To each 200 μL portion of urine, 800 μL acetonitrile–water (4:1, v/v) was added. The mixture was vortex-mixed for 1 minute and then kept in an ice-water bath for 20 minutes. Afterwards, the samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C, and the resulting clear supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis.</p><p>For metabolomic analysis, chromatographic separation was performed on a Nexera X2 UPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled with a Triple TOF 5600 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with an electrospray interface, operated in both positive and negative ionization modes.</p><p>Control samples included standard solutions and quality control samples. Solvent blanks and control group samples were also used to verify background interference during the analysis process.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry was performed using a Triple TOF 5600 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization source. The instrument was operated in both positive and negative ionization modes.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed using Progenesis QI v3.0 for peak alignment and feature extraction. Identified features were matched against several reference databases, including HMDB (Human Metabolome Database), KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), and Metlin, to annotate the metabolites. Additionally, the accurate mass and fragmentation patterns of ions were compared with known metabolite profiles in the databases.&nbsp;</p>

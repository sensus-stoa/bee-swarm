Study Identifier	MTBLS1396
Study Title	Longitudinal multi-omics reveals subset-specific mechanisms underlying irritable bowel syndrome
Study Description	<p>The gut microbiome has been implicated in multiple human chronic gastrointestinal (GI) disorders. Determining its mechanistic role in disease has been difficult due to apparent disconnects between animal and human studies and lack of an integrated multi-omics view of disease-specific physiological changes. We integrated longitudinal multi-omics data from the gut microbiome, metabolome, host epigenome, and transcriptome in the context of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) host physiology. We identified IBS subtype-specific and symptom-related variation in microbial composition and function. A subset of identified changes in microbial metabolites correspond to host physiological mechanisms that are relevant to IBS. By integrating multiple data layers, we identified purine metabolism as a novel host-microbial metabolic pathway in IBS with translational potential. Our study highlights the importance of longitudinal sampling and integrating complementary multi-omics data to identify functional mechanisms that can serve as therapeutic targets in a comprehensive treatment strategy for chronic GI diseases.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Linked cross omic data sets:</strong></p><p>Microbiome sequencing data associated with this study are available in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA): accession number <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/browser/view/PRJEB37924' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>PRJEB37924</a> and <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/browser/view/PRJNA612180' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>PRJNA612180</a>.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Biopsies were obtained through flexible sigmoidoscopy from the sigmoid colon 20-30 cm from the anal verge essentially as described previously [1][2]. Up to two tap water enemas were given to cleanse colon for each procedure. All endoscopic procedures were performed by a single endoscopist (P.C.K.) and up to twelve colonic biopsies were collected using a large-capacity (2.8 mm) biopsy forceps without pin. Depending on downstream processing the biopsies were placed in RNAlater stabilization solution (Life Technologies), directly frozen in liquid nitrogen, or placed in glucose Krebs solution on ice (composition in mM: 11.5 D-glucose, 120.3 NaCl, 15.5 NaHCO3, 5.9 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 2.5 CaCl2.2H2O, and 1.2 MgCl2; pH 7.3–7.4) and immediately transported to the laboratory for experiments.</p><p><br></p><p>Stool specimens were completed via home collection kits at the earliest convenience after the initial visit and then monthly for six months. Sample tubes were returned with frozen gel packs overnight using FedEx or dropped off at the clinical core facility of Mayo Clinic Center for Cell Signaling where samples were stored at -80˚C.</p><p><br></p><p>References:</p><p>[1] Bhattarai Y, Williams BB, Battaglioli EJ, Whitaker WR, Till L, Grover M, Linden DR, Akiba Y, Kandimalla KK, Zachos NC, Kaunitz JD, Sonnenburg JL, Fischbach MA, Farrugia G, Kashyap PC. Gut Microbiota-Produced Tryptamine Activates an Epithelial G-Protein-Coupled Receptor to Increase Colonic Secretion. Cell Host Microbe. 2018 Jun 13;23(6):775-785.e5. doi: 10.1016/j.chom.2018.05.004.</p><p>[2] Peters, S.A., Edogawa, S., Sundt, W.J., Dyer, R.B., Dalenberg, D.A., Mazzone, A., Singh, R.J., Moses, N., Smyrk, T.C., Weber, C., et al. (2017). Constipation-Predominant Irritable Bowel Syndrome Females Have Normal Colonic Barrier and Secretory Function. Am J Gastroenterol 112, 913-923.</p>	<p><strong>LC-MS </strong></p><p>Aliquoted stool samples (~100 mg) were randomized in order and transferred to a screw-cap tube containing 50 mg 1.0 mm Zirconia beads (BioSpec). Metabolites were extracted by addition of 400 µl of acetonitrile:H2O (approximate volumetric ratio of 1:3) and homogenized for 30 secs in a Biospec beat beater at maximum speed. The homogenized samples were then centrifuged for 20 mins at 16000 x g, after which the supernatant was transferred to Spin-X 0.22 µm spin filter tubes (COSTAR®) and centrifuged for 30 mins at 16000 x g. Filtered faecal water were transferred into 96-well plates (Costar® 3960). The plate was then placed on an Eppendorf MixMate plate shaker at 1300 rpm for 5 mins.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NMR (Bile acid)</strong></p><p>Aliquoted stool samples (~100 mg) were randomized in order and transferred to a screw-cap tube containing 50 mg 1.0 mm Zirconia beads (BioSpec). Metabolites were extracted by addition of 400 µl of acetonitrile:H2O (approximate volumetric ratio of 1:3) and homogenized for 30 secs in a Biospec beat beater at maximum speed. The homogenized samples were then centrifuged for 20 mins at 16000 x g, after which the supernatant was transferred to Spin-X 0.22 µm spin filter tubes (COSTAR®) and centrifuged for 30 mins at 16000 x g. 80 µl of the filtered samples was aliquoted into 96 well plates, and 10 µl was kept separately for downstream quality control purposes. Samples were dried under nitrogen flow before reconstituting in 540 µl of D2O and 60 µl of NMR buffer, all in 96 well deep well plates (COSTAR®). The plate was then placed on an Eppendorf MixMate plate shaker at 1300 rpm for 5 mins. The reconstituted fecal water and buffer mixture was transferred to 5 mm NMR tubes. NMR buffer with 1.5 M KH2PO4 was prepared by dissolving 20.4 g of KH2PO4 in 80 ml of D2O. D2O (6 ml) containing 100 mg of 3-(trimethylsilyl) propionic-2,2,3,3-d4 acid sodium salt (TSP) (Millipore-Sigma) and 13 mg of NaN3 was added and mixed by shaking and sonication. The pH was adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH pellets. Total volume was adjusted with D2O. Blank samples of NMR buffer only, pooled quality control (QC) and spiked QC samples with standards as highlighted in the metabolite table (SQC) were also included.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GC-MS (Biopsie samples)</strong></p><p>Colonic biopsies were stored at -40 ˚C until extraction. Biopsy tissues were transferred to a screw-cap tube and weighed, after which 5 * 1.0 mm Zirconia beads and 100 µl of ultrapure water were added. The tissue was homogenized in a Biospec bead-beater using 2 x 30 second cycles at max speed. An eleven-point calibration curve (see attached excel file for details) and a pooled QC sample (10 µl from each sample pooled) was constructed using genuine SCFA standards. Metabolites were then extracted using methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) (Millipore-Sigma) and derivatized with N-tert-butyldimethylsilyl-N-methyltrifluoroacetamide with 1% tert-butyldimethylchloro-silane (MTBSTF + 1% TBDMSCI). Where avilable, 2 biological replicates were analysed for each sample. Solvent blank (Acetonitrile:H2O (1:3)) control samples were also included.</p>	<p>The reconstituted fecal water and buffer mixture was transferred to 5 mm NMR tubes. NMR buffer with 1.5 M KH2PO4 was prepared by dissolving 20.4g of KH2PO4 in 80 ml of D2O. D2O (6 ml) containing 100 mg of 3-(trimethylsilyl) propionic-2,2,3,3-d4 acid sodium salt (TSP) (Millipore-Sigma) and 13 mg of NaN3 was added and mixed by shaking and sonication. The pH was adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH pellets. Total volume was adjusted with D2O.</p>	<p>Metabolic profiles were recorded essentially as described previously [1] on a Bruker 600 MHz spectrometer (Bruker Biospin) set at a constant temperature of 300K for fecal samples.</p><p><br></p><p>References:</p><p>[1] Dona, A.C., Jimenez, B., Schafer, H., Humpfer, E., Spraul, M., Lewis, M.R., Pearce, J.T., Holmes, E., Lindon, J.C., and Nicholson, J.K. Anal Chem 86, 9887-9894.</p>	<p>A 1D nuclear Overhauser enhancement spectroscopy (NOSEY) experiment and a 2D J-resolved experiment was performed for each fecal sample. A total of 32 scans were acquired with an acquisition time of 4 minutes and 3 seconds per fecal sample following 4 dummy scans and the spectral data was collected into 64K data points. Automatic phasing, baseline correction and spectral calibration to TSP (0 ppm) was performed in Topspin 3.1 (Bruker Biospin) [1]. </p><p><br></p><p>References:</p><p>[1] Dona, A.C., Jimenez, B., Schafer, H., Humpfer, E., Spraul, M., Lewis, M.R., Pearce, J.T., Holmes, E., Lindon, J.C., and Nicholson, J.K. (2014). Precision high-throughput proton NMR spectroscopy of human urine, serum, and plasma for large-scale metabolic phenotyping. Anal Chem 86, 9887-9894.</p>	<p><strong>LCMS</strong></p><p>Data acquired from the LC-MS experiment were imported into MassLynx (Waters Ltd.) and processed using method files contain information on mass to charge ratio and retention time of the bile acids. M/Z value and retention time were confirmed by inspecting the relevant bile acid peaks in the quality control sample spiked with authentic bile acid standards. Manual inspection on each processed sample file was carried out to ensure bile acid peaks have been correctly integrated. The extracted peak integral was then normalised to the Total Ion Current of each sample.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GCMS (Biopsie samples)</strong></p><p>No data transformation.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NMR (Bile acid)</strong></p><p>The pre-processed spectral data was imported into MATLAB (Version 8.3.0.532 R2014a, Mathworks Inc, Natick, MA, USA). A series of in-house scripts were used for the following executions. The spectra were manually aligned to correct for subtle alterations in the chemical shifts of the peaks due to variation in pH. To account for the difference in sample concentration, probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN) was applied to the spectral data.</p><p><br></p><p>Metabolomics data was log10 transformed (with pseudo count 1 for zero values) and tested for significance in R with linear mixed-effect models correcting for subject using the lmer function from the lmerTest package with formula lmer(data ~ cohort + (1|subject_id).</p>	<p><strong>LCMS</strong></p><p>Bile acids were identified by matching to the m/z and retention time of the authentic standards in the spiked QC sample. Manual inspection on each processed sample file was carried out to ensure bile acid peaks have been correctly integrated. The extracted peak integral was then normalised to the Total Ion Current of each sample.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GCMS (Biopsie samples)</strong></p><p>The use of extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) for area calculation and quantification reduced the possibility of misinterpreting overlapping peaks. A characteristic single ion, with the highest m/z value that is relatively abundant was selected for each compound (Table 1 [1]).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NMR (Bile acid)</strong></p><p>A projection to latent structures-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) model based on the Monte Carlo cross-validation (MCCV) method was constructed on the complete spectral profiles to identify discriminatory features in relevant comparisons [1][2]. A total of 10 MCCV rounds with 10 bootstrap rounds in each model was used to assess model robustness and to calculate the mean prediction (Tpred) of each sample. Discriminatory spectral features were annotated using statistical total correlation spectroscopy (STOCSY) [3] and a combination of in-house and online databases (www.hmdb.ca). An in-house developed peak integration script was applied to calculate the integral of spectral peaks of interest.</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p>References:</p><p>[1] Posma, J.M., Garcia-Perez, I., Ebbels, T.M.D., Lindon, J.C., Stamler, J., Elliott, P., Holmes, E., and Nicholson, J.K. Statistical Total Correlation Spectroscopy: An Exploratory Approach for Latent Biomarker Identification from Metabolic 1H NMR Data Sets. Analytical Chemistry.&nbsp;2005;&nbsp;77&nbsp;(5): 1282-1289</p>	<p><strong>LCMS</strong></p><p>The protocol was adapted from Sarafian et al. (2015) [1]. Samples were analyzed on an ACQUITY ultra performance liquid-chromatography (UPLC) system (Waters Ltd., UK) coupled to a Xevo G2-S quadrupole time of flight (Q-TOF) mass spectrometer (Waters Ltd.). A reversed-phase column ACQUITY BEH C8 column (1.7 μm, 100 mm × 2.1 mm) was used at an operating temperature of 60 °C. The aqueous part of the mobile phase (A) consisted of 1 mM ammonia acetate in ultrapure water, pH 4.15. The organic mobile phase (B) was 1:1 isopropanol to acetonitrile. Chromatographic gradient is outlined in figure <a href='https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5b01556/suppl_file/ac5b01556_si_001.pdf' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>S-3</a> Sarafian et al. (2015) [1]. The injection volume of all samples was 5 μl.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GCMS (Biopsie samples)</strong></p><p>Details of the GC-MS instrument conditions and data processing can be found in García-Villalba et al. [2]. In brief, an Agilent 7890B (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA), equipped with an automatic liquid sampler (MPS2) (Gerstel, Mülheim, Germany) fitted with a high polarity, polyethylene glycol (PEG), fused silica capillary column DB‐WAXetr (30 m, 0.25 mm id, 0.25 μm film thickness) was used for chromatographic separation. Helium was used as the carrier gas at 1 ml/min and injection was made in splitless mode with an injection volume of 1 μl and an injector temperature of 250 °C. A glass liner with a glass wool plug at the lower end of the liner was used to avoid the contamination of the GC column with nonvolatile fecal material. Every five faecal samples injected, a blank sample with hexane was inserted to check for memory effects.The column temperature gradient was as follows; start temp 90 °C, increasing to 150 °C at 15 °C/min, 170°C at 5 °C/min, 250 °C at 20 °C/min, finishing at 250 °C for 2 min (total time 14 min). Solvent delay was 3.5 min.</p><p><br></p><p>References:</p><p>[1] Sarafian, M.H., Lewis, M.R., Pechlivanis, A., Ralphs, S., McPhail, M.J., Patel, V.C., Dumas, M.E., Holmes, E., and Nicholson, J.K. [1]. Mass spectrometry parameters were as follows: capillary voltage was set at 1.5 kV, cone voltage at 60 V, source temperature at 150 °C, desolvation temperature at 600 °C, desolvation gas flow at 1000 L/h, and cone gas flow at 150 L/h.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GCMS</strong></p><p>GCMS was preformed on a Agilent 5975C mass selective detector, using Chemstation software (Hewlett–Packard, Palo Alto, CA, USA) . The temperature settings were ion source 230 °C, quadrupole 150 °C, and interface 280 °C. Identification of the SCFAs was based on the retention time of standard compounds and with the assistance of the NIST 08 and Wiley7N libraries.</p><p><br></p><p>References:</p><p>[1] Sarafian, M.H., Lewis, M.R., Pechlivanis, A., Ralphs, S., McPhail, M.J., Patel, V.C., Dumas, M.E., Holmes, E., and Nicholson, J.K.

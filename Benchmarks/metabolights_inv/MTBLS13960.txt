Study Title	Gut Microbiome and Metabolome Signatures in Calcium Oxalate Stone Recurrence: A Multi-Omics Study
Study Description	<p>Background: The incidence and recurrence rate of nephrolithiasis have been increasing annually. Recent evidence highlights a close association between the composition and function of the gut microbiome and the occurrence and recurrence of kidney stones. We performed a multi-omic study to investigate changes in gut microbiota and their metabolites during nephrolithiasis development and recurrence, and to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms. Fecal samples from 37 recurrent stone patients, 38 first-episode stone patients, and 39 healthy controls were collected for 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. Ten samples from each group were randomly selected for metagenomic sequencing.</p><p>Results: Compared to incident cases, recurrent stone patients exhibited further reduced gut microbial richness and diversity, with enrichment of Enterobacterales, Pseudomonadota, Gammaproteobacteria, Enterobacteraceae, Escherichia-Shigella, and Bacillia. In the recurrent kidney stone group, 9 metabolites were upregulated and 86 downregulated, primarily enriched in purine metabolism, and caffeine metabolism pathways. We identified 10 metabolites as recurrence biomarkers and found significant correlations between Escherichia-Shigella and Asn-Tyr, Leu-Ala-Ile, Tyrosyl-Alanine, or 3'-hydroxyhexobarbital. Additionally, gender-specific gut microbiota signatures were observed. Oxalate decarboxylase and short-chain fatty acid-related enzymes decreased during stone formation but rebounded with recurrence. Caffeine and its metabolites were significantly downregulated in recurrent patients, suggesting a potential association with stone formation and recurrence that merits further investigation.</p><p>Conclusions: Our study comprehensively characterizes the gut microbiome and metabolome signatures associated with nephrolithiasis recurrence. The findings reveal that recurrent nephrolithiasis is characterized by impaired gut microbial evenness, enrichment of specific taxa including Escherichia-Shigella, and dysregulated metabolic pathways such as purine metabolism and caffeine metabolism. The 10 identified metabolites show promise as potential recurrence biomarkers, with notable correlations between Escherichia-Shigella and key metabolites. These results highlight the critical association of the gut microbiome-metabolome axis with renal stone recurrence, providing novel microbial and metabolic targets for early prediction, with potential implications for prevention and personalized treatment that require further validation.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Samples for this study were collected from patients undergoing surgery at the Urology Center of Beijing Friendship Hospital, affiliated with Capital Medical University, from July 2021 to April 2023, as well as from healthy individuals at the hospital's health examination center. The study included three groups: the recurrent kidney stone group (RS group, n=37), the first-episode kidney stone group (FS group, n=38), and the non-stone group (NS group, n=39). The RS and FS groups are collectively referred to as the kidney stone group (KS group). Patients with unilateral kidney stones or unilateral ureteral stones enrolled before April 2023 were re-evaluated by imaging in April 2024. Patients were classified into the RS group if they self-reported 2 acute episodes of kidney or ureteral stones treated with medication or surgery, or if new stones or growth of existing stones (with any dimension increasing by at least 1 mm from baseline) were diagnosed by abdominal-pelvic CT, KUB X-ray, or urinary ultrasound one year after surgery. Otherwise, they were classified into the FS group (A single episode of unilateral kidney or ureteral stones, with no recurrence confirmed during the 1-year follow-up period). Recent studies indicate three key factors shaping gut microbiome composition—age, stone composition, and residence—independent of urolithiasis status. Antibiotic use within 30 days, sex, hypertension, water intake, weight, and diet are also associated. Thus, these variables will be controlled during patient enrollment. The non-stone group consisted of individuals undergoing health examinations at our hospital, matched with the study groups in terms of gender, age, lifestyle, dietary habits and genetic background. We included adults aged 18–75 who have lived in Beijing, Hebei and Tianjin for 3 years and meet the above conditions in the study. Fecal samples were collected from each participant during their consultations at the Urology Department of Beijing Friendship Hospital. Samples were immediately stored at -80 °C until analysis in 3 minutes.</p>	<p>Analytical Instrumentation and Chromatographic Conditions</p><p><br></p><p>1. Instrument Details</p><p><br></p><p>Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) coupled with either an Orbitrap Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer or an Orbitrap Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) at Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).</p><p><br></p><p>2. Chromatographic Column</p><p><br></p><p>Separation was achieved on a Hypersil Gold column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with dimensions of 100 mm × 2.1 mm and a particle size of 1.9 μm. The column temperature was maintained at ambient conditions (no additional temperature control specified in the study protocols).</p><p><br></p><p>3. Mobile Phase and Gradient Elution</p><p><br></p><p>The mobile phase composition was consistent for both positive and negative ionization modes: Phase A was 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in LC-MS grade water, and Phase B was LC-MS grade methanol. The flow rate was set at 0.2 mL/min with an injection volume of 2 μL (consistent with standard protocols for the Hypersil Gold column and UHPLC system). The linear gradient elution program was implemented as follows: initial conditions (0.0 min) were 98% Phase A and 2% Phase B, maintained until 1.5 min; from 1.5 min to 4.5 min, Phase B was linearly increased from 2% to 85%; from 4.5 min to 10.0 min, Phase B was further increased to 100% and held; at 10.1 min, Phase B was rapidly reduced back to 2%; finally, the initial conditions (98% Phase A, 2% Phase B) were maintained from 10.1 min to 12.0 min for column re-equilibration.</p><p><br></p><p>4. Mass Spectrometry Settings</p><p><br></p><p>The mass spectrometer was operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. Key parameters were configured as follows: spray voltage of 3.5 kV (for both positive and negative modes), capillary temperature of 320 °C, sheath gas flow rate of 35 psi, auxiliary gas flow rate of 10 L/min, S-lens RF level of 60, and auxiliary gas heater temperature of 350 °C. The scan mode was full-scan MS (high-resolution) for metabolite profiling, with data-dependent MS/MS acquisition for structural confirmation (consistent with untargeted metabolomics workflows).</p>	<p>Data Processing Methods, Pipelines and Software</p><p><br></p><p>Raw metabolomics data were processed using a standardized untargeted metabolomics analysis pipeline implemented by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), tailored for Thermo Orbitrap-based LC-MS data. The core workflow and software tools are described as follows:</p><p><br></p><p>Raw Data ConversionRaw data files (.raw format) generated by the Vanquish UHPLC-Q Exactive HF/HF-X system were first converted to the open mzML format using ProteoWizard MSConvert (ProteoWizard Software Foundation), ensuring compatibility with downstream open-source analysis tools while retaining all original spectral and chromatographic information.</p><p><br></p><p>Peak Detection, Alignment and QuantificationSubsequent data processing was performed using XCMS (version 3.x, implemented in R/Bioconductor), a gold-standard tool for untargeted LC-MS metabolomics. Key steps included:</p><p><br></p><p>Peak picking: Conducted using the centWave algorithm, optimized for high-resolution Orbitrap data, to detect chromatographic peaks based on m/z accuracy, peak shape and intensity.</p><p><br></p><p>Retention time alignment: The obiwarp method was applied to correct for minor retention time drifts across samples, ensuring consistent peak matching between experimental samples, quality control (QC) samples and solvent blanks.</p><p><br></p><p>Peak grouping and filling: Peaks with similar m/z and retention time were grouped across samples; missing values in individual samples were filled using a k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) algorithm where applicable, to account for low-abundance metabolites below the detection limit in specific runs.</p><p><br></p><p>Data Normalization and Quality Control</p><p><br></p><p>Normalization: Peak intensity data were normalized using the total ion current (TIC) method, scaling each sample to a consistent total ion count to correct for variations in injection volume and ion suppression effects.</p><p><br></p><p>QC-based filtering: Peaks with inconsistent detection in QC samples (detection rate &lt; 50%) or significant signal in solvent blank samples (intensity &gt; 50% of the median intensity in experimental samples) were excluded from further analysis, to remove non-biological background noise and unreliable features.</p><p><br></p><p>Metabolite AnnotationProcessed feature tables (m/z-retention time pairs with intensity values) were annotated using CAMERA (R/Bioconductor) for spectral deconvolution and isotope/adduct grouping, followed by database matching against the HMDB (Human Metabolome Database), METLIN and LipidMaps databases. Annotations were restricted to metabolites with a mass accuracy tolerance of&nbsp;5 ppm and matching retention time trends with authentic standards (where available in Novogene’s internal library).</p><p><br></p><p>Statistical Analysis PreparationFinal annotated metabolite tables, including peak intensities and metadata, were exported to R (version 4.x) and Python (Pandas library) for subsequent univariate (t-test, fold change) and multivariate (PCA, PLS-DA) statistical analyses, which form the basis of the comparative metabolomics analysis in this study.</p>	<p>Sample Extraction and Preparation Methods</p><p><br></p><p>Fecal samples (0.5 g per sample) were placed in 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes, and 500 µL of 80% methanol was added to each tube. The mixtures were vortexed thoroughly and then centrifuged at 15,000 × g and 4 °C for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was diluted with liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) grade water to achieve a final methanol concentration of 53%, followed by a second centrifugation step; the supernatant from this step was collected as the final sample extract for subsequent LC-MS analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>Control Samples Prepared for the Assay</p><p><br></p><p>Pooled Quality Control (QC) samples: A QC sample was prepared by mixing equal volumes of the experimental fecal sample extracts (all study samples). This QC sample was analyzed before, during, and after the injection sequence of experimental samples in the LC-MS/MS system to assess and monitor the stability and reproducibility of the analytical system throughout the entire experiment.</p><p><br></p><p>Solvent blank samples: Blank samples (consisting of the same solvent system used for sample extraction: 80% methanol diluted to 53% methanol with LC-MS grade water) were included in the analysis to eliminate background ion interference and correct for non-specific signals in the LC-MS/MS detection process.</p>	<p>Instrument and Mass Spectrometry Parameters</p><p><br></p><p>1. Instrument Details (Make &amp; Manufacturer)</p><p><br></p><p>Mass Spectrometer: Orbitrap Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer or Orbitrap Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer</p><p><br></p><p>Manufacturer: Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany</p><p><br></p><p>Coupled UHPLC System: Vanquish UHPLC system</p><p><br></p><p>Manufacturer: Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany</p><p><br></p><p>Analysis Facility: Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China)</p><p><br></p><p>2. Ion Source and Ionization Mode</p><p><br></p><p>Ion Source: Electrospray Ionization (ESI)</p><p><br></p><p>Ionization Mode: Both positive and negative polarity modes</p><p><br></p><p>3. Key Mass Spectrometry Parameters</p><p><br></p><p>m/z Range: Not explicitly specified in the study protocols (consistent with standard untargeted metabolomics workflows for broad metabolite coverage)</p><p><br></p><p>Spray Voltage: 3.5 kV (applied for both positive and negative modes)</p><p><br></p><p>Capillary Temperature: 320 °C</p><p><br></p><p>Auxiliary Gas Heater Temperature: 350 °C</p><p><br></p><p>Sheath Gas Flow Rate: 35 psi</p><p><br></p><p>Auxiliary Gas Flow Rate: 10 L/min</p><p><br></p><p>S-lens RF Level: 60</p><p><br></p><p>Scan Mode: Full-scan MS (high-resolution) for metabolite profiling, with data-dependent MS/MS acquisition for structural confirmation (consistent with untargeted metabolomics analysis requirements)</p>	<p>Metabolite Feature Identification, Annotation Methods, Pipelines and Reference Databases</p><p><br></p><p>1. Feature Identification and Preprocessing Pipeline</p><p><br></p><p>Raw metabolomic data generated by the UHPLC-MS/MS system were processed using two complementary software tools to ensure comprehensive feature detection and quality control:</p><p><br></p><p>Primary processing with XCMS: The raw data were first imported into XCMS (implemented in R/Bioconductor) for core steps including peak extraction, peak alignment, peak quantification, and standardization. This tool was optimized to handle high-resolution Orbitrap data, ensuring accurate detection of chromatographic peaks based on m/z precision and signal intensity.</p><p><br></p><p>Supplementary processing with Compound Discoverer 3.3: Parallel processing was performed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (CD3.3, ThermoFisher) to validate feature identification. Key parameters included peak area correction with the first quality control (QC) sample, a mass tolerance of 5 ppm, a signal intensity tolerance of 30%, and a minimum intensity threshold. Peak intensities were normalized to the total spectral intensity to correct for inter-sample variations.</p><p><br></p><p>Quality control filtering was applied to retain reliable features: compounds with a coefficient of variation (CV) of relative peak areas greater than 30% in QC samples were excluded. For non-normally distributed data, relative peak areas were calculated using the formula: sample raw quantitation value / (sum of sample metabolite quantitation values / sum of QC1 sample metabolite quantitation values).</p><p><br></p><p>2. Metabolite Annotation Workflow</p><p><br></p><p>Annotated metabolites were derived through a multi-step process combining molecular formula prediction and database matching:</p><p><br></p><p>Molecular formula prediction: Normalized data from Compound Discoverer 3.3 were used to infer molecular formulas based on additive ions, molecular ion peaks, and fragment ions, ensuring consistency with high-resolution mass spectral data.</p><p><br></p><p>Database matching: Peaks were matched against three primary reference databases for qualitative confirmation:</p><p><br></p><p>mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org/)</p><p><br></p><p>mzVault (ThermoFisher internal database)</p><p><br></p><p>MassList (ThermoFisher internal database)</p><p><br></p><p>Functional annotation: Further functional annotation of identified metabolites was conducted using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html), Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, https://hmdb.ca/metabolites), and LIPIDMaps database (http://www.lipidmaps.org/), to link metabolites to biological pathways and functional categories.</p><p><br></p><p>3. Software and Tools for Data Transformation and Validation</p><p><br></p><p>Data conversion and integration: Raw data (.raw format) were processed for compatibility using relevant modules within Compound Discoverer 3.3 and XCMS, ensuring seamless integration of results from both processing pipelines.</p><p><br></p><p>Statistical validation software: metaX (a specialized metabolomics data processing tool) was used to convert data formats and support partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), generating variable importance in projection (VIP) scores for metabolites. Additional statistical analyses (including t-tests for fold change calculation) were performed using R (version 3.4.3) and Python (version 2.7.6), with CentOS (release 6.6) as the operating environment.</p><p><br></p><p>Visualization tools: ggplot2 and Pheatmap packages in R were used for generating volcano plots and clustering heatmaps, respectively, to visualize differential metabolites. Correlation analyses between metabolites were conducted using the cor() function (Pearson method) and cor.mtest() in R, with corrplot for correlation plot visualization.</p>

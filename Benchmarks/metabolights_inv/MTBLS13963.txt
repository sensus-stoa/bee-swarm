Study Title	Multi-omics reveals effects of several rumen bacteria on reproductive performance of sheep
Study Description	<p>Background: Mounting evidence indicates that the rumen microbiota plays a crucial role in the reproductive health of sheep. However, the potential beneficial effects of rumen microbiota on lambing performance in sheep across different stages of the reproductive cycle and the precise mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear. We aimed to elucidate the rumen microbial regulatory network underlying differences in reproductive performance in sheep by integrating multi-stage metagenomics and metabolomics.</p><p>Results: The study found that although there was no significant difference in the ruminal microbial α-diversity between sheep with high and low litter size, significant stage-specific segregation was observed in their community structures. We identified a cohort of key species strongly associated with litter size. These included Asaia bogorensis, Methanolobus zinderi, Erwinia gerundensis, Marinobacter sp. BSs20148, and Lactobacillus amylolyticus, enriched during pregnancy; Rhizobium gallicum, Aeromonas caviae, Pseudolysobacter antarcticus, Mucilaginibacter rubeus, Thermococcus paralvinellae, and Janthinobacterium svalbardensis enriched during lactation; Pseudomonas mandelii, Gordonia sp. HY186, Arachidicoccus sp. BS20, Mesotoga prima, Acidovorax ebreus, Donacia cinerea, and Salmonella enterica enriched during estrus. Host plasma metabolomics analysis further revealed an enrichment of a set of core metabolites in the blood of high-fertility sheep, including Inositol, 2-Linoleoylglycerol, lysophosphatidylcholines (LPCs) and neuromodulatory substances such as tyramine and sphingosine-1-phosphate. We constructed stage-specific 'rumen microbe–rumen metabolite–plasma metabolite' regulatory axes. These results collectively demonstrate the influence of the rumen microbiome on plasma metabolic profiles and subsequent fertility outcomes in sheep.</p><p>Conclusion: We elucidates the dynamic mechanism by which the rumen microbiota in high-fertility sheep supports superior reproductive performance through stage-adaptive community succession and functional remodeling, which in turn regulates the host's neuroendocrine and lipid metabolic profiles. These findings provide a new perspective for understanding the regulation of fertility in ruminants and lay a theoretical foundation for improving reproductive efficiency through nutritional strategies targeting the rumen microbiota.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>All experimental animals were purebred Hu sheep raised on the same farm (Zhejiang Huaxin Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd, Quzhou, Zhejiang Province). The trial commenced on September 10, 2024. A total of 120 healthy sheep with good body condition and complete lambing records were selected and maintained under standardized management with a consistent diet throughout the study, details of total mixed ration (TMR)&nbsp;formulation are provided in Table 1. 35 days after the trial started, rams were introduced for natural mating. At 35 days post mating, sheep were divided into two groups based on the litter size of their previous parity: high fertility (≥3 lambs) and low fertility (single lamb). Pregnancy was confirmed by ultrasound. Six successfully pregnant sheep from each group were randomly selected, labeled G1 to G6 (high fertility) and D1 to D6 (low fertility), and transferred to the same pen for uniform management. Blood samples were collected at three time points: day 2 after grouping (Pregnancy), upon colostrum secretion after parturition (Lactation), and on the first day of estrus after weaning (Estrus). Blood (5 mL) was collected from the jugular vein into EDTA coated tubes. Tubes were gently inverted, transported on ice at 4℃, and centrifuged (4℃, 3000 rpm, 15 min) to harvest plasma, aliquoted, frozen at -20 ℃, and stored long term at -80 ℃.&nbsp;</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on a Thermo Scientific Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) using a Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and an injection volume of 3 µL. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B), with the following gradient: 0-1 min, 5-20% B; 1-3 min, 20-99% B; 3-4.5 min, hold at 99% B; 4.5-4.6 min, return to 5% B; 4.6-6.0 min, hold at 5% B, resulting in a total run time of 6.0 min. Samples were prepared by thawing on ice, vortexing for 10 s, mixing 50 µL of sample with 300 µL of extraction solution (acetonitrile:methanol = 1:4, v/v) containing internal standards, vortexing for 3 min, centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, collecting 200 µL of supernatant, placing at -20 °C for 30 min, centrifuging again at 12,000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C, and transferring 180 µL of supernatant to LC vials for analysis.</p>	<p>The raw mass spectrometry data were converted to mzML format using ProteoWizard.&nbsp;</p>	<p>The sample stored at -80 °C refrigerator was thawed on ice and vortexed for 10 s. 50 μL of sample and 300 μL of extraction solution (ACN : Methanol = 1:4, V/V) containing internal standards were added into a 2 mL microcentrifugetube. The sample was vortexed for 3 min and then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 10 min (4 °C). 200 μL of the supernatant was collected and placed in -20 °C for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 3 min (4 °C). A 180 μL aliquots of supernatant were transferred for LC-MS analysis.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry analysis was performed on a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source. Data acquisition was carried out in both positive and negative ionization modes separately, with full scan analysis over a mass range of m/z 70 to 1000 at a resolution of 60,000. The ion spray voltage was set to 3.8 kV for positive mode and 3.4 kV for negative mode. Sheath gas (N2) flow rate was 60 arbitrary units, and auxiliary gas flow rate was 20 arbitrary units. The ion transfer tube temperature was maintained at 320 °C, and the vaporizer temperature was set to 350 °C. Data-dependent MS/MS acquisition was performed using stepped collision energies of 30, 40, and 50 eV, with the top 10 most abundant ions selected for fragmentation per scan cycle.</p>	<p>Peak extraction, alignment, and retention time correction were performed using XCMS. Peaks with a missing rate greater than 50% in any group were filtered out, and missing values were imputed using a combination of K-nearest neighbor (KNN) imputation and 1/5 minimum value method (if missing rate &gt;50%, 1/5 minimum value imputation was applied; if missing rate &lt;50%, KNN imputation was applied). Peak areas were corrected using the support vector regression (SVR) method. Metabolites were identified by searching the corrected and filtered peaks against an in-house laboratory database, integrated public databases, and a prediction database. Finally, compounds with an identification score greater than 0.5 and a coefficient of variation (CV) value less than 0.3 in quality control (QC) samples were retained. Data from positive and negative ionization modes were then merged, retaining the metabolite with the highest identification confidence level and the smallest CV value for each feature, resulting in the all_sample_data.xlsx file.</p>

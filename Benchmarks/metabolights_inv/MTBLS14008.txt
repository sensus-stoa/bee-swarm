Study Title	Heat Stress Drives Campylobacter–Cholic Acid Signaling to Skew Th17/Treg Balance and Promotes Colitis-Like Inflammation in Pigs
Study Description	<p>Background</p><p>Heat stress can induce severe inflammatory responses in the intestines of livestock and poultry, resulting in typical symptoms of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). The development and balance of Th17 and Treg cells play important roles in IBD. Studies have shown that heat stress leads to an imbalance in Th17/Treg cells, as well as structural changes in the intestinal microflora. However, whether the Th17/Treg cell imbalance induced by heat stress is related to alterations in the intestinal microflora and the underlying mechanisms involved remain poorly understood.</p><p>Results</p><p>Heat stress adversely affects the defense function of the porcine digestive system by altering colonic histopathology, disrupting the Th17/Treg cell ratio, and triggering inflammation. Metagenomic analysis revealed significant changes in the gut microbiota, particularly an increased relative abundance of Campylobacter in the intestinal tracts of heat-stressed pigs. Flow cytometry and single-cell transcriptomic analyses revealed significant upregulation of Th17 cell content, a decrease in Treg cells, and notable alterations in the Th17 cell differentiation pathway. To determine whether Campylobacter contributes to the Th17/Treg imbalance in heat-stressed pigs, different concentrations of Campylobacter were administered to mice via gavage. The results demonstrated that Campylobacter-treated mice presented decreased body weight, an increased disease activity index, shortened colon length, and a severe imbalance in the Th17/Treg cell ratio in the colon. Furthermore, intestinal metabolome analysis revealed a significant increase in bile acid abundance in both heat-stressed pigs and Campylobacter-treated mice. Feeding mice with bile acid induced an imbalance in Th17/Treg cells, indicating that bile acid is a key metabolite involved in the aggravation of colitis in mice following intragastric Campylobacter administration.</p><p>Conclusions</p><p>Heat stress increases the abundance of Campylobacter and its metabolite bile acid in the pig colon. Both play important roles in activating the Th17 cell differentiation signaling pathway, resulting in an imbalance of Th17/Treg cells and an inflammatory response.</p><p>Keywords: Heat stress, Pig, Intestinal microbiota, Campylobacter, Th17/Treg cells are imbalanced</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Intestinal contents were collected from mice in the control group, low-dose Campylobacter gavage group, medium-dose Campylobacter gavage group, and high-dose Campylobacter gavage group for untargeted metabolomic analysis.</p>	<p>The instrument platform for this LC-MS analysis was the AB SCIEX UHPLC-Triple TOF 6600 system. Chromatographic conditions were as follows: the chromatographic column was an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 µm; Waters, Milford, USA); mobile phase A was 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), and mobile phase B was 47.5% acetonitrile + 47.5% isopropanol + 5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid); the flow rate was 0.40 mL/min, the injection volume was 10 μL, and the column temperature was 45°C.</p>	<p>The raw data were imported into the metabolomics processing software ProgenesisQI v3.0 (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment, ultimately yielding a data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and peak intensity information.</p>	<p>1. Transfer 30.1 mg of the sample to a 2 mL centrifuge tube and add a 6 mm diameter grinding bead; 2. Add 400 µL of extraction buffer (methanol:water = 4:1 (v:v)) containing four internal standards (L-2-chlorophenylalanine (0.02 mg/mL), etc.); 3. Grind the frozen tissue for 6 minutes (-10°C, 50 Hz); 4. Perform low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 minutes (5°C, 40 kHz); 5. Store the sample at-20°C for 30 minutes; 6. Centrifuge for 15 minutes (13,000 g, 4°C), transfer the supernatant to a sample vial with an insert, and analyze using the instrument; 7. Additionally, take 20 µL of supernatant from each sample, mix them, and use as quality control samples.</p>	<p>The instrument platform for this LC-MS analysis was the AB SCIEX UHPLC-Triple TOF 6600 system, which features ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem time-of-flight mass spectrometry.</p><p>The mass spectrometry was performed by electrospray ionization with positive and negative ion modes.</p>	<p>Subsequently, the software was employed for feature peak search and library identification, matching MS and MS/MS mass spectrometry data with metabolic databases. The MS mass error was set to less than 10 ppm, and metabolites were identified based on the matching scores from the secondary mass spectrometry. The primary databases included mainstream public databases such as http://www.hmdb.ca/ and https://metlin.scripps.edu/, as well as self-built databases.</p>

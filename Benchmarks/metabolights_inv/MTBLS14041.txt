Study Title	Coupled nitrogen fixation and substrate stabilization drive ecological strategy shifts during microbial succession
Study Description	<p>Background:</p><p>Primary succession is predicted to proceed through shifts from stress-tolerant to competitive to cooperative ecological strategies as resource availability increases, yet molecular evidence for these transitions in microbial systems remains limited. Here, we used an integrated multi-omics approach to investigate how microbial functional traits and chemical outputs change across early ecosystem development using experimental basaltic hillslopes spanning succession from bare rock through biocrust to moss-dominated communities.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>Results:</p><p>Genome-resolved analyses revealed stress-tolerant pioneer communities enriched in oxidative and temperature stress-response genes on nitrogen-limited and physically unstable basalt. These communities were replaced by competitive nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria that formed biocrusts enriched in carbon and nitrogen fixation pathways while stabilizing substrates through exopolysaccharide production. This stabilization enabled the establishment of functionally diverse moss-associated communities characterized by complete biogeochemical cycling and cooperative metabolic interactions. Metabolomic profiling linked these functional transitions to distinct chemical signatures across succession. Early stages were enriched in organic nitrogen- and sulfur-containing compounds, intermediate stages accumulated nucleosides and organic acids, and later stages were characterized by complex lipids and secondary metabolites. Metabolites discriminated successional stages with substantially higher accuracy than taxonomic markers, indicating that functional outputs in this system more reliably capture ecosystem state than community composition. While microbial community assembly shifted from stochastic to deterministic processes over succession, metabolite assembly remained predominantly deterministic throughout.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>Conclusions:</p><p>Integration across genomic and metabolomic data demonstrates that cyanobacterial nitrogen fixation coupled with substrate stabilization alleviates nutrient limitation and modifies physical habitat conditions, driving successional progression through ecological strategy shifts. These results advance succession theory by showing that biogeochemical and physical processes operate in tandem rather than sequentially. These molecular insights provide the first comprehensive evidence for predicted ecological strategy shifts at the molecular level and offer a framework for ecosystem monitoring and restoration, with stage specific metabolic signatures serving as quantitative biomarkers for assessing recovery trajectories.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Surface soil samples were collected from the Landscape Evolution Observatory (LEO) at Biosphere 2 (Arizona, USA), which consists of three replicate basaltic hillslopes constructed from crushed basaltic tephra. Each hillslope measures approximately 30 m in length, 11 m in width, and 1 m in depth and is positioned at approximately 10 degrees slope to simulate simplified natural landscapes undergoing early ecosystem development. Six sampling locations were established on each hillslope to capture topographic variability. At each location, surface material representing three biological cover types was sampled: bare basalt substrate, cyanobacteria-dominated biological soil crust (biocrust), and moss-dominated surfaces. These cover types represent progressive stages of ecological succession. Collected samples were transported on ice and stored at −80 °C until metabolite extraction and analysis.</p>	<p>LC-MS/MS raw data were processed using Compound Discoverer version 3.3 following an untargeted metabolomics workflow. Data processing included spectral alignment, retention time correction, peak detection, and feature grouping across samples. Quality control correction was performed using a linear regression model within Compound Discoverer to correct signal drift across the analytical sequence. Features were retained when quality control peak area relative standard deviation was below 30 percent and correction values remained below 25 percent. Duplicate features and in-source fragments were manually reviewed and removed based on spectral quality, retention time, and peak shape.</p><p><br></p><p>FT-ICR MS spectra were processed using Bruker Data Analysis software version 5.0. Peak picking was performed using the FTMS peak picker module with a signal-to-noise threshold of 7 and an absolute intensity threshold of 100. Internal calibration was applied using homologous series of organic matter compounds separated by 14 Da.</p>	<p>Water-soluble metabolites were extracted from soil samples by adding 10 mL of Milli-Q water to 5 g of wet soil in sterile 50 mL centrifuge tubes. Samples were vortexed twice for 30 seconds to mix the soil suspension and sonicated for 2 hours at 22 °C to facilitate extraction of dissolved organic matter. Samples were then centrifuged and approximately 9 mL of the supernatant was collected as the aqueous extract. The extraction procedure was repeated three times and the resulting supernatants were pooled to generate a representative extract for each sample. Extracts were stored at −80 °C until analysis.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>For LC-MS/MS analysis, water extracts were divided into two aliquots for hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) and reverse-phase (RP) separations. Extracts were dried completely using a Genevac Series II evaporation system freeze-dryer. For HILIC analysis, dried extracts were reconstituted in a solvent mixture consisting of 50 percent acetonitrile and 50 percent water. For RP analysis, dried extracts were reconstituted in 80 percent water and 20 percent HPLC-grade methanol. Reconstituted samples were vortexed and transferred to autosampler vials prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>For FT-ICR MS analysis, water extracts were purified using solid phase extraction with Bond Elut PPL cartridges (Agilent Technologies) to remove salts and contaminants. Prior to loading onto cartridges, extracts were acidified to pH 2–3 using hydrochloric acid. Samples were passed through the cartridges under vacuum, washed with 15 mL of 0.01 M HCl, dried, and eluted with 1.5 mL methanol into autosampler vials. Purified extracts were stored at −80 °C until FT-ICR MS analysis.</p>	<p>LC-MS/MS analyses were performed using a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer equipped with a heated electrospray ionization source. For reverse-phase LC-MS/MS analysis, spectra were acquired in positive ion mode with a spray voltage of 3500 V. For HILIC LC-MS/MS analysis, spectra were acquired in negative ion mode with a spray voltage of 2500 V. The ion transfer tube and vaporizer temperatures were maintained at 350 °C. Data-dependent MS/MS acquisition was used to obtain fragmentation spectra, and precursor ions were fragmented using higher-energy collisional dissociation with stepped collision energies of 20, 40, and 80.</p><p><br></p><p>Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR MS) analysis was performed using a 9.4 Tesla Bruker SolariX MRMS instrument equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in negative ion mode. Samples were introduced via automated direct infusion using Flow Injection Analysis (FIA). The instrument collected spectra across an m/z range of 150 to 1000 at a resolution of 8 million. A needle voltage of +4.0 kV was applied and 300 scans were co-added for each sample to improve signal-to-noise ratios. Mass measurement accuracy was maintained within 1 ppm using internal calibration based on homologous series separated by 14 Da.</p>	<p>Metabolite annotation for LC-MS/MS data was performed using a multi-step workflow incorporating spectral library matching, database searching, and computational prediction tools. Fragmentation spectra, retention times, and precursor ion masses were compared against an in-house spectral library of approximately 1200 reference standards stored in MzVault. MS/MS spectra were also searched against the mzCloud spectral database containing curated MSn spectra. Candidate molecular compositions were predicted using exact mass measurements, isotopic pattern matching, and MS/MS fragmentation data with a mass tolerance of 5 ppm. Additional candidate structures were searched using ChemSpider.</p><p><br></p><p>Further annotation support was provided using SIRIUS for formula prediction and fragmentation analysis, while chemical classification was assigned using ClassyFire. Annotation confidence levels were assigned according to Metabolomics Standards Initiative guidelines, including Level 1 confirmed identifications using reference standards, Level 2 putative identifications based on spectral library matches, and Level 3 putative annotations based on molecular formula or chemical class predictions.</p><p><br></p><p>For FT-ICR MS data, molecular formulas were assigned using Formularity with criteria including signal-to-noise ratios greater than or equal to 7, mass error less than or equal to 0.5 ppm, and elemental constraints allowing carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separations for LC-MS/MS analyses were performed using a Thermo Scientific Vanquish Duo ultra-high performance liquid chromatography system. Water extracts were split into two aliquots for hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) and reverse-phase (RP) separations. Samples were dried using a Genevac Series II evaporation system freeze-dryer prior to analysis. HILIC samples were reconstituted in 50 percent acetonitrile and 50 percent water, while RP samples were reconstituted in 80 percent water and 20 percent HPLC-grade methanol.</p><p><br></p><p>For HILIC analysis, metabolites were separated on a Waters ACQUITY BEH Amide column. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1 percent formic acid and 10 mM ammonium acetate in 90 percent acetonitrile and 10 percent water, while mobile phase B consisted of 0.1 percent formic acid and 10 mM ammonium acetate in 50 percent acetonitrile and 50 percent water. The gradient progressed from 99 percent mobile phase A to 95 percent mobile phase B over approximately 16 minutes.</p><p><br></p><p>For RP analysis, metabolites were separated using a Waters ACQUITY HSS T3 C18 column. The gradient progressed from 99 percent mobile phase A (0.1 percent formic acid in water) to 95 percent mobile phase B (0.1 percent formic acid in methanol) over 16 minutes.</p><p><br></p><p>Both columns were operated at a temperature of 45 °C with a flow rate of 300 microliters per minute and an injection volume of 1 microliter.</p>	<p>Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR MS) analysis was performed using a 9.4 Tesla Bruker SolariX MRMS instrument equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in negative ion mode. Samples were introduced via automated direct infusion using Flow Injection Analysis (FIA)</p>

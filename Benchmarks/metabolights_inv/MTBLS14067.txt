Study Title	Royal jelly and 10-hydroxy-2-decenoic acid mitigate alcoholic fatty liver disease in mice via the gut-microbiota-metabolite axis
Study Description	<p>Royal jelly (RJ) and its signature bioactive compound, 10-hydroxy-2-decenoic acid (10-HDA), are believed to possess hepatoprotective properties. However, their protective effects and underlying mechanisms against alcoholic fatty liver disease (AFLD) remain unclear. In the present study, we integrated 16S rRNA sequencing, untargeted metabolomics, and molecular biology analyses to evaluate their therapeutic potential in an AFLD mouse model. The results demonstrated that RJ and 10-HDA significantly improved alcohol-induced liver injury by reducing lipid accumulation and mitigating dyslipidemia, inflammation, and oxidative stress. The 16S rRNA sequencing revealed that RJ and 10-HDA ameliorated alcohol-induced gut microbiota disruption. In particular, supplementation with beneficial doses of RJ (200 mg/kg/day) and 10-HDA (100 mg/kg/day)&nbsp;reduced the abundance of Pseudomonadota and Escherichia, and increased the abundance of Verrucomicrobiota (e.g., Akkermansia) and Lactobacillus. Untargeted metabolomics further indicated that the host metabolic regulation involved key pathways such as serotonergic synapse, bile secretion, and tryptophan metabolism. Moreover, RJ and 10-HDA activated the adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway, promoting fatty acid β-oxidation while suppressing lipogenesis. Notably, integrated correlation analysis revealed that the beneficial faecal metabolites restored by the intervention were closely associated with the activation of the hepatic AMPK pathway. In contrast, alcohol-enriched deleterious metabolites exhibited the opposite trend, linking luminal metabolic shifts to upstream hepatic lipid regulation.&nbsp;Collectively, these findings suggest that RJ and 10-HDA mitigate AFLD by modulating the gut-microbiota-metabolite axis and activating the AMPK signaling pathway, supporting their potential use as dietary supplements in strategies aimed at managing AFLD and promoting liver health.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>1. Animal Treatment and Fecal Sample Collection</p><p>Male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old, 20 ± 2 g) were obtained from Sibeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (License number: SCXK (Beijing) 2024-0001). All mice were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities with controlled temperature and humidity (21–25°C, 12-hour light/dark cycle) and had free access to food and water. After one week of acclimatization, the mice were randomly allocated into seven groups (n = 6 per group). The alcoholic fatty liver disease (AFLD) model was established using chronic alcohol feeding combined with acute alcohol gavage. Mice in the control group (Con) received a Lieber-DeCarli (LD) liquid diet for 15 days. Mice in the other groups were gradually adapted to a 5% (v/v) ethanol LD liquid diet over 5 days and then maintained on this diet for an additional 10 days. On day 16, all ethanol-fed mice received a single oral gavage of 31.5% (v/v) ethanol (5 g/kg body weight), while the Con group received an isocaloric maltodextrin solution.</p><p>Based on physiological and biochemical evaluations, four representative groups (n = 6 per group) were selected for untargeted metabolomics analysis: the control group (Con), the model group (Mol), the high-dose royal jelly group (H-RJ, 200 mg/kg/day), and the high-dose 10-HDA group (H-10-HDA, 100 mg/kg/day). Fecal samples were collected from each mouse under sterile conditions on day 15, strictly representing the period after the chronic feeding phase and exactly one day prior to the final acute alcohol gavage. Upon collection, the fresh fecal samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further analysis.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on an HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 µm). The column temperature was maintained at 40°C. The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min. The mobile phase A was water/acetonitrile (95/5, v/v) with 0.1% formic acid. Mobile phase B was acetonitrile/isopropanol/water (47.5/47.5/5, v/v/v) with 0.1% formic acid. The injection volume was 3 µL.</p>	<p>The raw LC-MS data were imported into Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for preprocessing. The workflow included baseline filtering, peak picking, integration, retention time correction, and alignment. The resulting data matrix containing retention time, m/z, and peak intensities was then exported for further analysis.</p>	<p>50 mg of fecal sample was placed in a 2 mL centrifuge tube with one 6 mm grinding bead. 400 µL of extraction solvent (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v) containing internal standards (including 0.02 mg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanine) was added. The mixture was homogenized in a frozen tissue grinder at -10°C, 50 Hz for 6 min, followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction at 5°C, 40 kHz for 30 min. The sample was then incubated at -20°C for 30 min and centrifuged at 4°C, 13000 g for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to an autosampler vial with an insert for LC-MS analysis. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing equal volumes of supernatant from all samples. QC samples were injected every 5-10 sample runs to monitor the repeatability of the analysis.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry analysis was performed on a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system. Data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes. The mass scan range was m/z 70-1050. The ion spray voltage was set to 3500 V for positive mode and -3000 V for negative mode. Sheath gas flow rate was 50 arb, auxiliary gas flow rate was 13 arb, and ion source heating temperature was 450°C. The stepped normalized collision energy (NCE) was set to 20-40-60 V.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed by matching the mass spectrometry data (accurate mass and MS/MS fragmentation patterns) against public databases including the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB,&nbsp;http://www.hmdb.ca/), Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and the in-house Majorbio database. The data processing and identification steps were integrated within the Progenesis QI software workflow.</p>

Study Title	Fermented cotton stalks preserve colonic epithelial integrity in Hu sheep by modulating the microbiota–metabolite–NF-kB/MLCK axis
Study Description	<p>This study compared three cotton-stalk processing strategies—grinding (FS), steam explosion (PH), and microbial fermentation (FJ)—and evaluated whether the barrier-preserving phenotype observed with fermented cotton stalks is associated with coordinated changes in the microbiota–metabolite milieu and NF-?B/MLCK-related mucosal signaling in Hu sheep. Fifteen clinically healthy Hu sheep (26.7 ± 1.76 kg body weight; 115 ± 4 d of age) were used after a 14-d adaptation period and randomly assigned to one of three diets (n = 5 per treatment) containing 40% processed cotton stalks (FS, PH, or FJ) for 8 weeks. The results showed that PH and FJ increased final body weight compared with FS, and average daily gain increased progressively from FS to PH to FJ (206.07, 282.50, and 322.14 g/d, respectively; p &lt; 0.05). Colonic fermentation profiles were markedly improved by FJ, evidenced by lower pH, ammonia nitrogen, free gossypol, and acetate (p &lt; 0.05), alongside higher total VFAs with elevated propionate and butyrate (p &lt; 0.05), whereas LPS was not different among treatments (p &gt; 0.05). Histology and scanning electron microscopy indicated that FJ maintained intact crypt architecture and epithelial surface continuity, while FS exhibited epithelial detachment and surface erosion. Metagenomic analysis revealed distinct community structures among groups, with FJ showing higher richness and enrichment of taxa associated with carbohydrate utilization and butyrate-producing guilds (e.g., Lachnospiraceae-related genera such as Anaerostipes, Blautia, and Coprococcus). Consistently, FJ suppressed colonic mucosal inflammation, as reflected by reduced IL-1ß, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-a at both mRNA and protein levels (p &lt; 0.05). Mechanistically, FJ attenuated NF-?B activation and downstream MLCK signaling, shown by decreased p-p65/p65, p-I?B/I?B, MLCK abundance, and p-MLC/MLC ratio (p &lt; 0.05), while upregulating tight-junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin, claudin-1, and claudin-4; p &lt; 0.05). In conclusion, fermentation-based processing of cotton stalks enhanced growth performance and was accompanied by a favorable hindgut fermentation and microbial–metabolic milieu, together with reinforced colonic barrier integrity and reduced NF-?B/MLCK-associated inflammatory signaling, supporting fermented cotton stalk as a practical strategy to valorize cotton residues for ruminant feeding while mitigating gossypol-related hindgut stress.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Chromatography</p><p>Metabolomic profiling of colonic digesta samples was conducted using an ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled to a high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer (UHPLC–MS/MS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes for metabolite separation and detection.</p><p>1. Chromatographic Column</p><p>A reversed-phase analytical column was employed for the separation of small-molecule metabolites in colonic digesta samples (consistent with the column configuration for the Thermo Fisher UHPLC–MS/MS platform).</p><p>2. Mobile Phase</p><p>A binary gradient elution system was used, consisting of an aqueous phase and an organic phase (acetonitrile or methanol); both phases typically contained 0.1% formic acid (or another suitable modifier) as the additive.</p><p>3. Gradient Elution Program</p><p>Elution was performed following a binary gradient elution program optimized for the separation of small-molecule metabolites in colonic digesta samples, with the proportion of aqueous and organic phases adjusted in a gradient mode to achieve efficient separation of different types of metabolites (including organic acids, amino acids, heterocyclic compounds, benzene and substituted derivatives, etc.).</p><p>4. Core Chromatographic Parameters</p><p>All operating parameters were set according to the standard operating procedures of the UHPLC–MS/MS platform for untargeted metabolomic analysis of biological samples, including:</p><p>Column temperature: Set to a constant temperature (optimized for metabolite separation stability)</p><p>Flow rate: Fixed flow rate for the mobile phase (to ensure uniform elution of metabolites)</p><p>Injection volume: Predefined volume for sample loading (matched to the detection sensitivity of the mass spectrometer and column capacity)</p><p>All parameters were optimized to ensure robust separation of the small-molecule metabolites in colonic digesta samples.</p><p>5. Sample Loading</p><p>The final metabolite extracts (after reconstitution and centrifugation to remove particulate matter) were transferred into autosampler vials and automatically injected into the UHPLC system for chromatographic separation, with the injection operation consistent with the standard sample introduction protocol of the Thermo Fisher UHPLC platform.</p>	<p>Data transformation</p><p>Raw liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) metabolomic data were processed by dedicated data processing software (used by Wuhan Maiwei Metabolomics Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) for a series of core data transformation and preprocessing steps, including peak detection, peak extraction, peak alignment, deconvolution and signal normalization.</p><p>Metabolic features defined by retention time and mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) with poor reproducibility or a high proportion of missing values were removed according to predefined criteria during data preprocessing.</p><p>Subsequently, the peak intensities obtained from LC–MS analysis were first log-transformed and then Pareto-scaled for normalization to eliminate systematic errors and ensure the comparability of metabolomic data across all experimental samples (FS, PH and FJ groups).</p><p>The resulting data matrix, consisting of annotated metabolites and their normalized signal intensities across all samples, was used for subsequent multivariate and univariate statistical analyses of the metabolomic data.</p>	<p>Metabolite Extraction and Sample Preparation Colonic digesta samples were stored at -80°C prior to metabolomic analysis and transported on dry ice to ensure metabolic stability throughout handling. All sample preparation steps were performed on ice to minimize metabolite degradation. 1. Metabolite Extraction 1. Frozen colonic digesta samples were thawed slowly on ice and thoroughly homogenized to ensure uniform consistency. 2. Approximately 100 mg of homogenized colonic digesta was accurately weighed into pre-cooled 2 mL centrifuge tubes for each biological replicate. 3. Ice-cold methanol-water mixture (70:30, v/v) containing internal standards was added to the centrifuge tubes at a fixed solid-to-liquid ratio; the mixture was vortexed vigorously for 3 min to achieve full mixing. 4. The tubes were subjected to ultrasonic extraction in an ice-water bath for 20 min (ultrasonic power: 100 W, working time: 3 s, interval time: 2 s) to enhance the release of small-molecule metabolites from the sample matrix. 5. After ultrasonic extraction, the samples were incubated at -20°C for 30 min to precipitate proteins and macromolecular impurities. 6. The precipitated samples were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C; the clear upper supernatants were carefully transferred to new pre-cooled centrifuge tubes to avoid contamination by precipitate.&nbsp;2. Sample Pre-treatment for Instrumental Analysis The collected supernatants were evaporated to near dryness under a gentle stream of high-purity nitrogen gas at room temperature (to prevent metabolite volatilization and degradation). The dried metabolite extracts were reconstituted in 50% methanol aqueous solution, vortexed for 2 min to fully dissolve, and briefly centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C to remove residual particulate matter. The final clear reconstituted solutions were transferred into autosampler vials with glass inserts for ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS) analysis. 3. Quality Control (QC) Sample Preparation Pooled QC samples were prepared to monitor instrumental stability, analytical reproducibility and correct for systematic errors during metabolomic analysis: 1. Equal volume aliquots (10 μL) of the clear supernatant from each individual biological sample (all FS, PH and FJ group samples) were collected and mixed into a single pooled solution. 2. The pooled solution was processed following the identical extraction, protein precipitation, centrifugation, nitrogen evaporation and reconstitution steps as the experimental samples to generate QC samples. 3. QC samples were placed in the autosampler alongside experimental samples and injected at regular intervals (every 6–8 experimental samples) throughout the entire UHPLC–MS/MS analytical sequence.&nbsp;4. Other Control Samples • Solvent blank: 50% methanol aqueous solution (the same reconstitution solvent used for samples) was injected into the UHPLC–MS/MS system to eliminate background interference from solvents and reagents. • Instrument blank: Pure mobile phase (consistent with the UHPLC mobile phase system) was run to verify the cleanliness of the chromatographic system and avoid cross-contamination between samples.&nbsp;All glassware and centrifuge tubes used in the experiment were pre-washed with methanol and ultrapure water, and dried at 60°C to remove any organic residues that may cause metabolite contamination.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry Metabolomic profiling of colonic digesta samples was performed on an ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to a high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer (UHPLC–MS/MS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Ion source &amp; Ionization mode The mass spectrometer was operated with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source in both positive and negative ionization modes for comprehensive metabolite detection. Mass scanning parameters Mass spectrometric data were acquired in full-scan mode combined with data-dependent MS/MS; the instrument was set with appropriate parameters for scan range, spray voltage, capillary temperature and gas flows (optimized for the detection of small-molecule metabolites in colonic digesta samples). Quality control for mass spectrometry Quality control (QC) samples were injected at regular intervals throughout the analytical sequence to evaluate retention time stability and signal drift of the mass spectrometry system, ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of metabolite detection data. All mass spectrometry operating parameters were set in accordance with the standard operating procedures of the Thermo Fisher Scientific UHPLC–MS/MS platform for untargeted metabolomic analysis of biological samples.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification</p><p>Putative metabolite identification for colonic digesta metabolomic data was conducted by matching the analytical features of detected metabolites against in-house and public metabolite databases via the following methodological pipeline:</p><p>Core matching criteria: Accurate mass values of the metabolites, MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and chromatographic retention times were the three key dimensions used for metabolite annotation and identification.</p><p>Database references: The identification process integrated in-house metabolite databases and public metabolite databases (the standard database combination for untargeted metabolomic profiling of biological samples) to improve the coverage and accuracy of metabolite annotation.</p><p>All metabolite identification operations were completed using dedicated metabolomic data processing software (matching the standard analysis pipeline of the commercial metabolomic service provider for UHPLC–MS/MS data) with predefined screening criteria for matching reliability. Only metabolic features with valid matching results against the above databases were annotated as putative metabolites and included in the subsequent data matrix for statistical analysis; unannotated metabolic features were excluded from further analysis.</p>	<p>All experimental samples were derived from the colonic contents of healthy sheep, with a total of 15 individual samples collected and equally divided into three groups (PH, FJ, FS; 5 biological replicates per group). The colonic content samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen right after collection to minimize metabolic degradation and maintain the in vivo metabolic profile of the samples. Subsequently, all frozen samples were transported to the laboratory under low-temperature conditions and stored at -80 °C ultra-low temperature refrigerator until further metabolic extraction and detection, with no additional pre-treatment performed during the storage period. Strict aseptic and low-temperature operation was maintained throughout the entire sample collection and transportation process to ensure the integrity and reliability of the metabolic components in the samples.</p>

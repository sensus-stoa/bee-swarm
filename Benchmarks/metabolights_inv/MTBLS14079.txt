Study Title	Ionomic, Metabolomic, and Enzymatic Responses of the Sea Cucumber Apostichopus japonicus to Single and Combined Salinity and Metal Stress
Study Description	<p>Desalination brine discharge poses emerging threats to benthic marine organisms through combined salinity stress and metal contamination. This study investigated the individual and combined effects of salinity (35 ppt) and metal exposure (Cu, Fe, and Cd) on the sea cucumber Apostichopus japonicus by integrating ionomics, enzymatic biomarkers, and LC-MS/MS-based metabolomics. After 16 days exposure to metals, only Cd and Cu significantly accumulated in body wall tissues. Combined high salinity and metals contributed to a slight increase in Fe accumulation and a decrease in Cu accumulation. Metal exposure disrupted the balance of essential elements (e.g., Mn, Zn, Co, Ni), with interactive effects modulated by salinity. Antioxidants and immune-related enzymes (SOD, CAT, ACP, ALP, Na-K-ATPase) responded distinctly to metal and salinity stress, with salinity often dominating the combined stress response. Metabolomic profiling showed that Cd and Cu under ambient salinity induced widespread metabolic perturbations, particularly in lipid metabolism, glutathione metabolism, and saponin biosynthesis. Notably, high salinity (35 ppt) alleviated some metal-specific metabolic effects, while salinity alone caused significant downregulation of bioactive saponins and flavonoids. KEGG pathway analysis highlighted disrupted purine metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, and the citrate cycle under combined stress. These findings demonstrate that salinity modulates metal toxicity in sea cucumbers, with potential consequences for immune function, oxidative defense, and nutritional value. This study provides novel insights into the ecological risks of desalination brine discharge on benthic ecosystems.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a Thermo Scientific UHPLC system (Vanquish Horizon) coupled with an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA). The column temperature was maintained at 40°C. The mobile phase consisted of A: 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid) and B: 47.5% acetonitrile + 47.5% isopropanol + 5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid). The injection volume was 3 μL.</p>	<p>The acquired raw data were imported into Progenesis QI software (v3.0, Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for processing. The workflow included baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment. This process generated a data matrix containing retention times, mass-to-charge ratios (m/z), and peak intensities.</p>	<p>For metabolomic analysis, a precise amount of sample (50 ± 5 mg) was weighed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube. Metabolites were extracted by adding 400 μL of an extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v), which contained four internal standards (e.g., 0.02 mg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanine). The mixture was processed using a frozen tissue grinder for 6 minutes (-10°C, 50 Hz). This was followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 minutes (5°C, 40 KHz). The sample was then left to stand at -20°C for 30 minutes. After centrifugation at 13,000×g for 15 minutes at 4°C, the supernatant was collected and transferred into injection vials for analysis. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing 20 μL of the supernatant from each individual sample.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry was performed using a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. The sample was ionized by electrospray ionization (ESI), and data were acquired in both positive and negative ion scanning modes. The scan range (m/z) was 70-1050. Key parameters included: sheath gas flow rate (arb): 50; auxiliary gas flow rate (arb): 15; heater temperature: 400°C; capillary temperature: 350°C; spray voltage (+): 3400 V; spray voltage (-): -2800 V; S-Lens RF Level: 50; normalized collision energy (%): 20, 40, 60; resolution (Full MS): 60000; resolution (MS²): 7500.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed using the same Progenesis QI software. Feature peaks were identified by matching the MS and MS/MS mass spectral information against several metabolite databases. The mass error tolerance was set to less than 10 ppm. The databases used included public databases such as the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, http://www.hmdb.ca/), METLIN (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and the proprietary Majorbio Database (MJDB).</p>	<p>The sea cucumbers (Apostichopus japonicus) used in this study were obtained from a commercial farm in Yantai City, Shandong Province, China. Healthy individuals with an initial body weight of 25.0 ± 5.0 g were selected and acclimated in tanks for one week prior to the experiment. During the acclimation period, the water temperature was maintained at 16.5 ± 1.5 °C, pH at 8 ± 0.2, and salinity at 29 ppt. The animals were fed daily with a diet of commercial sea mud powder mixed with commercial seaweed powder at a 1:1 ratio, and half the volume of seawater was renewed daily.</p><p>Following acclimation, healthy individuals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, with eight sea cucumbers placed in each 4 L aquarium. The experiment consisted of the following eight treatment groups, each with three biological replicates:</p><p>Control (Ctrl_S29): Salinity 29 ppt, clean seawater.</p><p>Copper exposure (Cu_S29): Salinity 29 ppt, amended with 50 μg/L of CuSO4.</p><p>Iron exposure (Fe_S29): Salinity 29 ppt, amended with 100 μg/L of FeCl3.</p><p>Cadmium exposure (Cd_S29): Salinity 29 ppt, amended with 20 μg/L of CdCl2.</p><p>High salinity control (Ctrl_S35): Salinity 35 ppt, clean seawater.</p><p>High salinity + Copper exposure (Cu_S35): Salinity 35 ppt, amended with 50 μg/L of CuSO4.</p><p>High salinity + Iron exposure (Fe_S35): Salinity 35 ppt, amended with 100 μg/L of FeCl3.</p><p>High salinity + Cadmium exposure (Cd_S35): Salinity 35 ppt, amended with 20 μg/L of CdCl2.</p><p>To maintain stable target pollutant concentrations, the exposure solutions were completely renewed every 24 hours throughout the experimental period. After 16 days of exposure, body wall tissues were sampled from all sea cucumbers. The samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at -80°C for subsequent ionomic, enzymatic, and metabolomic analyses.</p>

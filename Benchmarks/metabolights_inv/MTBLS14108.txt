Study Identifier	MTBLS14108
Study Title	ABA accumulation confers cross-adaptation to saline-alkali and cold stresses in alfalfa
Study Description	<p>Combined saline-alkali and cold stresses severely constrain plant growth at middle and high latitudes. Plants have evolved cross-adaptation mechanisms wherein exposure to one stress enhances resistance to another. However, the specific mechanisms driving cross-adaptation between saline-alkali and cold stresses in alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) remain to be elucidated. Here, we performed integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses coupled with functional validation to elucidate these mechanisms. We found that saline-alkali pretreatment significantly enhanced the tolerance of alfalfa to subsequent cold stress. Compared to cold stress alone, cross-stress conditions increased osmolyte content and photosynthetic efficiency, while alleviating cellular oxidative damage. Integrated omics analyses revealed that cross-stress specifically activated flavonoid biosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism and abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis and signaling pathways. This promoted the accumulation of endogenous ABA, flavonoids, and carbohydrates. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis identified MsNCED3 as a critical hub gene. Exogenous ABA improved photoprotection and sugar accumulation, and enhanced cold tolerance. MsNCED3 overexpression in alfalfa validated its pivotal role in cross-adaptation by elevating ABA levels and mitigating oxidative damage. In conclusion, we found that MsNCED3-mediated ABA accumulation, along with enhanced antioxidant and osmotic adjustment capabilities, serve as key mechanisms underlying the cross-adaptation of alfalfa to saline-alkali and cold stresses.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC UHPLC system equipped with an Agilent 1290 Infinity autosampler, using an ACQUIY UPLC BEH Amide analytical column coupled with a matched ACQUIY UPLC BEH Amide guard column for hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) analysis. The column temperature was maintained at 25 °C, with a mobile phase flow rate of 0.5 mL/min and an injection volume of 2 μL. The autosampler temperature was controlled at 4 ℃ to preserve metabolite stability, and a gradient elution program using water (25 mM ammonium acetate, 25 mM ammonia) and acetonitrile as mobile phases was applied. Samples were analyzed in randomized order, with QC samples inserted periodically to monitor system stability and data reliability.</p>	<p>Wiff&nbsp;mass spectrometry data were first converted to standard mzXML format using ProteoWizard MSConvert software, and then imported into MS-DIAL software for subsequent data processing, including peak picking, peak alignment, noise filtering, and data normalization.</p>	<p>A 50 mg aliquot of powdered tissue was extracted with 1 mL of pre-cooled 80% methanol aqueous solution (v/v), followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 minutes and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4℃. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm hydrophilic PTFE syringe filter to remove particulate impurities, and the resulting filtrate was used for UHPLC-Q-TOF MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots of all experimental sample extracts.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection was carried out using an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC UHPLC system coupled with an AB Sciex TripleTOF 6600 quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-TOF) mass spectrometer, equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in both positive and negative ion modes. The instrument was set to scan over an m/z range of 60-1000 Da for full MS acquisition and 25-1000 Da for auto MS/MS acquisition, using information dependent acquisition (IDA) in high sensitivity mode. Optimized ESI source parameters and collision energy settings were applied to ensure efficient ionization and accurate fragment ion detection for comprehensive non-targeted metabolite profiling.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was conducted via database matching against a proprietary in-house database from Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd and multiple public metabolomics databases, including Fiehn HILIC, ReSpect, GNPS, MetaboBASE, OTCML, Fiehn/Vaniya, natural product database, HMDB, and Mass Bank. The identification criteria were set as an MS1 m/z tolerance of ± 5 ppm and an MS/MS similarity score greater than 0.7. CAMERA software was used to annotate isotopes and adduct ions, and only metabolite features with more than 50% non-zero values in at least one experimental group were retained for further analysis. Final compound confirmation was performed by comparing accurate mass (± 5 ppm tolerance) and MS/MS fragmentation spectra with an in-house authentic standard database.</p>	<p>Organism: Medicago sativa L. </p><p>Sample Type: Leaf tissue samples</p><p>Analysis Type: Non-targeted metabolomics profiling</p><p>Frozen alfalfa leaf samples were ground into fine powder under liquid nitrogen to prevent metabolite degradation.</p>

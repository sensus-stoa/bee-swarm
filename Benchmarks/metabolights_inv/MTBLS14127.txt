Study Title	Bioinspired Nanozyme-Exoskeletonized Yeast-derived Polysaccharides for Treating Comorbid Anxiety and Depression in Inflammatory Bowel Disease
Study Description	<p>Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic condition frequently accompanied by psychological comorbidities, such as anxiety and depression. Although current treatments may alleviate some of these symptoms, the intricate pathogenesis of these comorbidities highlights an urgent need for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. Herein, we develop a series of nanozyme-exoskeletonized yeast-derived polysaccharides (NZEP) by integrating nanozymes with yeast-derived polysaccharides using an insect sclerotization-inspired strategy. Among them, NZEPTA-Ce, with the highest degree of nanozyme-exoskeletonization, is capable of disrupting the inflammatory vicious cycle by inhibiting MAPK and NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling pathways. Oral administration of NZEPTA-Ce&nbsp;in male mice enables effective retention and accumulation in inflamed colonic regions, reverses the homeostasis imbalance of intestinal inflammation and promoting intestinal barrier repair. Furthermore, NZEPTA-Ce&nbsp;treatment&nbsp;reverses gut microbiota dysbiosis and optimize the composition of microbial metabolites, thereby alleviating anxiety and depressive symptoms associated with IBD. This study provides a safe and promising therapeutic strategy for the management of IBD patients and their associated psychological comorbidities.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The analytical platform consisted of a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class Plus coupled with a Thermo QExactive high-resolution mass spectrometer (UHPLC-MS/MS), and an Agilent 7890B-5977A gas chromatography-mass spectrometry system (GC-MS).</p><p>3.4.1. Chromatographic Conditions</p><p>LC Conditions</p><p>Column:&nbsp;ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm)</p><p>Column temperature:&nbsp;45 °C</p><p>Mobile phase:&nbsp;A: water (containing 0.1% formic acid); B: acetonitrile</p><p>Flow rate:&nbsp;0.35 mL/min</p><p>Injection volume:&nbsp;2 μL</p><p>GC Conditions</p><p>Column:&nbsp;DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent J&amp;W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA)</p><p>Carrier gas:&nbsp;High-purity helium (purity ≥ 99.999%)</p><p>Flow rate:&nbsp;1.0 mL/min</p><p>Inlet temperature:&nbsp;260 °C</p><p>Injection volume:&nbsp;1 μL, splitless injection</p><p>Solvent delay:&nbsp;5 min</p><p>Temperature program:&nbsp;The oven temperature was initially set at 60 °C and held for 0.5 min; then increased to 125 °C at 8 °C/min; subsequently increased to 210 °C at 8 °C/min; then increased to 270 °C at 15 °C/min; finally increased to 305 °C at 20 °C/min and held for 5 min.</p>	<p>Progenesis QI v3.0</p><p>Prior to pattern recognition, the raw data were subjected to preprocessing using the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI v3.0 (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle, UK) for baseline filtering, peak detection, integration, retention time correction, peak alignment, and normalization. The main parameters were as follows:</p><p>Precursor tolerance:&nbsp;5 ppm (HMDB + Lipidmaps) / 10 ppm (LuMet-Animal + METLIN)</p><p>Product tolerance:&nbsp;10 ppm (HMDB + Lipidmaps) / 20 ppm (LuMet-Animal + METLIN)</p><p>MS-DIAL v4.24</p><p>MS-DIAL was used for small molecule deconvolution and qualitative and quantitative analysis of GC-MS data acquired in full scan mode. Specifically, the raw GC-MS data (in .D format) were first converted to analysis base file (.abf) format using AnalysisBaseFileConverter software for rapid data retrieval. The converted data were then imported into MS-DIAL software for preprocessing. An algorithm was applied to extract 'model peaks' from the chromatograms, remove background noise, and achieve compound identification and quantification by matching retention time and fragment ion mass spectra against an in-house database.</p><p>MS-DIAL performed a series of processing steps on the imported data, including peak detection, peak identification, deconvolution, compound identification, peak alignment, filtering, and missing value imputation. Finally, a raw data matrix was exported, containing the following information: sample information, compound peak names, retention times, mass-to-charge ratios (m/z), and mass spectral response intensities (peak areas).</p>	<p>Weigh 60 mg of sample into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Add two small steel beads and 600 μL of methanol-water (4:1, v/v, containing mixed internal standards at 4 μg/mL).</p><p>Pre-cool the tube at –40 °C for 2 min, then place it into a grinder and homogenize at 60 Hz for 2 min.</p><p>Perform ultrasound extraction in an ice-water bath for 10 min, followed by standing at –40 °C for 2 h.</p><p>Centrifuge at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. Withdraw 150 μL of the supernatant using a syringe, filter through a 0.22 μm organic-phase syringe filter, and transfer the filtrate into an LC injection vial. Store at –80 °C until LC-MS analysis.</p><p>Simultaneously, transfer 100 μL of the supernatant into a glass derivatization vial and evaporate to dryness using a centrifugal concentrator.</p><p>Add 80 μL of methoxyamine hydrochloride in pyridine solution (15 mg/mL) to the glass derivatization vial. Incubate at 37 °C for 60 min in a shaking incubator to allow oximation reaction.</p><p>Afterward, add 50 μL of BSTFA derivatization reagent, 20 μL of n-hexane, and 10 μL of a mixture of ten internal standards (C8/C9/C10/C12/C14/C16/C18/C20/C22/C24, prepared in chloroform). React at 70 °C for 60 min.</p><p>After removal from the incubator, allow the sample to stand at room temperature for 30 min prior to GC-MS metabolomics analysis.</p><p>Quality control (QC) samples are prepared by pooling equal volumes of extracts from all samples.</p><p>Note:&nbsp;All extraction reagents are pre-cooled at –20 °C before use</p>	<p>Mass Spectrometry Parameters – LC-MS/MS</p><p><br></p><p>Parameter Positive Ion Mode Negative Ion Mode</p><p>Spray Voltage (V) 3800 –3000</p><p>Capillary Temperature (°C) 320 320</p><p>Aux Gas Heater Temperature (°C) 350 350</p><p>Sheath Gas Flow Rate (Arb) 35 35</p><p>Aux Gas Flow Rate (Arb) 8 8</p><p>S-lens RF Level5050Mass Range (m/z) 70–1050 70–1050</p><p>Full MS Resolution 70,000 70,000</p><p>MS/MS Resolution 17,500 17,500</p><p>NCE / Stepped NCE 10, 20, 40 10, 20, 40</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p>Mass Spectrometry Conditions – GC</p><p>Electron impact (EI) ion source was used with an ion source temperature of 230 °C, quadrupole temperature of 150 °C, and electron energy of 70 eV. The acquisition was performed in full scan mode (SCAN) with a mass scan range of m/z 50–500.</p>	<p>Compound Identification</p><p>Compound identification was performed based on multiple dimensions, including retention time (RT), accurate mass, MS/MS fragments, and isotopic distribution, using The Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), Lipidmaps (v2.3), the METLIN database, and the LuMet-Animal 3.0 local database.</p><p>The LuMet-Animal 3.0 database is an LC-MS/MS database established by Lumingbio through standardized hardware systems and based on reference standards. This database comprehensively covers over 10,000 common and important metabolites, including more than 2,000 compounds with reference standards (containing identification information across three dimensions: RT, MS¹, and MS²). It encompasses a total of 13 major classes of metabolites, primarily including amino acids and their derivatives, organic acids and their derivatives, carbohydrates and their derivatives, organic heterocyclic compounds, nucleosides and their derivatives, indoles and their derivatives, steroids and their derivatives, bile acids and their derivatives, acylcarnitines, lysophospholipids, benzenoids, and exposome-related compounds, among others. It covers dozens of important metabolic pathways, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, phenylalanine metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, primary bile acid biosynthesis, and glycerophospholipid metabolism.</p><p>For GC-MS identification, the LuMet-GC 5.0 database (Untarget database of GC-MS from Lumingbio), independently developed by Lumingbio, was used. This database contains 2,543 metabolites detectable by GC-MS, with continuous updates. The mass-to-charge ratio ranges from 85 to 650, covering a wide variety of compounds including lipids, amino acids, fatty acids, amines, alcohols, sugars, amino sugars, sugar alcohols, sugar acids, organic phosphates, hydroxy acids, aromatic compounds, purines, and sterols. The metabolites in this database are annotated with identifiers such as HMDB, KEGG, and CAS numbers, as well as compound classification, providing comprehensive information to facilitate subsequent functional studies of metabolites.</p><p>For GC-MS experiments involving the study of volatile substances and similar compounds using headspace sampling, as well as wax component analysis, the NIST database (https://webbook.nist.gov/chemistry/) was employed for compound identification and quantification. The NIST database is generally recognized as an EI-MS database; the latest version of the NIST database also contains ESI MS/MS spectra of small molecule compounds, including chemical standards of metabolites, lipids, and bioactive peptides.</p>	<p>Fresh fecal samples were collected individually from mice at the designated experimental time points. To avoid contamination, each mouse was placed in a sterile, empty cage until spontaneous defecation occurred. Fresh fecal pellets were immediately collected using sterile forceps and transferred into sterile microcentrifuge tubes. All samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 5 min of excretion and stored at&nbsp;−80&nbsp;°C&nbsp;until further analysis.</p>

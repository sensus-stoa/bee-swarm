Study Identifier	MTBLS14156
Study Title	Functional specialization of bacterial cellular and extracellular fractions contributes to polysaccharide degradation
Study Description	<p><em>Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron </em>(Bt), a key commensal of the human gut microbiota, exhibits robust capacity to degrade dietary polysaccharides via carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes). However, the exact spatial distribution and&nbsp;the functional coordination of these enzymes remain poorly understood. In this study, we&nbsp;characterized the “labor division”&nbsp;during inulin degradation across four cellular&nbsp;fractions&nbsp;of Bt: cytoplasm, outer membrane, extracellular vesicles&nbsp;(EVs), and supernatant. <em>In vitro</em>&nbsp;degradation confirmed&nbsp;that all four fractions exhibited potent&nbsp;hydrolytic activity. Proteomic analysis showed that all four fractions contained inulin-hydrolyzing CAZymes-primarily&nbsp;glycoside hydrolases and polysaccharide lyases-albeit with marked differences in hydrolase composition across fractions. Furthermore, untargeted metabolomic analysis of the hydrolysates identified 45 shared differential metabolites in pairwise comparisons, including carbohydrates and short-chain fatty acids. These results indicate that there are significant differences in the hydrolytic capacity among the various fractions. Notably, cross-feeding experiments revealed that the hydrolytic products generated by EVs can support the growth of bacteria with limited utilization capabilities. Taken together, our results highlight a synergistic multi-fraction strategy for inulin degradation in <em>Bacteroides</em>, offering novel insights into the polysaccharide utilization and a foundation for developing&nbsp;of postbiotics and functional microbial products.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Fermentation broth samples were collected from 4 experimental groups (total 12 samples: CP=3, CM=3, EV=3, SN=3). All samples were immediately frozen and stored at -80°C until further processing. No additional treatment was applied before sample preparation.</p>	<p>For polar metabolites, a Vanquish ultra-high performance liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used in this project. Chromatographic separation of target compounds was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (aqueous phase, containing 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia water) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The sample tray temperature was set at 4 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL.</p><p>An Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer was used to collect full-scan MS and MS/MS data under the control of Xcalibur software (version 4.4, Thermo). The detailed parameters were as follows: sheath gas flow rate: 50 Arb, auxiliary gas flow rate: 15 Arb, capillary temperature: 320 °C, full MS resolution: 60000, MS/MS resolution: 15000, collision energy: stepped normalized collision energy (SNCE) 20/30/40, spray voltage: 3.8 kV (positive mode) or -3.4 kV (negative mode).</p>	<p>Raw mass spectrometry data files (.raw) were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard software (version 3.0.24054). The converted data were then processed for peak picking, alignment, and feature annotation using the BiotreeDB (V3.0) metabolomics database. Subsequent data preprocessing included outlier filtering, missing value filtering (allowing ≤50% missing values in any group), missing value imputation (with half of the minimum value), and total ion current (TIC) normalization. All data processing steps were performed in accordance with standard non-targeted metabolomics workflows.</p>	<p>Samples were thawed on ice and subjected to metabolite extraction using the Starlid™ automated workstation.100 μL of the sample and 400 μL of the extraction solution (methanol:acetonitrile = 1:1, v/v, containing isotopic internal standards) were pipetted into a 96-well protein precipitation plate.The plate was shaken at 750 rpm for 5 minutes, allowed to stand for 5 minutes, then filtered and the filtrate was collected.An equal volume of the supernatant from all samples was pooled to prepare quality control (QC) samples for instrumental analysis.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source. Data were acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes. The full scan m/z range was 70-1050. The source parameters were set as follows: spray voltage of 3.8 kV (positive mode) and -3.4 kV (negative mode), capillary temperature of 320 ℃, sheath gas flow rate of 50 Arb, auxiliary gas flow rate of 15 Arb. The MS1 resolution was 60000, and the MS2 resolution was 15000 with stepped normalized collision energy (SNCE) of 20/30/40. Data acquisition was controlled by Xcalibur 4.4 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed by matching the MS/MS fragmentation spectra and accurate mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of detected features against the in-house BiotreeDB (V3.0) metabolomics database. The identification workflow included retention time correction, MS/MS spectral matching, and mass error filtering (mass tolerance ≤ 5 ppm for MS1, ≤ 10 ppm for MS2). Features with a matching score above the threshold were annotated with metabolite names, chemical formulas, and KEGG/HMDB identifiers. All annotations were performed in accordance with the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) level 2 criteria.</p>

Study Title	Structural and metabolic foundations of extreme animal thermophily
Study Description	<p>This project investigates the metabolic response of the deep sea hydrothermal vent worm Alvinellidae to acute heat stress. These animals are among the most thermotolerant metazoans known, yet the underlying metabolic adaptations remain poorly understood. To characterize the heat-induced metabolome, we exposed individuals to high temperatures and analyzed the resulting metabolic profiles.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>To characterize the metabolic shifts under heat stress in deep-sea hydrothermal vent organisms, an independent thermal exposure trial was conducted using the alvinellid worm, Paralvinella hessleri. Specimens were placed in 300-mL plastic jars filled with Filtered Sea Water (FSW). Each jar contained twelve individuals, with five individuals per group selected as biological replicates for metabolomic analysis.</p><p>The experimental design compared a control group at 20°C (representing the average in situ colony temperature) and a heat-stress group at 45°C (representing the ultimate incipient lethal temperature). For metabolomic profiling, specimens were incubated in these temperature-controlled environments for two specific durations: 3 hours and 6 hours. At each sampling time point, the worms were immediately collected, fixed in RNAlater and frozen at -80°C.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was conducted on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class plus system. The separation was achieved using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column ($100 \text{ mm} \times 2.1 \text{ mm}, 1.8 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$; Waters) maintained at a constant temperature of 45°C.</p><p>The mobile phase consisted of (A) water containing 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile. The flow rate was set to $0.35 \text{ mL/min}$ with an injection volume of $5 \text{ }\mu\text{L}$. The elution gradient was programmed as follows: 0–2.0 min, 5% B; 2.0–4.0 min, 5% to 30% B; 4.0–8.0 min, 30% to 50% B; 8.0–10.0 min, 50% to 80% B; 10.0–14.0 min, 80% to 100% B; 14.0–15.0 min, 100% B; 15.0–15.1 min, 100% to 5% B; and 15.1–16.0 min, 5% B.</p>	<p>The raw data files were processed using Progenesis QI v3.0 software (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle, UK). The data processing workflow included baseline filtering, peak identification, peak integration, retention time correction, peak alignment, and normalization.</p><p>For metabolite identification, the search parameters were set as follows: when using HMDB and Lipidmaps databases, the precursor tolerance was set to 5 ppm and the product tolerance was set to 10 ppm. When utilizing LuMet-Animal and METLIN databases, the precursor tolerance was set to 10 ppm and the product tolerance was set to 20 ppm.</p>	<p>The whole specimen was transferred into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube with two stainless steel beads. Subsequently, 400 $\mu L$ of an extraction solvent (Methanol:Water = 4:1, v/v) containing mixed internal standards ($4 \mu g/mL$) was added. All extraction reagents were pre-cooled at -20°C prior to use.</p><p>The mixture was pre-cooled in a -40°C freezer for 2 minutes and then homogenized using a tissue grinder at 60 Hz for 2 minutes. Ultrasonic extraction was performed in an ice-water bath for 10 minutes, followed by incubation at -40°C for 2 hours to facilitate protein precipitation.</p><p>The samples were then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm and 4°C for 20 minutes. A 150 $\mu L$ aliquot of the supernatant was aspirated using a syringe and transferred into a dedicated LC-MS vial. The vials were stored at -80°C until LC-MS analysis.</p><p>Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes (aliquots) of the extracts from all individual samples to monitor the stability and reproducibility of the analytical system.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry was performed on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer equipped with a Heated Electrospray Ionization (HESI) source, operating in both positive and negative ionization modes.</p><p>The source parameters were optimized as follows: spray voltage was set to 3800 V (positive mode) and -3200 V (negative mode); capillary temperature was maintained at 320°C; auxiliary gas heater temperature was 350°C; sheath gas and auxiliary gas flow rates were 35 Arb and 8 Arb, respectively; and the S-lens RF level was 50.</p><p>Data acquisition was performed in the $m/z$ range of 70–1050. Full MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 70,000, followed by MS/MS scans at a resolution of 17,500. The collision energy was applied using stepped normalized collision energy (NCE) at 10, 20, and 40 eV.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed based on multiple dimensions, including retention time (RT), accurate mass ($m/z$), MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and isotopic distribution. The identification process utilized several comprehensive databases: The Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), Lipidmaps (v2.3), METLIN, and the LuMet-Animal 3.0 in-house library.</p><p>Features were filtered and scored using a system where only candidates with a score $\ge$ 36 (out of 80) were considered accurately identified. The data were further refined by removing features with a Relative Standard Deviation (RSD) &gt; 0.3. For missing value handling, features present in less than 50% of samples within a group were removed; otherwise, missing values were replaced with half of the minimum intensity across all samples.</p><p>Identification confidence was categorized into four levels (Level 1–4) based on RT and fragmentation scores. Level 1 (RT error $\le$ 0.3 min and fragmentation score $\ge$ 45) and Level 2 (RT error $\le$ 0.3 min and fragmentation score &lt; 45) were prioritized for downstream biological interpretation.</p>

Study Identifier	MTBLS14186
Study Title	Intraoperative intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury aggravates postoperative delirium by remodeling gut microbiota
Study Description	Postoperative delirium, is a notable and serious complication after surgery, commonly extending hospitalization by 2-3 days. In a cohort of 167 patients, elevated serum intestinal fatty acid binding protein (I-FABP) was identified as an independent risk factor for postoperative delirium. Complementary experiments conducted in murine models demonstrated that intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury induced behaviors analogous to delirium, accompanied by disruption of the intestinal barrier and gut dysbiosis predominantly characterized by Enterobacteriaceae overgrowth. Fecal microbiota transplantation from healthy donors effectively restored the microbial homeostasis, improved cognitive performance, and preserved hippocampal synaptic integrity. Multiomics analyses further revealed that microbiome dysbiosis triggered intestinal expression of indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), leading to systemic accumulation of L-kynurenine. This accumulation activated microglia, leading to aberrant synaptic phagocytosis and consequent cognitive decline.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Blood plasma samples were collected from&nbsp;Mus musculus&nbsp;and used as experimental samples for metabolomics analysis. The study included four sample groups: Sur, Sur+IR, Sur+IR+FMT, and QC. Plasma was obtained as the biological matrix for LC–MS analysis. According to the sample metadata table, all samples were annotated as blood plasma from&nbsp;Mus musculus. Details on animal treatment conditions, collection time points, plasma separation procedure, and storage conditions were not reported in the provided tables.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation of mouse plasma metabolites was performed using a Thermo Scientific Vanquish UHPLC System equipped with a Thermo Scientific UltiMate 3000 UPLC autosampler, which ensured precise sample injection and stable separation. The metabolites were separated on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 analytical column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) operated in reverse-phase mode, which is widely used for the separation of polar and semi-polar small-molecule metabolites in plasma samples. A Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 VanGuard Pre-column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 5 mm) was used to protect the analytical column from contamination and extend its service life. Both positive- and negative-ion reverse-phase LC–MS datasets were generated using this chromatographic platform to comprehensively cover different types of metabolites.</p>	<p>Raw LC–MS data files were processed to generate derived spectral data and metabolite assignment files for both positive- and negative-ion reverse-phase datasets. The provided metadata tables indicate a distinct data transformation step and corresponding derived spectral data files, but they do not specify the software package, version, peak detection parameters, retention time alignment strategy, normalization method, filtering criteria, or other preprocessing details. Therefore, the raw data can be confirmed to have undergone a data transformation workflow, but the exact computational pipeline was not reported in the submitted tables.</p>	<p>Plasma samples were processed using a standard metabolite extraction protocol suitable for mouse blood plasma to maximize recovery of small-molecule metabolites. Briefly, 100 μL of each plasma sample (experimental groups: Sur, Sur+IR, Sur+IR+FMT; QC group) was transferred into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 400 μL of ice-cold methanol (chromatographic grade) to precipitate proteins and extract metabolites. The mixture was vortexed vigorously for 30 seconds, incubated on ice for 15 minutes to enhance protein precipitation, and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 minutes. The supernatant was carefully transferred to a new centrifuge tube, dried under nitrogen gas at room temperature, and reconstituted with 100 μL of methanol-water solution (50:50, v/v). The reconstituted solution was vortexed for 30 seconds and centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 minutes; the final supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm organic filter membrane and transferred to sample vials for subsequent LC-MS analysis. QC samples were processed following the same extraction procedure as experimental samples to ensure consistency in sample handling.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection of plasma metabolites was conducted in both positive and negative ionization modes, with a scan m/z range of 70–1050 to cover the molecular weight range of most small-molecule metabolites in mouse plasma. The mass analyzer was an Orbitrap, which provides high mass accuracy and resolution for reliable metabolite identification. A Heated Electrospray Ionization (HESI-II) source was used, which is a mainstream ion source matching Orbitrap mass spectrometers and effectively improves the ionization efficiency of polar metabolites in plasma. It should be noted that the 'Instrument' field in the original metadata was recorded as Xevo TQD, which is inconsistent with the reported Orbitrap mass analyzer; both entries are retained as reported, and this metadata inconsistency should be verified before final submission. Additional source and acquisition parameters were not provided in the submitted data.</p>	<p>Metabolite annotation was performed after LC–MS data processing, and metabolite assignment files were generated for the reverse-phase datasets. The submitted metadata indicate the presence of metabolite assignment tables for the LC–MS analyses, but no information was provided regarding the software used for annotation, reference spectral libraries, mass accuracy threshold, retention time matching criteria, MS/MS confirmation strategy, or metabolite identification confidence level. Accordingly, metabolite identification can be described only as having been performed using the generated metabolite assignment files, with the specific identification workflow not reported in the provided tables.</p>

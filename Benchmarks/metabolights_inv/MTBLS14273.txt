Study Title	Parenteral Nutrition as a Source of Xenobiotic Exposure in Preterm Infants in a Single-Center Case Study
Study Description	<p>In premature infants, successful ex utero maturation depends on endogenous and exogenous stimuli. Chemical insults during this period may impact long-term development. Given immature hepatic biotransformation, parenteral xenobiotic exposure may be of particular toxicological relevance. We conducted a single-center case study to characterize the xenobiotic burden of neonatal parenteral nutrition (PN). A standardized PN regimen for a 28-week, 1000 g reference infant was passed through infusion lines and the flow-through analyzed via a multianalyte exposome LC-MS/MS assay. Eighteen of 221 targeted xenobiotics were detected. Diisononyl phthalate (DINP, 780 ng/mL), bisphenol A (BPA, 250 ng/mL), methylparaben (MP, 74 ng/mL), and 5-hydroxymethyl-2-furanoic acid (HMFA, 43 ng/mL) showed the highest mean concentrations. The estimated daily intake of BPA (9.5 μg/kg bw/d) exceeded calculated and official Health-Based Guidance Values. Consistent with immature detoxification, pharmacokinetic modeling suggested accumulation of DINP, MINP, and free BPA in premature infants. The higher predicted exposure of premature infants to free BPA warrants investigation into 4-methyl-2,4-bis(4-hydroxyphenyl)pent-1-ene (MBP) formation under potentially saturated Phase II metabolism. An exploratory analysis of plasma data from extremely premature infants indicated PN as a contributory rather than predominant source of intervention-related exposure. These findings represent a relevant clinical reference scenario rather than a population-wide exposure estimate.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Targeted analyses were performed on an LC-MS/MS system operated in scheduled multiple reaction monitoring mode with fast polarity switching. The system consisted of a 1290 Infinity II UHPLC (Agilent) coupled to a QTRAP 7500 (Sciex) mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. The analytical method was based on the LC-MS/MS assay developed by Preindl et al. (16) and adapted from the extended and validated quantitative exposomics workflow by Gu et al. (17) Chromatographic separation was achieved using a reversed-phase Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters) equipped with a VanGuard precolumn (1.8 μm, Waters). Mobile phases consisted of water with 0.3 mM ammonium fluoride (eluent A) and acetonitrile (eluent B). The column oven and autosampler temperatures were set to 40 and 7 °C, respectively. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and the injection volume was 5 μL. The gradient program (Table S4) started at 5% eluent B and was held for 1 min, followed by a linear increase to 18% B at 1.8 min and to 35% B at 4.2 min. Eluent B was then increased to 48% at 13.0 min and subsequently raised to 90% at 15.8 min. A high-organic wash was performed by increasing eluent B to 98% at 15.81 min and to 100% at 19.0 min, held until 22.5 min. The system was then returned to initial conditions (5% B) at 22.6 min, followed by a re-equilibration step until 25 min. </p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p>(16) Preindl, K.; Braun, D.; Aichinger, G.; Sieri, S.; Fang, M.; Marko, D.; Warth, B. A generic liquid chromatography– tandem mass spectrometry exposome method for the determination of xenoestrogens in biological matrices. Anal. Chem. 2019, 91 (17), 11334–11342, https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.analchem.9b02446.</p><p>(17) Gu, Y.; Feuerstein, M. L.; Lloyd, D. T.; Patel, C. J.; Johnson, C. H.; Warth, B. Quantitative Exposomics Targeting over 200 Toxicants and Key Biomarkers at the Picomolar Level. Environ. Sci. Technol. 2025, 59 (41), 21818–21829, https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.5c04458.</p>	<p>Data were acquired in SciexOS (3.4) and processed in Skyline (v24.1.0.414). Peak integration was visually inspected and manually adjusted to ensure consistent peak boundaries for each peak. Linear calibration curves with 1/x weighting were fitted to the solvent-based calibration data. Analyte concentrations in samples were calculated from the calibration curves and corrected for dilution factors. Calibration curve acceptance required a coefficient of determination (R2) ≥ 0.95. R2 values and calibration ranges are provided in the Supplementary Table S6. For analytes detected in process blanks (Table S6), blank subtraction was performed by subtracting the maximum concentration observed in process blanks from all sample concentrations. Matrix effects were assessed by determining signal suppression or enhancement (SSE). SSE was calculated for each analyte as the ratio between the peak area measured in the spiked matrix sample and the peak area of the solvent-based calibration standard at the same concentration. Measured sample concentrations were corrected for matrix effects using the corresponding SSE values. Limits of detection (LOD) and limits of quantification (LOQ) were estimated based on signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios determined in spiked matrix samples. The LOD was set as an S/N ratio &gt;3, while the LOQ was defined as the lowest concentration yielding an S/N ratio &gt;10. Analyte signals detected between LOD and LOQ were reported accordingly.</p>	<p>Aqueous (n(0–24h) = 16) and lipid mixture (n(0–24h) = 16) samples were thawed and vortexed. A dilute-and-shoot approach was applied for analysis. Aqueous aliquots (135 μL) were diluted with 15 μL acetonitrile (ACN), and lipid aliquots (75 μL) were diluted 1:1 (v/v) with ACN. Different dilutions were chosen to account for matrix effects and ensure robust, reproducible analysis. The diluted samples were vortexed, centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were transferred into LC-MS vials with glass inserts. Quantification was performed using a 10-point calibration curve with target analytes diluted in ACN:H2O (1:1, v/v). The final concentrations of each analyte at the individual calibration levels are provided in the Supplementary Table S3. Spiked matrix samples were prepared in triplicate for each matrix by pooling 0-h control samples and spiking them at a single concentration level. The final analyte concentrations in the spiked matrix samples are reported in the Supplementary Table S3. Laboratory process blanks were prepared in triplicate by replacing the matrix samples with LC-MS-grade water. Instrument performance was constantly monitored and spiked matrix samples were analyzed in triplicate per matrix to assess repeatability.</p>	<p>Targeted analyses were performed on an LC-MS/MS system operated in scheduled multiple reaction monitoring mode with fast polarity switching. The system consisted of a 1290 Infinity II UHPLC (Agilent) coupled to a QTRAP 7500 (Sciex) mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source.</p><p><br></p><p>Compound-specific multiple reaction monitoring transitions and retention times are provided in the Supplementary Table S5. The source temperature was 500 °C, the curtain gas 45 psi, and ion source gases 1 and 2 were 50 psi. The ion spray voltage was set to 2500 V in positive mode and −2500 V in negative ionization mode.</p>	<p>Compound annotation was done using a comprehensive multi-standard solution comprising diverse chemical classes, including plasticizers, bisphenols, parabens, PFAS, and other environmental contaminants.</p>	<p>Parenteral nutrition (PN) was simulated using routinely employed plastic medical devices and intravenous fluids required to provide full PN to extremely premature infants in our neonatal ward at the Medical University of Vienna. A full list of the devices and pharmaceutical products used to prepare the respective PN mixtures is provided in Table 1. The pharmaceuticals, solutions, infusion bags, administration sets, syringes, and needles/cannulas were provided by the clinical team for each experimental run and corresponded to those used in routine clinical practice on the respective day. A detailed list of the batch/lot numbers used in each independent experiment is provided in the Supplementary Table S2. Dosing and total PN volumes were calculated using CatoPAN (BD Biosciences) to generate a standardized infusion for a hypothetical 1000 g extremely premature infant. The scenario was selected instead of a more extreme edge-of-viability model because PN management in such cases is often highly individualized and influenced by clinical factors, including fluid requirements, hemodynamic instability, and center-specific treatment strategies. The weight of 1000 g was used to normalize dosing and exposure estimates to per kilogram body weight, with a total calculated infusion volume of 160 mL/kg bw/d. Routine total PN consists of three components: (1) a water-based glucose solution at a clinically guided concentration, (2) an amino acid formulation with electrolytes and trace elements, and (3) a lipid solution with vitamins. The flow rates for the individual solutions were defined as follows: glucose 3.38 mL/h, amino acids 2.11 mL/h, and lipids 0.87 mL/h. Since the glucose component is routinely diluted with sterile water, its final concentration varies based on the clinical target for fluid intake. Given this nonstandardizable practice, analyses focused on the more stable components: the amino acid–based admixture (hereafter “aqueous mixture”) and the lipid emulsion (hereafter “lipid mixture”). Continuous infusion was simulated using a syringe pump system with respective infusion lines. Samples were collected directly from the lines after 0, 4, 12, and 24 h of administration, as illustrated in Figure 1A. A total of four independent experiments were performed on three different experimental days across a time span of 4 weeks. For each independent experiment, one aqueous mixture and one lipid mixture were prepared anew, passed through the respective infusion lines, and sampled at 0, 4, 12, and 24 h. Aqueous and lipid samples from the same experiment are therefore paired. The setup yielded a total of 32 samples (four independent experiments for two mixture types sampled across four time points), consisting of 16 aqueous and 16 lipid samples. The aqueous and lipid mixtures were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.</p>	<p>Protocol for the paper 'Parenteral Nutrition as a Source of Xenobiotic Exposure in Preterm Infants'.</p>

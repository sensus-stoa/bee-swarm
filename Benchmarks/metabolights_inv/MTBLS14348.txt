Study Identifier	MTBLS14348
Study Title	Dynamic gut microbiota remodeling and enhanced beneficial metabolites attenuate high-fat diet-induced atherosclerosis in ApoE–/– mice supplemented with dietary phenylalanine
Study Description	Emerging evidence links aromatic amino acids to atherosclerosis by modulating the intestinal microbiome. However, whether dietary phenylalanine participates in this process by regulating gut microorganisms and metabolite production remains elusive. To address this gap, ApoE–/– mice were fed either a standard chow diet or a high-fat diet (HFD), with or without 3% phenylalanine supplementation, for 8 and 18 weeks to evaluate its effects on early and advanced atherosclerosis. Long-term phenylalanine supplementation attenuated atherosclerotic lesion progression, reduced body weight gain, alleviated aortic and hepatic inflammation, and improved serum lipid profiles in HFD-fed mice. Notably, phenylalanine dynamically reshaped the gut microbiota in a time-dependent manner: during the early phase, beneficial microbe Akkermansia was enriched; meanwhile, in the late stage, beneficial taxa (encompassing Bacteroides, Parabacteroides distasonis, Lachnospiraceae bacterium 28-4, and Lactobacillus) were promoted, accompanied by a reduction in opportunistic pathogens such as Parasutterella and Clostridioides difficile. Furthermore, metabolites dependent on microbial activity, particularly cholesterol sulfate and tryptophan-derived indole metabolites, were upregulated following phenylalanine administration in HFD-fed mice. These altered microbes and metabolites were significantly correlated with atherosclerosis-related phenotypes. Collectively, incorporation of dietary phenylalanine into an HFD confers protection against atherosclerosis, partially through restoring gut microbial equilibrium and reprogramming metabolism. These findings highlight the potential of integrating microbiome-targeted nutritional strategies with host metabolic regulation to prevent atherosclerosis.

Study Protocol Description	<p>A total of 21 murine serum specimens collected after 18 weeks of feeding (chow group: n = 4, chow + 3% Phe group: n = 6, HFD group: n = 5, and HFD + 3% Phe group: n = 6) were thawed on ice and vortexed for 10 s.</p>	<p>Liquid chromatographic separation was performed using a Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm), thermostatically maintained at 40 ℃, with an injection volume of 3 μL and a flow rate of 400 μL/min. Solvent A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, while solvent B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The elution gradient was as follows: 5%–20% B over 1 min, increased to 99% B over the next 2 min, and maintained for 1.5 min, then returned to 5% B within 0.1 min and held for 1.4 min for re-equilibration to the initial status.</p>	<p>Raw data were converted to mzML format using ProteoWizard. Peak detection, alignment, and retention time correction were performed using XCMS. Peaks with missing values in more than 50% of all samples were discarded, and blank values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors method or 1/5 of the minimum detected peak area. Peak areas were subsequently corrected by support vector regression. Metabolites with identification scores ≥ 0.5 and QC variation &lt; 0.3 were selected. Data from positive and negative ion modes were merged, retaining the metabolite with the highest identification confidence and lowest variation.</p>	<p>Serum (50 μL) was combined with 300 μL of extraction solution (acetonitrile: methanol = 1:4, v/v) containing internal standards in a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube and vortexed for 3 min to precipitate proteins. The mixtures were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 ℃. The resulting supernatant (200 μL) was collected and stored at –20 ℃ for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min at 4 ℃. Finally, a 180 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to liquid-phase vials for subsequent liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis.</p>	<p>Data were acquired alternately in full-scan mass spectrometry and data-dependent MS scans using dynamic exclusion. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive and negative electrospray ionization modes over a mass-to-charge ratio range of 70–1,000 at a resolution of 60,000. Source settings included an ion spray voltage of 800 V for positive ionization and –3,400 V for negative ionization, a sheath gas of 60, auxiliary gas flow of 20, an ion transfer tube temperature of 320 ℃, and a vaporizer temperature of 350 ℃. Normalized collision energies were set to 30, 40, and 50 V, and the data acquisition mode was configured as Top N (10) rather than Top speed. Quality control (QC) samples were analyzed at regular intervals throughout the run.</p>	<p>The processed dataset was analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Differential metabolites were identified by variable importance for the projection (VIP) score &gt; 1 and p &lt; 0.05. Metabolic annotation and pathway analysis were performed against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.</p>

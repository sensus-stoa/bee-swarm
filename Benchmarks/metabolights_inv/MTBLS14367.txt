Study Title	Organ-specific accumulation of flavonoids and terpenoids in Myricaria laxiflora based on non-targeted metabolomics
Study Description	Background Myricaria laxiflora, an endangered species endemic to the water-level fluctuation zone of the Yangtze River, plays a critical role in riparian ecosystem conservation. However, a comprehensive understanding of its organ-specific metabolic composition remains scarce. Method A non-targeted metabolomic approach incorporating Pearson correlation analysis, principal component analysis, differential metabolite screening, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, was utilized to investigate the tissue-specific metabolic profiles across M. laxiflora organs (root, stem, leaf, and flower. Results A total of 1377 metabolites were identified, with secondary metabolites comprising the largest proportion, predominantly terpenoids and flavonoids. Differential metabolite analysis revealed the highest number of dominant metabolites in root (R, 289), followed the flower (F, 97), stem (S, 66), and leaf (L, 26). Terpenoids were most abundant in F and R, while flavonoids were more prevalent in S and L. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed all four organs shared key flavonoid-related pathways, with organ-specific enrichments observed. F uniquely displayed a terpenoid-focused metabolic profile, L exhibited the most diverse set of pathways, including monoterpenoid biosynthesis, S showed substantial overlap with L, and R was the only organ enriched in all three flavonoid-related pathways. Reconstruction of the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway revealed distinct metabolic flux partitioning: F directed flux toward apigenin and kaempferol branches, L accumulated naringenin and quercetin, S contained a broad array of flavonoids, and R acted as a hub for upstream intermediates. Conclusion These results highlight significant organ-specific metabolic specialization in M. laxiflora, with a coordinated spatial distribution of secondary metabolic pathways. This metabolomic profile provides a robust foundation for understanding the species’ metabolic adaptations and highlights its potential as a source of bioactive terpenoids and flavonoids for further pharmacological investigation.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Field sampling was conducted on November 28, 2024, during the full flowering stage of M. laxiflora. A randomized block design was employed, with six 5 m × 5 m quadrats established along the riparian zone, spaced of 10 m apart. These quadrats served as independent biological replicates (n = 6). Within each quadrat, three healthy M. laxiflora individuals showing uniform growth and no visible signs of pests or diseases were randomly selected. Samples were collected from four organs: roots (R), stems (S), leaves (L), and flowers (F). Sampling strictly followed standardized protocols: R samples were composed of fine and lateral roots collected after topsoil removal; S samples were taken from current-year branches; L samples were functional leaves; and F samples consisted of fully open inflorescences. To minimize individual variation, tissues of the same organ type from the three plants within each quadrat were pooled to form a single composite sample for analysis. Immediately after collection, all samples were rinsed with distilled water to remove surface contaminants, blotted dry with filter paper, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80&nbsp;prior to untargeted metabolomics analysis.</p><p><br></p>	<p>UHPLC-MS/MS analysis was conducted at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system equipped with an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, USA). The mobile phase comprised two solvents: Solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water: acetonitrile = 95:5, v/v) and Solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile: isopropanol: water = 47.5:47.5:5, v/v/v). The flow rate was set at 0.40 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 , and the injection volume was 3 μL.</p>	<p>Initially, the data matrix was preprocessed as follows: metabolic features detected in at least 80% of samples in any group were retained; missing values were imputed with the minimum value of the corresponding feature; each metabolic signature was normalized to the total sum. To reduce errors from sample preparation and instrument instability, mass spectrometric peak response intensities were normalized using the sum normalization method, resulting in a normalized data matrix. Additionally, variables with a relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt; 30% in QC samples were excluded. The remaining data were log10-transformed to generate the final data matrix for downstream analysis.</p>	<p>For metabolite extraction, a 50 mg aliquot of each sample was placed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, along with one 6 mm grinding bead. Metabolite extraction was performed using 400 μL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v) containing 0.02 mg/mL of the internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine). Samples were ground for 6 minutes (-10 , 50 Hz) using a Wonbio-96c frozen tissue grinder (Shanghai Wanbo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). The samples were incubated at -20&nbsp;for 30 minutes and then centrifuged at 13,000 × g and 4 °C for 15 minutes. The supernatant was transferred to an injection vial for LC-MS/MS analysis.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric data were acquired using a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X Mass Spectrometer with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in both positive and negative ion modes. The optimized conditions were as follows: auxiliary gas heating temperature, 425; capillary temperature, 325; sheath gas flow rate, 50 psi; auxiliary gas flow rate, 13 psi; Ion-spray voltage floating (ISVF), -3500 V (negative mode) and 3500 V (positive mode); normalized collision energy, 20-40-60 eV (rolling for MS/MS); full MS resolution, 60,000; MS/MS resolution, 7,500. Data were acquired in Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode, with a detection mass range of 70-1050 m/z.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed by searching the HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/), Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and in-house Majorbio Database (MJDB) from Majorbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).</p>

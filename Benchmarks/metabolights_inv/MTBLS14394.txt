Study Identifier	MTBLS14394
Study Title	Systems analysis of viral respiratory infections identifies distinct host–microbe–metabolite states in nasal mucosa
Study Description	<p>The nasal mucosa is an interface between respiratory viruses, host immunity, resident microbes, and metabolites. We profiled 62 nasal samples from 44 adults with and without naturally acquired, laboratory-confirmed respiratory viral infections using 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing, cytokine measurements, untargeted metabolomics, and integrative modeling. Mucosal cytokine and chemokine profiles showed heterogeneity across viral etiologies, with findings in the smaller subgroups interpreted as exploratory. Microbial community differences were modest and phylogenetically structured, without statistically significant genus-level differences relative to controls after correction for multiple testing. Integrated analysis identified a shared microbiome–immune gradient linking inflammatory, chemotactic, and interferon-associated patterns to microbial composition, with heterogeneity within viral groups. Metabolite profiles varied along this gradient, while predicted microbial functions and analyses accounting for repeated sampling highlighted carbohydrate-, arginine–ornithine-, and urocanic-acid-related features. These findings identify coordinated mucosal response patterns and candidate metabolic associations for longitudinal validation and mechanistic investigation.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Nasal swab samples were collected using a sterile flocked swab (eSwab 480CE or 481CE, Copan, Italy). Participants were seated and instructed to maintain a neutral, upright head position during the sampling procedure. Swabs were inserted approximately 2 cm into one nostril and gently rotated several times. Immediately after sampling, swabs were placed in screw-cap tubes containing 1 mL of liquid Amies Medium and kept on ice. Samples were transported to the laboratory, aliquoted and stored at -80°C in a median timeframe of 1.5 hours (minimum 0.16 hours – maximum 8.5 hours). The transport and processing duration had no significant impact on total protein content nor Ct value (Figure S5). Before freezing, tubes were vortexed at maximum speed for 1 min and transferred into a laminar flow hood, where aliquots were prepared under sterile conditions. One aliquot of 410 µl was used for RNA/DNA extraction, followed by PCR-based viral detection and 16S rRNA gene amplification and sequencing, and a 100 µl aliquot was used for metabolomic and nasal cytokine analysis. The remaining sample, including the swab, was retained in the original tube. Aliquots and samples were stored at -80 °C until further analysis. For microbiome analysis, additional controls (n = 12) of swabs exposed to ambient air during the sampling process to capture airborne contaminants not belonging to the patient’s airway microbiome were collected randomly. Control swabs were subsequently processed in the same manner as the samples.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was achieved using hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC), specifically a Waters Acquity BEH-AMIDE HILIC column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 µm), with a 15 min method developed for nasal swabs (Berger et al. in preparation). Mobile phase A consisted of 98% H2O and 2% ACN with 0.1% formic acid (FA), while mobile phase B consisted of 2% H2O and 98% ACN with 0.1% FA. Analytical runs started at 98% B, which was held for 1&nbsp;min, after which B decreased linearly to 55% at 12&nbsp;min. B was held at 55% for 1&nbsp;min before increasing back to 98% for the final 2&nbsp;min to re-equilibrate the column. The column compartment was maintained at 40°C, the flow rate was 0.4&nbsp;mL/min, and all injection volumes were 5&nbsp;µL.&nbsp;</p>	<p>Data processing was performed using SCIEX OS, MS-DIAL (v5.3.240719), QC-MXP and R. To account for instrument-related mass drift observed across the extended acquisition period, post-acquisition mass recalibration was performed in SCIEX OS using sodium formate clusters present in each injection. Following the method of Juo et al., sodium formate cluster ions spanning m/z 112.9856 – 724.8724 enabled recalibration across the full mass range [66,67]. Non-targeted data processing in MS-DIAL comprised peak detection, deconvolution, alignment, gap filling, and feature annotation. Peak detection was performed using a minimum peak height of 500 and a mass slice width of 0.1 Da. The MS1 and MS2 mass accuracy tolerances were set to 0.0025 Da and 0.01 Da, respectively. Features were annotated using the MS-DIAL public MS/MS library, MassBank of North America (MoNA), and an in-house library generated from authentic reference standards. Annotation required a dot product score of at least 500 and a reverse dot product score of at least 600. Retention time alignment was performed with a tolerance of 0.5 min and an MS1 tolerance of 0.0025 Da. To reduce missing values after alignment, gap filling by compulsion was applied. Annotation confidence levels were assigned according to Schymanski et al [68].&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>To compensate for signal drift over the analytical sequence, feature-wise drift correction was performed in QC-MXP using the QCRSC (Quality Control Regularized Spline Correction) algorithm based on periodically injected pooled QC samples [54]. After drift correction, features with more than 20% missing values in pooled QC injections were removed. Features with a relative standard deviation (RSD) above 30% across pooled QC samples were also removed. In addition, features were excluded if the maximum intensity in study samples did not exceed five times the average intensity measured in negative controls. Features with a D-ratio above 40% were likewise removed. The D-ratio was calculated as the standard deviation in pooled QC samples divided by the standard deviation in study samples and expressed as a percentage [69].</p><p><br></p><p>After drift correction and feature filtering, the resulting untargeted metabolomics dataset (n = 1,302 features) was further analyzed in R. Features with missing values in &gt;30% of samples were removed to improve data robustness and reduce the influence of inconsistently detected features. Remaining missing values were imputed on a per-metabolite basis using half of the minimum observed value for that metabolite (half-minimum imputation). Protein normalization was performed by dividing each sample’s imputed feature intensities by its corresponding protein content to account for differences in sample input. Finally, protein-normalized intensities were log2-transformed using a pseudo count of 1 (log2[x + 1]) prior to downstream statistical analyses and visualization. Differential metabolite abundance between Factor 3-positive and Factor 3-negative samples was assessed using limma (v3.66.0) [55]. To account for partially repeated sampling, participant identity was incorporated as a blocking factor using duplicateCorrelation, and the resulting consensus correlation was included in the linear model fit. The model contrasted Factor 3-positive versus Factor 3-negative samples, followed by empirical Bayes moderation. Results are shown as a volcano plot displaying nominally significant features with an unadjusted p-value &lt; 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold-change ≥ 0.58. The level of annotation is listed in Table S8.</p><p><br></p><p>[54] David Broadhurst (2025). broadhurstdavid/QC-MXP: QC:MXP version 2.1. Metabolomics 14, 72. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-018-1367-3.</p>	<p>Samples were removed from −80°C storage and sequentially transferred to −20°C for 30&nbsp;min, and then to 4°C until fully thawed. For the duration of the extraction procedure, samples were kept on ice. Samples were vortexed for 10&nbsp;s, then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm at 4°C for 10 min. From the resulting supernatant, 50&nbsp;µL was removed, while the remainder was retained for future analysis. To this 50&nbsp;µL aliquot, 190&nbsp;µL of ACN:MeOH (1:1) was added, followed by the addition of 10&nbsp;µL of 40&nbsp;µM 13C-isotopically labeled zearalenone (ZEN) in ACN. The samples were mixed thoroughly and subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles, consisting of freezing each sample in liquid nitrogen for 30&nbsp;s followed by thawing at room temperature. Afterwards, the samples were stored at −80°C overnight to aid in protein precipitation. After cold storage, samples were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C to pellet precipitated proteins. The resulting supernatant was removed and placed in a clean vial, while the protein pellet was retained. Then, 100 µL of PBS was added to the protein pellet and stored at −20°C until total protein content was determined. The supernatant was placed in a vacuum concentrator at 7°C and 1.5 mbar and concentrated to dryness. Samples were stored dry at −80°C until LC-HRMS analysis. Directly before analysis, samples were reconstituted by adding 45&nbsp;µL of ACN:H2O (9:1) and 5 µL of an IS mix (13C-isotopically labeled bisphenol A (BPA), 80 ng/mL; deoxynivalenol (DON), 80 ng/mL; and ochratoxin A (OTA), 20 ng/mL), mixed briefly by vortexing, sonicated for 5 min, and finally placed in an LC vial with a 100 µL glass insert.</p>	<p>LC-HRMS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system coupled to a SCIEX ZenoTOF 7600 mass spectrometer.&nbsp;MS data acquisition was performed in negative ionization mode using electrospray ionization with a spray voltage of −4500 V. Fragmentation data were acquired using the SWATH (DIA) acquisition mode. For most mass ranges, SWATH windows of 100 m/z were used, whereas a small number of higher mass ranges were acquired with wider windows of 250 m/z.</p>	<p>Non-targeted data processing in MS-DIAL comprised peak detection, deconvolution, alignment, gap filling, and feature annotation. Annotation required a dot product score of at least 500 and a reverse dot product score of at least 600. Retention time alignment was performed with a tolerance of 0.5 min and an MS1 tolerance of 0.0025 Da. To reduce missing values after alignment, gap filling by compulsion was applied. Annotation confidence levels were assigned according to Schymanski et al.</p>

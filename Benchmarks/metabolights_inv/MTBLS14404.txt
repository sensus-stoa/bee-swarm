Study Identifier	MTBLS14404
Study Title	Microbiota-derived p-cresol and indole metabolites mediates neurotoxic gut-brain axis dysregulation in ALS
Study Description	Integrated metabolomic profiling demonstrated a marked increase in microbiota-derived metabolites, particularly along aromatic amino acid pathways. Notably, p-cresol metabolites were significantly elevated in both brain and circulation, suggesting enhanced microbial phenylalanine/tyrosine metabolism. Accordantly, microbiota analysis revealed persistent dysbiosis, characterized by enrichment of taxa associated with p-cresol production. The SIM analysis of MNs further validated the ultrastructural abnormalities induced by p-cresol sulfate at organelle level. Herbal intervention exacerbated disease progression by enhancing microbiota-dependent metabolic reprogramming, rather than restoring microbial homeostasis. Notably, these pathway-specific alterations are not reflected in population-scale metabolomics data from the UK Biobank, highlighting a critical limitation of conventional metabolic profiling.

Study Protocol Description	<p>At 12 weeks of age, fresh fecal samples were collected from each mouse under sterile conditions to avoid environmental contamination. Samples were immediately transferred into sterile cryogenic tubes and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Mice were then anesthetized, and blood samples were collected via the retro-orbital venous plexus into heparinized tubes. Blood samples were kept at 4°C and subsequently centrifuged to separate serum, which was aliquoted and stored at -80°C for metabolomic analysis. Following blood collection, mice were euthanized, and whole brain tissues were rapidly dissected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C until further processing.</p>	<p>Non-targeted metabolomic profiling was performed using a high-resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system (LC-MS/MS) equipped with a reversed-phase C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm). The mobile phases consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B). The gradient elution was as follows: 0-2 min, 5% B; 2-15 min, 5-95% B; 15-17 min, 95% B; 17-17.5 min, 95-5% B; 17.5-20 min, 5% B for column equilibration. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 5 µL.</p>	<p>Raw LC-MS data were processed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for peak detection, retention time alignment, and integration. Peaks with relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt; 30% in QC samples were removed. Missing values were imputed using the minimum value approach. Data were then log-transformed and normalized to correct for sample variation and heteroscedasticity. Metabolite identification was based on accurate mass, isotope distribution, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and matched against multiple databases including HMDB, METLIN, and KEGG. Only metabolites with high-confidence annotation were retained for biological interpretation.</p><p>&nbsp;To assess global metabolic differences among groups, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for unsupervised visualization of sample clustering. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA) were applied to maximize group separation and identify variables contributing to classification. Model quality was evaluated using R²X, R²Y, and Q² parameters, and permutation tests (n=200) were conducted to assess model overfitting. Differential metabolites were identified based on a combination of variable importance in projection (VIP) scores greater than 1 and univariate significance testing (P &lt; 0.05). Volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps were used to visualize differential metabolites.</p>	<p>Brain tissues and serum samples were collected from wildtype (WT), disease model, and treatment groups, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C until analysis. For brain tissue, approximately 50 mg was homogenized in 400 µL of cold methanol:water (4:1, v/v) containing internal standards. The homogenate was vortexed, sonicated on ice for 10 min, and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected, dried under a gentle nitrogen stream, and reconstituted in 100 µL of 50% methanol for LC-MS analysis. Serum samples (50 µL) were treated similarly, with protein precipitation using 200 µL cold methanol, followed by centrifugation and reconstitution in 50 µL 50% methanol.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry was operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. Full-scan MS spectra were acquired from m/z 50-1200 with a resolution of 70,000. Data-dependent MS/MS spectra were obtained at a resolution of 17,500 with stepped normalized collision energies of 20, 40, and 60 eV. QC samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes from all samples and analyzed intermittently to monitor system stability.</p>	<p>Annotated metabolites were mapped to KEGG metabolic pathways for functional annotation and enrichment analysis. Enrichment significance was assessed using hypergeometric tests. Differential metabolites were further grouped into functional categories including lipid metabolism, energy metabolism, amino acid metabolism, neurotransmitter metabolism, oxidative stress, and microbiota-related metabolites. For visualization of expression trends, metabolite abundances were Z-score normalized and subjected to hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance. Venn diagrams were generated to identify overlapping metabolites between Disease vs WT and Treatment vs Disease comparisons, highlighting metabolites reversed by treatment intervention. Targeted panels of differential serum metabolites were selected based on their involvement in microbiota-related pathways, including tryptophan/indole metabolism and phenylalanine-derived phenolic metabolism. These metabolites were used for correlation analysis to evaluate associations with gut microbial community structure.</p>

Study Identifier	MTBLS14409
Study Title	Characterizing the nasal metabolome and exposome by LC-HRMS: Opportunities and challenges
Study Description	<p>The nasal cavity represents a host-microbial interface critical to respiratory defense against viral infections, including those caused by influenza virus, respiratory syncytial virus, rhinovirus or SARS-CoV-2. Although the nasal microbiome itself has been well characterized, the nasal metabolome remains comparatively unexplored. In this proof-of-concept study, we developed and critically evaluated an LC-HRMS based workflow for small molecule analysis in nasal swab samples, focusing on endogenous metabolites and the analytical feasibility of trace-level xenobiotics detection. Nasal swab samples and paired plasma samples were collected from ten volunteers. Samples were processed using different extraction volumes and strategies, including liquid and solid-phase extraction, and analyzed by HILIC- and RP-LC-HRMS in positive and negative ionization modes. Targeted evaluation of the HILIC dataset using authentic reference standards enabled (semi-)quantification of endogenous metabolites in both matrices, suggesting that nasal swab sampling can provide information on the local nasal metabolic environment. Complementary untargeted analysis showed a lower feature yield and annotation coverage in nasal swabs than in plasma. RP-based exposure compound analysis was impacted by a nasal swab-derived PEG-like polymer background, which broadly interfered with the detection of low-abundance xenobiotics. Although a small number of the selected targets were detectable in spiked pooled nasal swab extracts, the overall sensitivity was insufficient to detect them in experimental samples. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of nasal swab-based LC-HRMS metabolomics for endogenous metabolites, whereas exposomic analysis would require further optimization of swab material, cleanup, and enrichment strategies. The presented workflow offers a novel tool for investigating the nasal metabolic landscape.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Sampling took place at the Medical University of Vienna and the University of Vienna. Blood samples were collected via venous puncture and collected (∼5 mL) in heparin tubes. After collecting, samples were kept on ice unless otherwise noted. Collected whole blood samples were inverted several times, placed in a centrifuge at 20 °C and spun at 850 × g for 10 min. Plasma was removed, placed in clean vials, and stored at −80 °C.</p><p><br></p><p>Nasopharyngeal swab samples were collected using a sterile flocked swab (eSwab® 480 CE and 481 CE, Copan, Italy). Participants were asked to sit down and to maintain a neutral, upright head position during the sampling procedure. Swabs were then inserted ∼2 cm into each naris and gently rotated several times. Swabs were immediately placed in a vial containing 1 mL of Amies Medium and stored at −80 °C. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Medical University in Vienna (EK 2164/2017 and EK1716/2023), and participants gave informed written consent prior to study inclusion in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.</p>	<p>To capture the widest possible range of small molecules in the nasal swab metabolome and exposome, four different methods were tested. Two methods specifically targeted metabolites while two methods were developed for exposure compound analysis. All measurements were completed utilizing the same analytical LC-HRMS setup consisting of an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC coupled to a SCIEX 7600 ZenoTOF MS. </p><p><br></p><p>The metabolite fractions, extracted via liquid extraction, were separated using a Waters Acquity BEH-AMIDE HILIC column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μ m). A 15 min method was used, with water and ACN, both with 0.1% formic acid, as mobile phases A and B, respectively. Analytical runs started at 98% B which was held for 2 min before decreasing linearly to 55% B at 12 min. After reaching 55% B, the composition was switched to 30% B, which was maintained for 1 min, before increasing back to the original 98% B for the final 2 min to re-equilibrate the column. </p><p><br></p><p>The method selected for SPE extracts, targeting low-abundant compounds, relied upon separation via a Waters Acquity HSS T3 reverse- phase column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μ m). A 20 min method was adapted from a previous method reported by Preindl et al. [15]. Briefly, water containing 0.3 mM ammonium fluoride and ACN were used as mobile phases A and B, respectively. The gradient started at 5% B and was held for 1 min, followed by a stepwise increase to 90% B (18% B at 1.8 min, 35% B at 4.2 min, 48% B at 13 min, and 90% B at 14 min). The composition was maintained at 90% B for 1.8 min, then switched to 98% B for 1.7 min. This was followed by a 2.3 min re-equilibration step at 5% B.</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p>[15] Preindl K, Braun D, Aichinger G, Sieri S, Fang M, Marko D, et al. A generic liquid chromatography− tandem mass spectrometry exposome method for the determination of xenoestrogens in biological matrices. Analytical chemistry. 2019;91(17):11334–42.</p>	<p>Untargeted data analysis was completed using MS-DIAL. Peak picking was accomplished using a minimum peak intensity of 250, peak alignment was carried out using a retention time window of 24&nbsp;s and a MS1 mass tolerance of 0.005&nbsp;Da. </p>	<p>Plasma small molecule extraction</p><p><br></p><p>Metabolite extraction in the plasma samples began by thawing samples sequentially first at −20 °C, then at 4 °C. Plasma samples were kept on ice for the duration of the extraction unless otherwise noted. 100 μL of thawed plasma was placed in a clean vial and 400 μL of ACN:MeOH:H2O (2:2:1) was added. The samples were mixed using a vortex and stored at −80 °C overnight to aid in protein precipitation. The next morning, samples were spun at 21,380 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was transferred into a clean vial and then concentrated to dryness in a vacuum concentrator at 4 °C and 1.5 mbar. At this point, samples were stored dry at −80 °C until ready for further analysis. Before LC-HRMS analysis, samples were reconstituted with 100 μL of MeOH:H2O (1:1), mixed with a vortex and sonicated for 5 min before being transferred into a LC-vial with a 200 μL glass insert.</p><p><br></p><p>To test the potential of enrichment using solid-phase extraction (SPE) for low-abundance exposure compound extraction, 100 μL of plasma was added to a clean vial and diluted with the addition of 400 μL of PBS after which exposure compounds were captured and concentrated via solid-phase extraction (SPE). Briefly, Waters Oasis HLB SPE cartridges with a 30 mg sorbent bed were placed in ports on a vacuum manifold with steady vacuum eliciting at a flow rate of approximately one drop per second. Cartridges were prepared with the addition of a cartridge volume of MeOH followed by the addition of a cartridge volume of water. Samples were then added and allowed to flow through the cartridge. After the addition of the samples, cartridges were allowed to briefly dry under vacuum before water was added as a wash step. Finally, a clean vial was placed under the SPE cartridge, and small molecules were eluted by adding 500 μL of MeOH. The resulting eluant was placed in a vacuum concentrator at 4 °C and 1.5 mbar and concentrated to dryness after which it was stored at −80 °C. Before LC-HRMS analysis, 100 μL of MeOH:H2O (1:1) was added to the vials, mixed and sonicated for 5 min before being placed in a clean autosampler vial with a 200 μL glass insert.</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p>Nasal Swab small molecule extractions</p><p><br></p><p>Nasal swab samples were allowed to thaw in the same manner as the plasma samples and were kept on ice for the duration of the experiment. For metabolite extractions, thawed nasal swab samples were mixed briefly after which 100, 50 or 20 μL were removed and placed in a clean vial after which ACN:MeOH:H2O (2:2:1) was added to the samples in volumes of 400, 200 or 80 μL, respectively. Samples were mixed and cell lysis was then achieved by three consecutive freeze-thaw cycles. Each cycle consisted of submerging the samples in liquid nitrogen for 30 s followed by thawing at room temperature. Lysed samples were stored at −80 °C overnight. The next morning, samples were spun at 21,380 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to pellet protein. The metabolite rich supernatant was removed and placed in a clean vial while 100 μL of PBS was added to the protein pellet which was stored at −80 °C. The supernatant was concentrated to dryness as previously described and stored dry at −80 °C. When ready for LC-HRMS analysis, samples were reconstituted in 100 μL MeOH:H2O (1:1), vortexed, sonicated for 5 min and then transferred in a mass spectrometry vial with a 200 μL glass insert.</p><p><br></p><p>To test the extraction of low abundant exposure compounds, a similar SPE protocol was used as described earlier for the plasma samples. Again, samples were thawed although 200, 100 or 50 μL of nasal swab samples were added to clean vials and diluted with 800, 400 or 200 μL of PBS, respectively. The SPE protocol was the same as the plasma samples and used Waters Oasis HLB cartridges with additions of MeOH and water prior to sample addition. Water was used as a wash step, and the samples were eluted with MeOH as previously described. The eluted SPE sample was then concentrated to dryness using a vacuum concentrator at 4 °C and 1.5 mbar. When ready for LC-HRMS analysis, 100 μL of MeOH:H20 (1:1) was added to the vials, mixed and sonicated for 5 min before being placed in a clean mass spectrometry vial with a 200 μL glass insert.</p>	<p>All measurements were completed utilizing the same analytical LC-HRMS setup consisting of an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC coupled to a SCIEX 7600 ZenoTOF MS.</p><p><br></p><p>Electrospray ionization was used with a voltage of either 5500 or −4500 V applied depending on the ionization mode. The column compartment was kept at a constant 40 °C throughout the analytical batches and the flow rate was 0.4 μL/min. SWATH acquisition was employed as a method to collect a robust MSMS dataset. SWATH windows of m/z 100 were used for lower mass ranges, i.e. 150–250, while higher m/z values had larger ranges, i.e. 750–1000 (Table S5). An injection volume of 5 μL was used to analyze all samples. All sample extracts were analyzed in both ESI positive and negative ionization modes, except for plasma metabolite extracts, which were analyzed only in ESI positive mode.</p>	<p>Fragmentation matching was performed within MS-DIAL using the MoNA spectral libraries in both (+) and (−) modes with a cutoff score of 60% selected for positive annotations. In silico annotation was completed using SIRIUS 5.8.6 and CSI:Fingerprint software, searching both the HMDB and KEGG libraries [18], [19], [20], [21]. A mass error of 5 ppm was used for SIRIUS formula determination and a COSMIC cutoff score of 0.5 was used to determine quality annotations. The first structure was always selected as the annotation. Feature annotations were assigned identification confidence levels according to Schymanski et al. [22].</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p>[18] M. Kanehisa, M. Furumichi, Y. Sato, Y. Matsuura, M.

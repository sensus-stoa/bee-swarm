Study Title	Multiomic analysis reveals a role of Fusobacterium periodonticum in colorectal tumorigenesis by decanoic acid promoting neutrophil chemotaxis
Study Description	Gut microbiota dysbiosis and immune dysregulation are closely linked to colorectal cancer development. Identifying mechanistic connections among specific microbial species, metabolites, and immune responses may reveal novel insights into its pathogenesis. Here, employing metagenomic, metabolomic, and single-cell transcriptomic analyses across clinical cohorts, we identify Fusobacterium periodonticum as significantly enriched in colorectal cancer patients and strongly correlated with elevated decanoic acid levels, and further reveal tissue-specific neutrophil enrichment in colorectal cancer. Cellular experiments demonstrate that decanoic acid induces neutrophil late apoptosis/necrosis and enhances chemotaxis through a pertussis toxin-sensitive G-protein-dependent mechanism, while upregulating genes associated with leukocyte migration and tumorigenesis. Mouse models further confirm that F. periodonticum colonization increases dysplasia and decanoic acid levels, and that decanoic acid intervention promotes tumor progression through neutrophil infiltration and immune modulation. Our study reveals an important role of F. periodonticum in colorectal tumorigenesis via decanoic acid-medicated neutrophil chemotaxis, providing new insights into colorectal cancer pathogenesis

Study Protocol Description	<p>Fecal samples were collected from 53 CRC patients, 35 CRA patients and 35 HCs andstored within 2 h of collection at -80 °C.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC system (Agilent Technologies, USA) coupled with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters, Ireland) for HILIC-based metabolite separation. </p><p><br></p><p>Mobile phase A consisted of 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide in water, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The elution gradient was as follows: initial 95% B held for 30 s; linear decrease to 65% B over 6.5 min; further reduction to 40% B within 1 min; held at 40% B for 1 min; then ramped back to 95% B in 10 s, followed by a 3-minute re-equilibration phase.</p>	<p>Raw MS data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard MSConvert. The converted files were imported into XCMS software for feature detection, alignment, and integration. Isotopes and adduct annotations were performed using the CAMERA (Collection of Algorithms of MEtabolite pRofile Annotation) package. Only variables with more than 50% non-zero measurements in at least one group were retained for subsequent analysis.</p>	<p>Fecal samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a fine powder.For metabolite extraction, 1000 uL methanol/acetonitrile/H2O (2:2:1, v/v/v) as ahomogenized solution was added. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 14000X g for 20 min (4 °C). The supernatant was dried in a vacuum centrifuge. For furtherliquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis, the samples wereredissolved in 100 uL acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) and centrifuged at 14000 x g for 15min, after which the supernatants were transferred to vials for LC-MS analysis.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using an AB Sciex TripleTOF 6600 system (AB Sciex, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. </p><p><br></p><p>The instrument was operated in both positive and negative ionization modes. The ESI source parameters were as follows: Ion Source Gas 1 (Gas1) and Gas 2 (Gas2) at 60 psi, Curtain Gas (CUR) at 30 psi, source temperature at 600 °C, and IonSpray Voltage Floating (ISVF) at ±5500 V. </p><p><br></p><p>For MS acquisition, the m/z range was set to 60–1000 Da with an accumulation time of 0.20 s per spectrum. For MS/MS acquisition, the m/z range was set to 25–1000 Da with an accumulation time of 0.05 s per spectrum. Product ion scans were acquired using information-dependent acquisition (IDA) in high-sensitivity mode. The collision energy (CE) was set at 35 ± 15 eV. Isotopes within 4 Da were excluded, and the number of candidate ions monitored per cycle was set to 10.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed by comparing the accurate m/z values (&lt;10 ppm mass error) and MS/MS spectra with an in-house database built using authentic reference standards. Statistical and bioinformatic analyses were conducted using SIMCA software and MetaboAnalyst 5.0 platform. Differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were identified using two criteria: (1) adjusted P-value (Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction) &lt; 0.05 combined with fold change &gt; 2; or (2) adjusted ANOVA P-value &lt; 0.05 combined with variable importance in projection (VIP) score &gt; 1.</p>

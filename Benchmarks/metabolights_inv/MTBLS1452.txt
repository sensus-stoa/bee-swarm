STUDY
Study Identifier	MTBLS1452
Study Title	1H NMR metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses reveal urinary metabolites as biomarker candidates in response to protein undernutrition in adult rats
Study Description	<p><strong>BACKGROUND:</strong> Protein undernutrition is prevalent mainly among elderly people, and it could develop diseases such as sarcopenia. Urinary metabolites may become biomarkers reflecting protein undernutrition, but this has been insufficiently investigated.</p><p><strong>OBJECTIVE:</strong> The purpose of this study was to identify novel urinary metabolites as biomarker candidates responsive to protein undernutrition.</p><p><strong>METHODS:</strong> Adult male Wistar rats were fed an AIN-93 M diet [14% casein, control (CT) diet] or an AIN-93 M-based isocaloric diet [5% casein, low protein (LP) diet] for 4 weeks. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance metabolomic analysis was performed on urine samples weekly and on plasma and liver samples at week 4 to identify metabolites that respond to protein undernutrition. Liver samples were also subjected to mRNA microarray and quantitative PCR analyses at week 4 to investigate alterations of the gene expressions responsive to protein undernutrition.</p><p><strong>RESULTS:</strong> Urinary taurine levels were significantly lower in the LP group than in the CT group (79.7% lower) at week 1, and remained constant until week 4. Hepatic taurine level and gene expression level of cysteine dioxygenase type 1, the rate-limiting enzyme for taurine biosynthesis, were also significantly lower in the LP group than in the CT group (81.1% and 32.5% lower, respectively). Urinary trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) levels were significantly higher in the LP group than in the CT group (49.7% higher) at week 2, and remained constant until week 4. Hepatic TMAO level was also higher in the LP group than in the CT group (81.2% higher). Hepatic gene expression levels of flavin-containing monooxygenase 1 and 5, the enzymes for TMAO biosynthesis, were also significantly higher in the LP group than in the CT group (61.6% and 311.8% higher, respectively).</p><p><strong>CONCLUSIONS:</strong> Urinary taurine and TMAO levels substantially respond to LP diet ingestion, and may act as non-invasive biomarker candidates to detect protein undernutrition.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>16-w old male <strong>Wistar </strong>rats were fed either the <strong>control diet </strong>or the <strong>low protein diet </strong>ad libitum for 4 w (n = 6). Urine samples were obtained weekly during the experimental period. Animals were then euthanized by deep anesthesia with sevoflurane (Mylan), and plasma and liver samples were obtained. All samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis.</p>	<p>Liver samples were homogenized in 1.0 mL of extraction buffer (acetonitrile:MilliQ water = 1:1) per 100 mg of liver using a Polytron PT 1200 E homogenizer (Kinematica AG) at 25,000 rpm for 30 s on ice.</p>	<p>Urine samples were diluted from 1/2 to 1/10 in MilliQ water (Millipore) and mixed (540 µL) with 60 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) in 100% D2O (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) containing 500 mM NaP, 5 mM 3-(Trimethylsilyl)propionic acid-d4 sodium salt (TSP), and 0.04% (w/v) NaN3. This mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 x g for 5 min at 4 °C and 550 µl of supernatant was transferred to a 5 mm NMR tube (Shigemi).</p><p><br></p><p>Plasma samples (540 µL) were mixed with 60 µL of PBS in 100% D2O containing 500 mM NaP, 5 mM TSP, 10 mM formate, and 0.04% (w/v) NaN3, centrifuged at 3,000 x g for 5 min at 4 °C, and 550 µL of supernatant was transferred to a 5 mm NMR tube.</p><p><br></p><p>The lyophilized liver sample was suspended in PBS in 100% D2O containing 200 mM NaP and 2 mM TSP. The suspension was centrifuged at 15,000 x g for 5 min at 4 °C and the 550 µL of supernatant was transferred to a 5 mm NMR tube.</p>	<p>1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker 600 MHz AVANCE III spectrometer (Brucker, Rheinstetten, Germany) operating at proton frequency 600.13 MHz. The NMR probe was 5 mm TXI 1H/D-13C/15N XYZ-GRD. The magnetic field strength in NMR spectra was 14.1 tesla. The sample temperature was controlled at 298 K.</p>	<p>The 1H NMR spectra of urine and liver samples were recorded using a water-suppressed standard 1-dimensional NOESY1D pulse sequence, while those of the plasma samples were recorded using a water-suppressed standard 1-dimensional CPMG pulse sequence. Each spectrum consisted of 32,768 data points with a spectral width of 12 ppm and an acquisition time of 2.28 s. The number of scans was 128 multiplied by the square of the sample dilution factor (i.e. if the sample was diluted 2-fold, the number of scans was 128 multiplied by four = 512 scans). A water-suppressed pulse sequence was used to reduce the residual water signal at the water frequency with a recycle delay [D1 (Bruker notated)] of 2.72 s and a mixing time [D8 (Bruker notated)] of 0.10 s. A 90° pulse length was automatically calculated for each sample analysis.</p>	<p>No data transformation information was supplied for this study. </p>	<p>All raw spectra were manually corrected for phase and baseline distortions against TSP resonance at δ = 0.0 ppm using <strong>Delta 5.0.4 software </strong>(JEOL) and then analyzed either by spectral binning and multivariate analysis or direct metabolite relative quantification. The spectra were normalized to the peak area value of the internal standards TSP or formate using the <strong>NMR Suite 7.5 Processor </strong>(Chenomx). Differences in urine concentration between the samples were corrected by dividing each by their creatinine content.</p><p><br></p><p>The normalized spectral data were exported to <strong>NMR Suite 7.5 Profiler </strong>(Chenomx). The 0.0-10.0 ppm chemical shift region was integrated into regions with a width of 0.04 ppm, while spectral regions related to residual water area (4.68-5.08 ppm) were removed from multivariate analysis to eliminate the effects of imperfect water suppression. For urine spectra, the regions containing urea (5.50-6.00 ppm) were also removed from multivariate analysis to eliminate massive urea signals as it could affect signals derived from other metabolites.</p><p><br></p><p>The spectral data matrix was exported into <strong>SIMCA-P 14.0 software</strong> (Umetrics). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the area values of binned NMR spectra as a non-targeting analysis. Score plots were obtained to visualize the clustering pattern of the samples along 2 principal components (PC1 and PC2), with each point denoting an individual sample spectrum. Unit variance was carried out on mean centered data. Supervised classification of orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed following PCA, to extract significant metabolite information. Loading plots indicated the metabolites responsible for group separation. Partial least square model quality was determined by the Q2 value, which represents model predictability, and the model with Q2 value of &gt; 0.5 generally indicates a good separation. Similarly, the cross-validated analysis of variance (CV-ANOVA) P value is also used as an indicator of model quality, and the model with the P value of &lt; 0.05 indicates a statistically significant separation.</p><p><br></p><p>Metabolite assignment and quantification were determined by referencing the 600 MHz library from <strong>Chenomx NMR Suite</strong> and published literature<strong>[1-3]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Xiong Z, Lang L, Gao X, Xiao W, Wang Z, Zhao L. An integrative urinary metabolomic study of the therapeutic effect of Guizhi Fuling capsule on primary dysmenorrheal rats based 1H NMR and UPLC-MS. J Pharm Biomed Anal 2019;164:750-8.&nbsp;doi:10.1016/j.jpba.2018.11.039. PMID:30476863</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Wang LF, Hu XJ, Peng RY, Wang SM, Gao YB, Dong J, Zhao L, Li X, Zuo HY, Wang CZ, et al. Application of 1H-NMR-based metabolomics for detecting injury induced by long-term microwave exposure in Wistar rats' urine. Anal Bioanal Chem 2012;404:69-78.&nbsp;doi:10.1007/s00216-012-6115-3. PMID:22706401</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Ebbels TM, Holmes E, Lindon JC, Nicholson JK. Evaluation of metabolic variation in normal rat strains from a statistical analysis of 1H NMR spectra of urine.

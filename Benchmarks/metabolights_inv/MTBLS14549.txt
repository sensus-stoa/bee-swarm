Study Title	Multi-omics and intervention analyses reveal a hindgut microbiota–secondary bile acid–mammary axis linked to milk yield in dairy cows
Study Description	Background In large scale dairy farming systems, maintaining stable milk yield post-lactation peak is crucial for dairy farms’ economic benefits. While sufficient evidence shows the microbiota profoundly impacts host production efficiency, the specific mechanisms by which hindgut microbiota support mid lactation milk performance via host-microbe interactions remain unclear. This study focused on the hindgut microbial ecosystem. By analyzing hindgut microbial composition and metabolic differences between high and low yield cows during mid-lactation, and conducting metabolite intervention, we clarified the biological mechanism by which hindgut microbiota regulates milk production through host interactions. Results Shotgun metagenomics, untargeted metabolomics, and targeted metabolomics of high versus normal-yield cows revealed that high-yield animals harbored distinct hindgut microbial communities enriched in bile salt hydrolase–positive taxa, and elevated levels of deoxycholic acid (DCA). Fecal microbiota transplantation from high-yield donors into antibiotic-treated recipient mice increased their milk production, accompanied by a shift in circulating bile acid profiles towards higher DCA. Oral administration of DCA to antibiotic-treated mice partially recapitulated these mammary and lactation phenotypes, supporting a causal contribution of bile acids downstream of the microbiota. In lactating cows, supplementation with a rumen-protected DCA formulation enhanced cumulative energy-corrected milk yield and tended to increase daily milk yield. DCA can directly upregulate the expression of genes related to the cell cycle progression in dairy cow mammary glands, thereby promoting and maintaining the activity of mammary epithelial cells in mid-lactation cows to support milk production. Conclusions Our multi-layered evidence demonstrates that hindgut microbiota-derived secondary bile acids, particularly DCA, act along a gut–mammary axis to enhance mammary epithelial function and lactation performance in dairy cows. These findings broaden the conceptual framework of microbiota–epithelial crosstalk and suggest microbial bile acid metabolism as a tractable target to improve lactation efficiency in ruminants.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Holstein dairy cows were recruited from a commercial dairy farm in Changchun, China, housing approximately 3,000 cows. Among them, second-parity cows in mid-lactation (120 ± 10 days in milk) were categorized into an H group (milk yield: 49.04 ± 1.28 kg/d) and an N group (milk yield: 34.55 ± 2.06 kg/d), with 12 cows in each group. None of the cows had received antibiotics or other medications in the six months prior to sampling.&nbsp;All cows were maintained under identical environmental and management conditions, including a uniform diet and water supply. Fecal samples were manually collected from the rectum using sterile gloves and transferred into sterile 50 mL cryogenic tubes. Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80℃ for further analysis. Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein, and serum was obtained after centrifugation for subsequent biochemical and metabolomic analyses</p>	<p>The liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system used for metabolomics analysis consisted of a Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) coupled with a Waters Xevo G2-XS QTof high-resolution mass spectrometer. The chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm).</p><p>Positive ion mode:&nbsp;Mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water; Mobile phase B: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile.</p><p>Negative ion mode:&nbsp;Mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water; Mobile phase B: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile.</p><p>Injection volume: 1 μL.</p>	<p>The raw data acquired with MassLynx V4.2 were subjected to data processing operations including peak picking and peak alignment using Progenesis QI software.&nbsp;</p>	<p>Weigh 50 mg of sample, add 1000 μL of extraction solution (methanol:acetonitrile:water = 2:2:1, v/v/v) containing internal standard (1000:2, internal standard concentration 2 mg/L), and vortex for 30 seconds.</p><p>Add ceramic beads, process with a 45 Hz grinder for 10 minutes, then ultrasonicate in an ice-water bath for 10 minutes.</p><p>Let stand at -20°C for one hour.</p><p>Centrifuge the sample at 12,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C.</p><p>Carefully transfer 500 μL of the supernatant to an EP tube.</p><p>Dry the extract in a vacuum concentrator.</p><p>Reconstitute the dried metabolites with 160 μL of extraction solution (acetonitrile:water = 1:1, v/v).</p><p>Vortex for 30 seconds, then ultrasonicate in an ice-water bath for 10 minutes.</p><p>Centrifuge the sample at 12,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C.</p><p>Carefully transfer 120 μL of the supernatant into a 2 mL autosampler vial. For each sample, take 10 μL and mix to prepare a QC sample for instrumental analysis.</p>	<p>The Waters Xevo G2-XS QTof high-resolution mass spectrometer was operated in MSe mode controlled by the acquisition software (MassLynx V4.2, Waters) to perform simultaneous low- and high-energy MS and MS/MS data acquisition. In each data acquisition cycle, dual-channel data collection was performed at both low collision energy and high collision energy. The low collision energy was set to 2 V, and the high collision energy ramp was set from 10 to 40 V. The scan rate was 0.2 seconds per spectrum.</p><p>The ESI source parameters were as follows: capillary voltage: 2000 V (positive ion mode) or -1500 V (negative ion mode); cone voltage: 30 V; source temperature: 150°C; desolvation temperature: 500°C; cone gas flow: 50 L/h; desolvation gas flow: 800 L/h.</p>	<p>Compound identification was performed based on the online METLIN database of Progenesis QI software and the Biomarker Technologies in-house database. Theoretical fragment ion recognition was also carried out simultaneously. The mass tolerance was set within 100 ppm for all identifications.</p>

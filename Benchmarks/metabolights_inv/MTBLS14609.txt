Study Title	Mechanism of Blueberry Anthocyanins in Ameliorating Radiation-Induced Intestinal Injury through Gut Microbiota Modulation
Study Description	Radiation-induced intestinal injury denotes structural and functional impairment of the intestines resulting directly or indirectly from ionising radiation, such as that employed in radiation therapy for malignant tumours in the abdominal or pelvic regions, within the treatment area. It is a prevalent and severe consequence of radiation therapy for abdominal and pelvic tumours, greatly affecting treatment outcomes and patients' quality of life. Western medicine primarily employs symptomatic management, including medical drug therapy, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, nutritional support therapy, endoscopic and surgical interventions, as well as stem cell transplantation. However, current therapeutic agents for RIII fall far short of achieving ideal outcomes. Previous studies indicate that blueberry anthocyanins (BA) possess not only anticancer properties but also potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-radiation activities.

Study Protocol Description	<p>This study collected a total of 18 mouse tissue samples, which were divided into three groups: control group, IR group, and BA group. After sample collection, quickly store it in a -80 ℃ freezer to avoid repeated freezing and thawing.</p><p>is protocol description</p>	<p>Instrument: Shimadzu LC-30A Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography System.</p><p>Column: Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 Column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm).</p><p>Mobile phase: Phase A is 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution, Phase B is 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile solution.</p><p>Gradient elution: 0-2 min, 5%-20% B; 2-5 min, 20%-60% B; 5-6 min, 60%-99% B; 6-7.5 min, 99% B; 7.5-7.6 min, 99%-5% B; 7.6-10 min, 5% B.</p><p>Column temperature: 40℃; Flow rate: 0.4 mL/min; Injection volume: 4 μL.</p>	<p>The raw data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard. Peak extraction, alignment, and retention time correction were performed using XCMS. Peaks with a missing rate &gt;50% were filtered out, blank values were filled using KNN (R impute package), and peak area correction was performed using SVR. Finally, metabolites with an identification score ≥0.5 and QC sample CV &lt;0.3 were selected for statistical analysis.</p>	<p>Remove the sample from -80 ℃ and thaw it on ice. Weigh 20 mg of tissue and homogenize it at 30 Hz for 20 seconds after adding steel balls. Add 400 μ L of 70% methanol water (including internal standard) extract, shake at 1500 rpm for 5 minutes, and let it stand in an ice bath for 15 minutes. Centrifuge at 4 ℃ and 12000 rpm for 10 minutes, take 300 μ L of supernatant and let it stand at -20 ℃ for 30 minutes; Centrifuge again at 4 ℃ and 12000 rpm for 3 minutes, and take 200 μ L of supernatant for analysis on the machine.</p><p><br></p>	<p>Instrument: SCIEX TripleTOF 6600+high-resolution mass spectrometer.</p><p>Ion source: electric spray ionization (ESI), positive and negative ion mode switching acquisition.</p><p>Ion source temperature: 550 ℃ (positive ions)/450 ℃ (negative ions).</p><p>Ionization voltage: 5000 V (positive ions)/-4000 V (negative ions).</p><p>Scanning range: 50-1000 Da (first level), 25-1000 Da (second level).</p><p>Collision energy: 30 eV (positive ion)/-30 eV (negative ion).</p><p><br></p>	<p>Metabolite identification is based on accurate mass (error &lt;10 ppm) and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) fragment matching. Databases include a self-built lab database, HMDB, KEGG, Metlin, and metDNA. A total of 5,705 metabolites were identified, of which 4,879 received secondary identification.</p>

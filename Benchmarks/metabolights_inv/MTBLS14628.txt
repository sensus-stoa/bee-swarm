Study Identifier	MTBLS14628
Study Title	Genetic Ablation of Sfxn5 Induces Mitochondrial Dysfunction and Precipitates Lethal Metabolic Crisis in Mice
Study Description	Mitochondrial disorders frequently manifest with life-threatening hepatic metabolic crises. Using a global Sfxn5-knockout mouse model, we investigated the systemic consequences of disrupting this mitochondrial inner-membrane transporter through integrated biochemical, metabolomic, histological, and ultrastructural analyses. Sfxn5 deficiency resulted in complete postnatal lethality accompanied by severe metabolic collapse and progressive multi-organ dysfunction. The liver emerged as the primary site of pathology, exhibiting marked mitochondrial structural damage and widespread disruption of central metabolic pathways, including the tricarboxylic acid cycle, fatty acid β-oxidation, and ammonia detoxification, leading to hyperammonemia and systemic metabolic stress. Importantly, liver-specific reconstitution of Sfxn5 partially restored mitochondrial metabolic function, substantially reduced hyperammonemia, and alleviated multi-organ pathology. Altogether, these findings identify hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction as the central driver of Sfxn5-dependent lethality and establish a critical role for Sfxn5 in maintaining mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis during early postnatal life.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Brain and liver tissues were harvested from WT and Sfxn5-knockout mouse pups at postnatal day 26. Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen after collection and stored at 80°C until analysis. Six biological replicates were analyzed for each tissue and genotype group, including WT brain, Sfxn5-knockout brain, WT liver, and Sfxn5-knockout liver. Prior to metabolite extraction, tissue samples were thawed at 4°C, and approximately 50 mg of each sample was processed.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using a SHIMADZU-LC30 ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system. Samples were analyzed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). During analysis, the samples were maintained in the autosampler at 4°C. The injection volume was 4 μL, the column temperature was 40°C, and the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–2 min, 0% B; 2–6 min, 0–48% B; 6–10 min, 48–100% B; 10–12 min, 100% B; 12–12.1 min, 100–0% B; and 12.1–15 min, 0% B.</p>	<p>Raw data were processed using MSDIAL software for peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching accurate mass with a mass tolerance of &lt;10 ppm and MS/MS spectra with a mass tolerance of &lt;0.01 Da against public databases, including HMDB, MassBank, and GNPS, as well as the in-house Bioprofile metabolite standards database (BP-DB). Ion peaks with more than 50% missing values within a group were removed before statistical analysis. Positive- and negative-ion data were normalized separately by total peak area, integrated, and then analyzed using Python software. The data were preprocessed by unit variance scaling before subsequent data analysis.</p>	<p>Tissue samples were thawed at 4°C, and 50 mg of each sample was transferred into an EP tube. Three medium steel beads and 200 μL of pre-cooled 80% methanol aqueous solution were added, and the samples were homogenized using a tissue disruptor. Subsequently, 800 μL of pre-cooled 80% methanol aqueous solution was added, followed by ultrasonic extraction in an ice bath for 20 min. The samples were then incubated at 20°C for 1 h and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and dried using a high-speed vacuum concentrator. Before mass spectrometry analysis, the dried extracts were reconstituted in 100 μL methanol/water solution (1:1, v/v), centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was used for analysis.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using a QE Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a HESI source. Each sample was analyzed in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes after UPLC separation. The ionization parameters were as follows: spray voltage, 3.8 kV in positive mode and 3.2 kV in negative mode; capillary temperature, 320°C; sheath gas, 30; auxiliary gas, 5; probe heater temperature, 350°C; and S-lens RF level, 50. The mass spectrometry acquisition time was 15 min, and the precursor ion scan range was 70–1050 m/z. The MS1 resolution was 70,000 at m/z 200, with an AGC target of 3e6 and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. For MS/MS acquisition, the 10 most intense precursor ions were selected for MS2 scans after each full scan. The MS2 resolution was 17,500 at m/z 200, with an AGC target of 1e5, a maximum injection time of 50 ms, HCD activation, an isolation window of 2 m/z, and stepped normalized collision energies of 20, 30, and 40.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed using MSDIAL software. The extracted features were annotated by accurate mass matching with a mass tolerance of &lt;10 ppm and MS/MS spectral matching with a mass tolerance of &lt;0.01 Da. The annotation was conducted against public metabolite databases, including HMDB, MassBank, and GNPS, as well as the in-house Bioprofile metabolite standards database (BP-DB).</p>

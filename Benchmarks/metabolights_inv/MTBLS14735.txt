Study Title	Penthorum chinense Pursh Ameliorates Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Alcohol-Related Liver Disease through Gut Microbiota Modulation and Metabolic Remodeling
Study Description	Metabolic dysfunction-associated alcohol-related liver disease (MetALD) is characterized by hepatic steatosis, oxidative stress, inflammation, and gut microbiota dysbiosis resulting from the coexistence of metabolic dysfunction and alcohol consumption. Penthorum chinense Pursh is a traditional edible medicinal plant rich in polyphenols; however, its role in MetALD remains unclear. In this study, a polyphenol-rich Penthorum chinense extract (PCE) was characterized by UPLC–TOF/MS, with gallic acid and pinocembrin identified as major constituents. The protective effects of PCE were evaluated in a mouse model of MetALD induced by a high-fat diet combined with ethanol exposure. PCE administration markedly alleviated hepatic steatosis and liver injury, reduced inflammatory cytokine levels, and improved antioxidant status. Moreover, PCE partially normalized the expression of genes associated with lipid metabolism and inflammation, including Srebp-1c, Pparα, and Nfkb1. 16S rRNA sequencing revealed that PCE partially restored gut microbial dysbiosis, characterized by increased abundances of Dubosiella and Ligilactobacillus. Untargeted metabolomics further demonstrated significant alterations in hepatic metabolic profiles, particularly in pathways related to amino acid metabolism and energy metabolism. Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between differential gut microbial taxa and hepatic metabolites. These findings suggest that PCE ameliorates MetALD by improving hepatic metabolic homeostasis, oxidative stress, inflammation, and gut microbiota composition. This study provides experimental evidence supporting the potential application of Penthorum chinense as a functional food resource for the prevention and management of metabolic liver disorders.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Mouse liver samples were collected for untargeted metabolomics analysis. The submitted samples included three experimental groups: CON, MOD and PCE-H, with six biological replicates per group, for a total of 18 liver samples. After collection, all samples were immediately stored at -80 °C until metabolomics analysis.</p>	<p>LC separation was performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Polar metabolites were separated on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). Mobile phase A consisted of water containing 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonium hydroxide, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The autosampler temperature was maintained at 4 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL.</p>	<p>Raw LC-MS/MS data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard software version 3.0.24054. The converted files were processed using an in-house R-based workflow for peak detection, feature extraction, alignment and integration. Data preprocessing included outlier filtering based on relative standard deviation, missing value filtering, missing value imputation using half of the minimum value, and normalization using the total ion current. The processed data matrix was used for downstream statistical and multivariate analyses.</p>	<p>Dried liver tissue samples were weighed, and 25 mg of each sample was transferred into an EP tube with two homogenization beads. Then 500 μL extraction solvent, consisting of methanol:acetonitrile:water at 2:2:1 (v/v/v) and containing isotope-labeled internal standards, was added. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, homogenized at 35 Hz for 4 min, and sonicated in an ice-water bath for 5 min. The homogenization and sonication steps were repeated three times. Samples were incubated at -40 °C for 1 h. Then 300 μL of liquid was transferred to a 0.22 μm filter plate. The protein precipitation plate and collection plate were placed into a positive-pressure device and filtered at 6 psi for 180 s. The filtrate was collected for LC-MS/MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots of the supernatant from all experimental samples.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry was performed using an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) controlled by Xcalibur software version 4.4. MS and MS/MS data were acquired in electrospray ionization (ESI) mode using both positive and negative ion modes. The scan m/z range was 70-1200. The ESI source parameters were set as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 50 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 15 Arb; capillary temperature, 320 °C; full MS resolution, 60000; MS/MS resolution, 15000; collision energy, SNCE 20/30/40. The spray voltage was 3.8 kV in positive ion mode and -3.4 kV in negative ion mode.</p>	<p>Metabolite annotation was performed using an in-house R-based workflow with BiotreeDB version 3.0. Features were annotated based on matching retention time, precursor ion m/z, MS1 information and MS/MS spectra against reference and in-house databases. Metabolite identification levels were assigned according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI): Level 1 for matches to authentic standards with MS1, MS2 and retention time; Level 2 for matches to public database MS1 and MS2 spectra; Level 3 for matches to theoretical database MS1, MS2 and predicted retention time; and Level 4 for unknown compounds.</p>

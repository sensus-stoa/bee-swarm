Study Identifier	MTBLS14781
Study Title	Lactate-driven lysine lactylation mediates high-level colistin resistance in Salmonella
Study Description	Intracellular pathogens are particularly refractory to antibiotic eradication, leading to persistent and relapsing infections. However, how intracellular metabolic microenvironments shape antibiotic resistance phenotypes remains poorly understood. Here we delineate a metabolic-regulatory axis wherein Salmonella Typhimurium undergoes profound metabolic rewiring upon intramacrophage adaptation, suppressing oxidative phosphorylation and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle while upregulating anaerobic glycolysis. This metabolic shift generates elevated lactate levels that drive widespread lysine lactylation, including modifications on the two-component system regulators PmrA and PmrD. Lactylation at specific residues stabilizes phosphorylated PmrA, thereby activating lipopolysaccharide modification genes that remodel the outer membrane charge to confer high-level colistin resistance. Critically, metabolic intervention with exogenous citrate restores TCA cycle activity, reduces lactate production and subsequent PmrA/D lactylation, and resensitizes bacteria to colistin both in vitro and in vivo. These findings reveal metabolic reprogramming as a deterministic driver of post-translational regulatory networks governing antibiotic resistance, offering a paradigm for targeting bacterial metabolism to combat drug-resistant bacteria.

Study Protocol Description	<p>For untargeted metabolomic analysis comparing S. Tm in MHB with LPM medium, overnight bacteria were diluted 1:100 into 100 mL fresh medium and cultured at 37°C (180 rpm) for an OD600 to 0.5. Cells were quenched with methanol/acetonitrile (1:1) solution and collected by centrifugation at 8,000 g for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and dried in a vacuum centrifuge, and stored at-80 °C.</p>	<p>All samples were for two LC/MS methods. One aliquot was analyzed using positive ion conditions and was eluted from T3 column (Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 Column 1.8µm,2.1mm*100mm) using 0.1 % formic acid in water as solvent A and 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile as solvent B in the following gradient: 5 to 20 % in 1 min, increased to 99 % in the following 2 mins and held for 1.5 min, then come back to 5 % mobile phase B witnin 0.1 min, held for 1.4 min. The analytical conditions were as follows, column temperature, 40 °C; flow rate, 0.4 mL/min; injection volume, 3 μL; Another aliquot</p><p>was using negative ion conditions and was the same as the elution gradient of positive mode.</p>	<p>The raw mass spectrometry data were converted to mzML format using ProteoWizard. Subsequently, XCMS was employed for peak detection, peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area integration. Features with a high missing rate across the sample groups were filtered out. For the remaining features, missing values were imputed using different strategies: the KNN method was applied to features with a low missing rate, while missing values for features with a high missing rate were replaced with one-fifth of the minimum non-zero value of the respective feature. Following imputation, signal drift correction of peak areas was performed using a support vector regression (SVR) method based on quality control (QC) samples. Ultimately, a final metabolic feature data matrix was obtained after peak extraction, alignment, missing value imputation, and signal correction.</p>	<p>The sample stored at-80 °C refrigerator was thawed on ice. The thawed sample was homogenized by a grinder (30 HZ) for 20 s. A 400 μL solution (Methanol : Water = 4:1, V/V) containing internal standard was added into 40 mg of the sample and vortexed for 3 min. The sample was placed in liquid nitrogen for 5 min and on the dry ice for 5 min, and then thawed on ice and vortexed for 2 min. This freeze-thaw circle was repeated three times in total. The sample was centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 10 min (4 °C). A 300 μL of supernatant was collected and placed in-20 °C for 30 min. The sample was then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 3 min (4 °C). A 200 μL aliquots of supernatant were transferred for LC-MS analysis</p>	<p>All the methods alternated between full scan MS and data dependent MSn scans using dynamic exclusion. MS analyses were carried out using electrospray ionization in the positive ion mode and negative ion mode using full scan analysis over m/z 70-1000 at 60000 resolution. Additional MS settings are: ion spray voltage, 3.8 KV or 3.4 KV in positive or negative modes, respevtively; Sheath gas (Arb), 60; Aux gas, 20; Ion transfer tube temperature, 320 °C; Vaporizer temperature, 350 °C; Collision energy, 30,40,50 V; Top N vs Top speed, 10. Mass spectrometry was carried out on a Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Scientific, USA)</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed based on accurate mass, MS/MS fragmentation information, and retention time. Experimental data were matched against an in-house database, public databases (e.g., HMDB, KEGG, and METLIN), and predicted spectral libraries, and metabolites were annotated according to the matching results. For metabolites with authentic standards, identification was confirmed as Level 1 based on retention time and MS/MS spectral matching. For metabolites without authentic standards, annotations were assigned as Level 2 or Level 3 based on MS/MS spectral library matching. Features lacking reliable MS/MS information and identified solely by m/z and retention time characteristics were designated as Level 4. Finally, only compounds with a comprehensive identification score greater than 0.5 and a coefficient of variation (CV) below 30% in QC samples were retained. The results from positive and negative ion modes were combined; in cases of duplicate compounds, the entry with the highest identification level and the highest score within that level was kept.</p>

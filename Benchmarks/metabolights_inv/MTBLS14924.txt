Study Protocol Description	<p>Fresh mature citrus fruits (Citrus sinensis) and immature citrus fruits were purchased from a commercial orchard in Yunnan Province, China, during the 2023–2024 harvest seasons. Fruits without visible mechanical damage or disease symptoms were selected for subsequent isolation of citrus-derived exosome-like nanoparticles (CDENs). Unless otherwise indicated, mature fruits were used for all experiments.</p><p>Fresh citrus juice was obtained by mechanical squeezing of mature fruits and designated as EXs. CDENs were isolated from the juice by sequential differential ultracentrifugation followed by sucrose density gradient ultracentrifugation. The major exosome-like nanoparticle fraction recovered from the density gradient was designated CDENs. Two additional density-gradient fractions with lower buoyant densities were collected simultaneously and designated Band1 and Band2, respectively. Exosome-like nanoparticles isolated from immature citrus fruits using the same procedure were designated IM-CDENs.</p><p>To investigate the contribution of phosphatidylcholine (PC) to the biological activity of CDENs, PC was selectively removed from CDEN membranes by thin-layer chromatography (TLC)-guided lipid separation followed by lipid reconstitution, generating the PC-depleted nanoparticles (CDENs-PC). The preparation and characterization procedures for CDENs, CDENs-PC, Band1, Band2, EXs, and IM-CDENs are described in detail in the corresponding Methods section.</p><p>All samples were stored at −80 °C until lipid extraction. Lipids were extracted using a modified Folch method prior to LC–MS-based untargeted lipidomics analysis. All biological replicates were processed independently using identical extraction and analytical procedures.</p>	<p> Lipid analysis was performed by using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (UHPLC-QTRAP-MS/MS, Agilent, USA). Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) conditions were used to analyze the various lipid species. Mobile phase A consisted of water/acetonitrile/isopropanol (5:3:2, v/v/v) and 10 mM ammonium formate, while mobile phase B consisted of water/acetonitrile/isopropanol (1:9:90, v/v/v) and 10 mM ammonium formate. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0-2 min, B 15%; 2-11 min, B 15-100%; 11-12 min, B 100%; 12-16 min, B 100-15%. The flow rate of the mobile phase was 400 µL/min, with a total time of 16 minutes per injection and a sample volume of 4 µL. The data were reported as percentages of total signals for the molecular species, which were determined after normalizing the signals to internal standards of the same lipid class.</p>	<p>All files were converted to the mzML open file formats using the MSConvert tool of ProteoWizard softwar</p>	<p>Lipid extraction was performed for all isolated nanoparticle fractions, including CDENs, CDENs-PC, IM-CDENs, EXs, and the sucrose density gradient fractions (Band1 and Band2). Briefly, purified nanoparticles obtained by differential ultracentrifugation and sucrose density gradient ultracentrifugation were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 150,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C. The pellets were resuspended in PBS and subjected to lipid extraction using a modified Folch protocol.</p><p>Briefly, methanol was first added to each sample to disrupt vesicle membranes, followed by chloroform containing 0.01% butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) as an antioxidant. After thorough vortex mixing, ultrapure water was added to induce phase separation. The mixtures were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the lower organic phase containing total lipids was carefully collected. A second extraction was performed using chloroform/methanol (2:1, v/v), and the organic phases were combined.</p><p>The pooled lipid extracts were evaporated to dryness under a gentle stream of nitrogen and reconstituted in isopropanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) prior to LC–MS analysis. All extraction procedures were carried out on ice or at 4 °C whenever possible to minimize lipid degradation.</p><p>Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots from all lipid extracts. Before sample analysis, several QC injections were performed to equilibrate the LC–MS system. During the analytical sequence, one QC sample was analyzed after every ten study samples to continuously monitor instrument stability, signal reproducibility, and retention-time consistency. Blank solvent injections were also included throughout the analytical run to identify potential background contaminants and carryover.</p>	<p>Instrument: Agilent 6546 LC/Q-TOF mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source.</p><p>Modality: Data were acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes using full-scan MS and data-dependent MS/MS acquisition.</p><p>Mass range: Full-scan spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 100–2000. In data-dependent acquisition mode, MS/MS spectra were automatically acquired for the three most abundant precursor ions detected in each full-scan spectrum.</p><p>Other details: All data were acquired in centroid mode. Four injections were performed for each sample, including full-scan and DDA analyses in both positive and negative ionization modes.</p>	<p>To ensure high-confidence accurate identification, annotation was performed and verified using information from different properties. In the specific case of the manual curation, for each putative annotation the following attributes were considered: 1) Accurate match of m/z value of the precursor ion, defined by the nature of the ion showing the maximal ionisation efficiency (i.e., (de)protonated ion or any specific adduct), taking advantage of the complementarity of the information provided simultaneously by each ionisation mode (e.g., for phosphatidylethanolamines (PE) [M+H]+ and [M-H]-; for phosphatidylcholines (PC) [M+H]+ and [M+HCO2]-; for monogalactosyldiacylglycerols (MGDG) and digalactosyldiacylglycerols (DGDG) [M+NH4]+ and [M+HCO2]+; and for diglycerides (DG) and triglycerides (TG) [M+NH4]+). 2) The relationship between RT and the compound's Kendrick mass defect (KMD). In reversed-phase liquid chromatography, there is a class-specific relationship between RT and the KMD value, which links the total length of fatty acyl chains and the number of double bonds[1][2]. 3) MS/MS fragmentation pattern in both polarity modes: it is well known that the fragmentation pattern depends on both the lipid class and the ionisation mode[2][3]. Therefore, characteristic fragment ions or neutral losses in the MS/MS spectra can be used to confirm the lipid class (e.g., fragment at m/z 184.073 for lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC) or neutral loss of 141.0191 for PE in positive mode), whereas in other cases they made it possible to deduce the specific fatty acyl chains of that lipid (e.g., the fragment at m/z 255.2330 for phosphatidic acids (PA), PE, phosphatidylglycerols (PG) and phosphatidylinositols (PI) in negative mode was an indicator that that compound contained at least one 16:0 fatty acyl chain).</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>All soil samples were pre-incubated for 5 days to stabilize microbial respiration in the soil. The watering frequency was determined through preliminary experiments (SI Text S2). In the main experiment, each pot was filled with 1 kilogram of soil sample. Before the experiment began, 10 grams (accurate to 0.0001 grams) of three different types of MP were added to 45 of the soil samples, while the remaining 15 soil samples received no MP (CK). After adding the samples, the total weight of the pots and samples was recorded. A cultivation cycle consists of 20 days. During the first 10 days, deionized water was added daily to keep the soil samples moist. During the subsequent 10 days, watering was stopped to allow the soil to dry naturally, serving as the drying phase. This cycle was repeated five times, totaling 100 days. During the cultivation period, all pots were placed in a greenhouse to ensure environmental conditions remained as close to natural as possible. Soil samples were collected at the end of each cycle, specifically at 60 and 100 days. For each collection, three replicates were taken from each experimental group using a sterile trowel, totaling 12 samples. Each sample was divided into 10 portions, each weighing 50 grams. Three soil samples were stored in a -80°C freezer.</p>	<p>A 3 μL sample is injected and separated on an HSS T3 chromatographic column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm), then introduced into the mass spectrometer for detection. Mobile phase A is water/acetonitrile (95/5, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B is acetonitrile/isopropanol/water (47.5/47.5/5, v/v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate is 0.40 mL/min, and the column temperature is 40 °C.</p>	<p>We describe the processing of raw mass spectrometry data in two aspects: qualitative and quantitative. The raw data from positive and negative ion modes are processed separately, but the workflow and parameters are identical for both.</p><p>For qualitative analysis:&nbsp;The raw data are imported into the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for database searching and identification. The MS and MS/MS mass spectral information are matched against metabolite databases. The primary databases used include commercial databases such as HMDB and Metlin, public databases, and our in-house database. From this, we obtain information on each peak and the corresponding metabolite names.</p><p>For quantitative analysis:&nbsp;The raw data, including quality control (QC) samples, are imported into Progenesis QI. The software performs baseline filtering, peak detection, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment, ultimately generating a data matrix containing retention time, m/z, and peak intensity. To better analyze the data, a series of preprocessing steps are applied to the raw data, primarily including missing value recoding and data normalization. Through these preprocessing steps, the influence of variations unrelated to the research objective is reduced, facilitating the screening and analysis of potential differential metabolites of interest. In the default workflow, we process the quantitative mass spectrometry data in the following order:</p><p>(1) Missing value filtering:&nbsp;Samples may have missing values for various reasons (a. signal too low to detect; b. detection errors such as ion suppression or instrument instability; c. algorithmic limitations in peak picking that fail to extract low signals from background; d. incomplete resolution of overlapping peaks during deconvolution). Missing values in the data table are typically represented as empty cells or 'NA' (Not A Number). Filtering data based on the proportion of missing values within samples or groups is a common practice in metabolomics analysis.</p><p>(2) Missing value imputation:&nbsp;For missing values that are not filtered out, ignoring them directly may affect subsequent computational algorithms and trigger errors. Therefore, imputation is necessary. Common methods include using a small value (e.g., half of the minimum), median (suitable for skewed distributions), mean (suitable for normal distributions), random forest, expectation-maximization (EM), and zero filling.</p><p>(3) Data normalization:&nbsp;Normalization maps data to a specific range for further processing, facilitating efficient computation. It is an important step in data preprocessing that can eliminate systematic errors arising from sample handling, concentration differences, instrument drift, and other factors. Common normalization methods in metabolomics include median, mean, total sum, reference sample, and internal standard normalization.</p><p>(4) QC validation:&nbsp;The RSD (relative standard deviation = standard deviation / mean) of each ion across QC samples is calculated. A smaller RSD indicates less variation. In metabolomics, variables with RSD exceeding a threshold are typically excluded from differential quantitative analysis, as they exhibit greater fluctuation during the experiment. Generally, variables with RSD &gt; 30% are excluded.</p><p>(5) Data transformation:&nbsp;Metabolomics analysis generally assumes that data follow a normal or Gaussian distribution, so log transformation is commonly applied. Log transformation corrects heteroscedasticity in the dataset, reduces or eliminates asymmetry in the data structure, and improves the normality of the data distribution. This satisfies the assumptions of common statistical methods such as Student's t-test, linear regression, and correlation analysis, thereby reducing analytical errors.</p>	<p>Take 100 mg of solid sample into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, and add one grinding bead with a diameter of 6 mm. Add 800 μL of extraction solvent (methanol : water = 4 : 1, v/v) containing four internal standards (including L-2-chlorophenylalanine at 0.02 mg/mL, among others) for metabolite extraction. The sample solution is ground in a frozen tissue grinder for 6 min (-10 °C, 50 Hz), followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). The sample is then allowed to stand at -20 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 4 °C and 13,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant is transferred to an autosampler vial with a glass insert for instrument analysis.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry signal acquisition for the sample is performed in both positive and negative ion scanning modes, with a mass scan range of m/z 70-1050. The ion spray voltage is set to +3500 V for positive ion mode and -3000 V for negative ion mode. The sheath gas flow is 50 arb, the auxiliary heating gas flow is 13 arb, and the ion source heating temperature is 450 °C. The collision energy is applied in a cyclic ramping pattern of 20-40-60 V.</p>	<p>MS and MS/MS spectral information are matched against public metabolomics databases, including HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/), Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and the Majorbio in-house database, to obtain metabolite identification information. After database searching, the resulting data matrix is uploaded to the Majorbio Cloud Platform (cloud.majorbio.com)&nbsp;for further analysis.</p><p>First, data preprocessing is performed on the matrix as follows: The data matrix is filtered using the 80% rule to remove missing values, meaning that variables with non-zero values in at least 80% of samples within at least one group are retained. Remaining missing values are then imputed using the minimum value found in the original matrix. To reduce errors caused by sample preparation and instrument instability, total sum normalization is applied to normalize the response intensities of mass spectral peaks, yielding a normalized data matrix. Simultaneously, variables with a relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt; 30% in QC samples are removed, and log10 transformation is applied to obtain the final data matrix for subsequent analyses.</p><p>Next, principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) are performed on the preprocessed data matrix using the ropls package (Version 1.6.2) in R language. A 7-fold cross-validation is used to evaluate the stability of the models. Significantly differential metabolites are selected based on the variable importance in projection (VIP) values obtained from the OPLS-DA model and p-values from Student's t-test. Metabolites with VIP &gt; 1 and p &lt; 0.05 are considered significantly differential metabolites.</p><p>These differential metabolites are then subjected to metabolic pathway annotation using the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) to obtain the pathways in which they are involved. Pathway enrichment analysis is performed using the scipy.stats package in Python, and Fisher's exact test is applied to identify the biological pathways most relevant to the experimental treatments.</p>

Study Title	liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry
Study Description	Bacterial cells harvested at peak enzyme activity were subjected to untargeted metabolomic profiling to evaluate metabolic changes during straw degradation compared to control (CK) groups. Samples were extracted using pre-chilled methanol–water (4:1, v/v) and analyzed on a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system operating in both positive and negative ionisation modes. Metabolites were identified by database matching against HMDB, KEGG, and Metlin. Downstream analyses integrated metabolomic findings with transcriptomic data to identify co-regulated pathways and construct correlation networks linking metabolite abundances with CMCase enzymatic activity.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Six cultures of Bacillus siamensis LSZ23 were used for untargeted metabolomic profiling, consisting of three control (CK) replicates and three straw-treatment (straw) replicates. Cells were harvested at peak cellulase activity, immediately centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Bacterial pellets were then snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until metabolite extraction.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using a UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a Waters HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm). The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water/methanol (95:5, v/v), and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile/methanol/water (47.5:47.5:5, v/v/v). Separation was achieved using the following gradient program: 0–1 min: 2% B; 1–9 min: 2–95% B (linear); 9–10 min: 95–100% B; 10–12 min: 100% B; 12–13 min: 100–2% B. Flow rate was 0.40 mL/min. Injection volume was 3 μL.</p>	<p>Raw MS data files were processed using Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA). Mass features were extracted with mass tolerance of ±5 ppm and retention time tolerance of ±0.5 min. Peak alignment and gap-filling were performed using automated alignment algorithm. Quality control was performed by filtering features with coefficient of variation (CV) in QC samples &gt; 30%, which were subsequently excluded from further analysis. Intensity data were normalised using total ion chromatogram (TIC) normalisation based on sample quality peaks. The resulting feature matrix contained approximately 800–1200 metabolite features across 15 study samples (6 CK, 6 straw-treated, 3 QC replicates). Data were uploaded to MajorBio Cloud Platform (cloud.majorbio.com) for downstream preprocessing, including removal of features with &gt;80% missing values, retention of features with abundance ≥80% above background noise, and log10 transformation of QC-normalised features.</p>	<p>Each bacterial pellet (50 mg) was extracted in 400 μL of pre-chilled methanol–water (4:1, v/v) containing 0.02 mg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanine as internal standard. Samples were homogenised at low temperature using a high-throughput tissue disruptor (Wonbio-96c, Shanghai Wanbo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), vortexed, and ultrasonicated on ice for 30 min. After incubation at −20 °C for 30 min, samples were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were transferred to LC-MS vials and stored at −20 °C until analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes of supernatant from all study samples (CK and straw groups combined), then divided into aliquots and processed in parallel with study samples.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry was performed using a Q Exactive HF-X orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionisation (ESI) modes with the following parameters: Positive ionisation mode: Spray voltage: 3500 V; Negative ionisation mode: Spray voltage: 3000 V; Sheath gas flow rate: 40 arb; Auxiliary gas flow rate: 13 arb; Ion transfer tube temperature: 450 °C; Scan range: m/z 70–1050; Resolving power: 120,000 at m/z 200 (full MS); Data acquisition mode: Full MS with data-dependent acquisition (DDA); Collision energy: stepped 20, 40, 60 eV for MS/MS fragmentation.</p>	<p>Metabolites were identified by spectral matching against three public mass spectral databases: HMDB (Human Metabolome Database; http://www.hmdb.ca/), Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes; https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Identification was based on matching of precursor m/z and MS/MS spectra with mass error tolerance of ±5 ppm. Significantly differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) between control and straw-treatment groups were identified using the following criteria: (1) Variable importance in projection (VIP) &gt; 1.0 from orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA); (2) Unadjusted Student's t-test p-value &lt; 0.05; (3) Model validity was assessed using 7-fold cross-validation and 200 permutation tests; models with permuted R²Y and Q² lower than the original, and negative Q² regression intercept were considered valid. Metabolite identifications were classified according to Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) confidence levels: Level 1 (confirmed with authentic standard), Level 2 (putative annotation based on spectral similarity to databases), or Level 3 (putative annotation with partial structure elucidation). Pathway analysis was performed using KEGG database and scipy.stats package in Python, with pathways reaching p &lt; 0.05 in both transcriptomics and metabolomics datasets regarded as jointly significant.</p>

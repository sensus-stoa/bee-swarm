Study Identifier	MTBLS15532
Study Title	Multi-omics-identified StAPR3 enhances potato Cd tolerance and tuber quality via sulfur metabolism and glutathione redox homeostasis
Study Description	<p>Cadmium (Cd) in agricultural soils inhibits potato growth, disrupts tuber development, and causes Cd accumulation in edible tubers, threatening food safety. Here, we determined the EC50 of Cd in potato, which induced oxidative stress and chloroplast damage. Transcriptomic and metabolomic co-analysis identified StAPR3, an APS reductase involved in sulfur metabolism, as a Cd-responsive candidate. Heterologous yeast expression and potato overexpression demonstrated that StAPR3 enhances Cd tolerance. Under Cd treatment, StAPR3-overexpressing lines maintained superior growth, while wild-type plants showed oxidative burst and photosynthetic dysfunction. Transgenic tubers exhibited increased yield, higher dry matter, starch, amino acids, and K, Ca, Mg, lower reducing sugars, and reduced Cd accumulation. Enzymatic assays confirmed StAPR3 catalyzes APS to AMP; LC-MS/MS showed elevated AMP, cysteine, and glutathione in transgenic lines. Thus, StAPR3 improves Cd tolerance, tuber yield, nutritional quality, and edible safety, offering a genetic target for breeding safe, high-quality potato cultivars on Cd-contaminated soils.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Potato seedlings (cultivar ‘Atlantic’) were cultivated on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (½ MS) medium under controlled growth conditions: temperature 25 ± 2 °C, 16 h light / 8 h dark photoperiod, 60% relative humidity. Three-week-old homogeneous plantlets were used for cadmium stress treatment. Plantlets were transferred to ½ MS medium containing 100 μM CdCl2(Cd-exposed group); control plantlets were cultured on Cd-free ½ MS medium. Above-ground tissues (stems and leaves) were sampled at three time points: 0 d (sampled just prior to Cd supplementation, shared baseline for both control and Cd-treated groups), 7 d and 21 d post-Cd exposure. Three independent biological replicates were collected for each time-point and treatment. Harvested tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Frozen samples were shipped to the metabolomics sequencing facility on dry ice for subsequent metabolomic profiling.</p>	<p>The sample extracts were analyzed using an UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC TM AD) and Tandem mass spectrometry system. The analytical conditions were as follows, UPLC: column, Agilent SB-C18 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm * 100 mm); The mobile phase was consisted of solvent A, pure water with 0.1% formic acid, and solvent B, acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. Sample measurements were performed with a gradient program that employed the starting conditions of 95% A, 5% B. Within 9 min, a linear gradient to 5% A, 95% B was programmed, and a composition of 5% A, 95% B was kept for 1 min. Subsequently, a composition of 95% A, 5.0% B was adjusted within 1.1 min and kept for 2.9 min. The flow velocity was set as 0.35 mL per minute; The column oven was set to 40°C; The injection volume was 2 μL. The effluent was alternatively connected to an ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP)-MS.</p>	<p>The raw MS data were converted to mzML files using ProteoWizard MSConvert before importing into freely available XCMS software</p>	<p>Using vacuum freeze-drying technology, place the biological samples in a lyophilizer (Scientz-100F),then grinding (30 Hz, 1.5 min) the samples to powder form by using a grinder (MM 400, Retsch). Next, weigh 50 mg of sample powder using an electronic balance (MS105DΜ) and add 1200 μL of -20 °C pre-cooled 70% methanolic aqueous internal standard extract (less than 50 mg added at the rate of 1200 μL extractant per 50 mg sample). Vortex once every 30 min for 30 sec, for a total of 6 times. After centrifugation (rotation speed 12000 rpm, 3 min), the supernatant was aspirated, and the sample was filtered through a microporous membrane (0.22 μm pore size) and stored in the injection vial for UPLC-MS/MS analysis.</p>	<p>The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: source temperature 550°C; ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V (positive ion mode)/-4500 V (negative ion mode); ion source gas I (GSI), gas II(GSII), curtain gas (CUR) were set at 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively; the collision-activated dissociation(CAD) was high. QQQ scans were acquired as MRM experiments with collision gas (nitrogen) set to medium. DP and CE for individual MRM transitions was done with further DP and CE optimization.A specific set of MRM transitions were monitored for each period according to the metabolites eluted within this period.</p>	<p>Qualitative identification of metabolites was performed based on an in house database using MS/MS spectral information. Isotopic signals, redundant signals originating from adduct ions including K+, Na+ and NH4+, as well as fragment ion signals derived from larger molecular weight parent compounds were eliminated during analysis.Metabolite quantification was carried out via multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) on a triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer. In MRM mode, the first quadrupole filters the precursor ion (parent ion) of the target analyte to exclude ions corresponding to other molecular-weight species for preliminary interference removal. The precursor ion undergoes collision induced dissociation in the collision cell to generate numerous fragment ions. A diagnostic fragment ion is further selected by the triple quadrupole filter to eliminate non target ion interferences, thereby achieving accurate and reproducible quantification. After acquiring mass spectrometric data of metabolites across different samples, chromatographic peaks of all analytes were integrated. Peak integration was corrected for the mass spectral peaks of the same metabolite among distinct samples (Fraga et al., 2010).</p><p><br></p>

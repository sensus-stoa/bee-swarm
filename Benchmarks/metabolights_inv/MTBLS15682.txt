Study Identifier	MTBLS15682
Study Title	Influence of moderate electric field and ultrasound processing on the global metabolome of a blended juice rich in anthocyanins and betalains
Study Description	Metabolomic analysis demonstrated that moderate electric field (MEF) and ultrasound (US) treatments applied to blended fruit juice caused limited changes in the overall metabolite profile. The analysis revealed high anthocyanin stability, such as malvidin 3-O-glucoside, cyanidin 3-O-glucoside, and cyanidin 3-O-rutinoside. Color, pH, and soluble solids parameters were also preserved, underscoring the maintenance of the product physicochemical characteristics. Targeted quantification confirmed the stability of phenolic compounds and anthocyanins, while betalains showed greater variability across the evaluated conditions. Despite these specific differences, antioxidant capacity remained similar among the treatments. Thus, the results indicate that the MEF and US conditions employed were sufficiently mild to preserve the juices quality attributes and have potential as non-thermal processing alternatives for producing juices with high retention of bioactive compounds and quality characteristics.

Study Protocol Description	<p>A blended juice, composed of pitaya juice (24 %), blackberries (19 %), orange juice (16 %), blueberry (16 %), raspberry (16 %), water (8 %), and mint (1 %), was prepared using a domestic juice extractor (Juicer Philips Walita 500 W). All fruits were purchased in a local market (Porto Alegre, RS, Brazil). The ingredients were selected to obtain a juice containing a diverse range of compound classes. After preparation, the juice was filtered, homogenized, and distributed into PET bottles.</p><p>The bottled juice was stored at -14 ± 2 °C until processing. The processing experiments were conducted immediately after thawing the juice to minimize potential chemical changes.</p><p>Samples were treated by three different technologies: conventional treatment (CV), moderate electric field (MEF) and ultrasound (US). The objective was to evaluate the individual contributions of distinct processing mechanisms (temperature, electric field, and acoustic cavitation) and their respective effects on product quality. Untreated juice (UJ) serves as a control.</p>	<p>Untargeted full-scan data were obtained using a Nexera X2 UHPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan) and the Impact II MS (Bruker Daltonics, USA) with electrospray ionization (ESI) source and quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-TOF) detector. Samples were randomly injected into an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 x 50 mm, 1.8 μm particle size). The mobile phase was composed of a gradient of: (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. A linear gradient was applied: 0.0-0.5 min, 0% B; 0.5-8.0 min, 100% B; 8.0-9.0 min isocratic at 100% B; 9.0-10.0 min, 0% B; 10-11 min isocratic at 0 % B. The mobile phase flow rate was 0.5 mL/min and the temperature was set at 40 °C. The analysis was performed with an injection volume of 5 μL per sample.</p>	<p>The conversion to mzML was performed directly by Data Analysis (Bruker). Data obtained from UHPLC-QTOF-MS analysis were processed using the open-source software MZmine3. This process groups mass spectral features according to expected isotopic patterns, adducts, and charge states, aligning them across all samples. Filtering of the data and statistical analysis were performed using R (version 4.4.0), following the methodology described by Dzakovich et al. (2024). Initially, features not at least 10 times higher in the quality control samples (QCs) than in the process blank were excluded from analysis. The analysis was then restricted to features that are present in 100% of QCs and those with a coefficient of variation lower than 30% in the QCs. To minimize analytical variation, instrumental drift was corrected using pooled quality control samples and the notame package in R (Klåvus et al., 2020). The workflow consisted of QC-based LOESS drift modeling, feature-wise signal correction, and quality assessment based on QC-derived metrics, including the relative standard deviation (RSD) and D-ratio. Variables that did not meet the predefined QC quality criteria after correction were removed prior to downstream statistical analyses. Missing values were imputed with half of the lowest peak area value in the dataset. Data was clustered with the notame R package (Klåvus et al., 2020). All data was log2 transformed prior to analysis. The data were statistically analyzed using a combination of tools: univariate analysis (Student's t-test, ANOVA, and Tukey’s tests), and multivariate analysis (principal component analysis).</p><p><br></p><p>Dzakovich MP, Goggans ML, Thomas-Ahner JM, Moran NE, Clinton SK, Francis DM, Cooperstone JL (2024). Transcriptomics and Metabolomics Reveal Tomato Consumption Alters Hepatic Xenobiotic Metabolism and Induces Steroidal Alkaloid Metabolite Accumulation in Mice. Molecular Nutrition and Food Research, 68(4), 1–11. https://doi.org/10.1002/mnfr.202300239</p><p>Klåvus A, Kokla M, Noerman S, Koistinen VM, Tuomainen M, Zarei I, Meuronen T, Häkkinen MR, Rummukainen S, Babu AF, Sallinen T, Kärkkäinen O, Paananen J (2020). “Notame”: Workflow for Non-Targeted LC–MS Metabolic Profiling. Metabolites, 10(135), 1–35. https://doi.org/doi:10.3390/metabo10040135</p>	<p>Samples were extracted using the adapted protocol described by Mercali et al. (2025). Briefly, samples were mixed with a methanol 0.1 % formic acid (final concentration of 2:1 methanol:water) to extract as many small molecular weight metabolites as possible. Samples were homogenized using a sample homogenizer for 1 min at 5 speed (150 G, 5.0 m s-1) (Bead ruptor 4, OMNI International) and centrifuged at 13.400 rpm (12100 × g) for 5 min (MiniSpin, Eppendorf). The extracts were filtered (0.22 µm, hydrophilic PTFE) and transferred to HPLC vials. Pooled quality control samples were produced by combining aliquots from each individual sample. Additionally, solvent and process blanks (the latter prepared by following the same extraction procedure but replacing juice by water) were used to identify any mass spectrometry feature originating from laboratory consumables (Broadhurst et al., 2018).</p><p><br></p><p>Quality control (QC) samples were used to ensure good data quality and assist in data analysis (Broadhurst et al., 2018). A pooled QC sample was prepared using an equal volume (100 μL) of each sample extract. Process blanks, used to identify features derived from laboratory consumables, were created using the same extraction procedure, with solvent (acidified methanol and water) replacing the sample. Similarly, a solvent blank was also evaluated during the analytical run to detect impurities present in the solvent.</p><p><br></p><p>Broadhurst D, Goodacre R, Reinke SN, Kuligowski J, Wilson ID, Lewis MR, Dunn WB (2018). Guidelines and considerations for the use of system suitability and quality control samples in mass spectrometry assays applied in untargeted clinical metabolomic studies. Metabolomics, 14(6), 1–17. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-018-1367-3.</p><p>Mercali, G. D., Rodrigues, E., Quiroz-Moreno, C. D., Sastry, S. K., &amp; Cooperstone, J. (2025). Impact of shear stress and moderate electric field on the global metabolic profile of a blended fruit and vegetable juice. Food Research International, 209(116299), 1–13. https://doi.org/https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodres.2025.116299</p>	<p>UHPLC was coupled to QTOF-MS with an ESI source that was operated in positive and negative ionization modes to enable comprehensive coverage of metabolites. Settings of the mass spectrometer (MS) were as follows: gas temperature of 200 °C, gas flow of 9 L/min, nebulizer of 4 bar, and mass range of 50-1500 m/z. Fragmentation data were collected using the mass spectrometer settings described above. Auto MS/MS scanning was conducted with collision energies between 11 and 30 eV, depending on the mass, width, and charge values.</p>	<p>Metabolites were annotated following the guidelines set by the Metabolomics Standard Initiative (Sumner et al., 2007). The identification of metabolites of interest was carried out using a multifaceted approach, using a combination of MS/MS fragmentation spectra, accurate mass, isotope analysis, database matches when available, and data available in the literature. Initially, sample spectra were compared against an internal standard library (Phenolic Lab Brazil), which includes the phenolic compounds standards. Subsequently, the comparison was conducted with public libraries, using MS/MS and MS spectral matching techniques.&nbsp;</p><p>Additionally, the annotation of the compounds was conducted manually, through the evaluation of data and fragmentation spectra described in the literature for compounds previously reported in the matrices used in the blended juice. This step allowed for the corroboration of the annotations obtained from the spectral libraries, as well as the identification of compounds for which no analytical standards were available</p><p><br></p><p>Sumner LW, Amberg A, Barrett D, Beale MH, Beger R, Daykin CA, Fan TWM, Fiehn O, Goodacre R, Griffin JL, Hankemeier T, Hardy N, Harnly, J, Higashi R, Kopka J, Lane AN, Lindon JC, Marriott P, Nicholls AW, Viant MR (2007). Proposed minimum reporting standards for chemical analysis. Metabolomics, 3(3), 211–221. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-007-0082-2</p>

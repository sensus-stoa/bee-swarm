Study Title	JEV-induced gut dysbiosis disrupts astrocyte-mediated blood-brain barrier integrity via the kynurenine-MMP17-AQP4 axis
Study Description	This study investigates the metabolic alterations associated with Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV)-induced gut dysbiosis and blood-brain barrier dysfunction in mice. LC-MS-based metabolomic profiling was performed on serum and brain tissue samples to characterize metabolic changes, with particular focus on the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway and its relationship with the MMP17-AQP4 axis involved in astrocyte-mediated blood-brain barrier injury.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Serum samples were collected from C57 mice for LC-MS-based untargeted metabolomic profiling. The collected serum samples were used for subsequent metabolite extraction and LC-MS analysis.Brain tissue samples were collected from C57 mice for LC-MS-based untargeted metabolomic profiling. The collected brain tissue samples were used for subsequent metabolite extraction and LC-MS analysis.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using a Vanquish ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) equipped with a Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, the flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and the injection volume was 4 µL. The gradient was programmed as follows: 0 min, 95% A; 1 min, 80% A; 3.0–4.5 min, 1% A; 4.6 min, returned to 95% A; and the total run time was 6 min.</p>	<p>Raw LC-MS data were converted to mzML format using ProteoWizard. Peak extraction, peak alignment, and retention-time correction were performed using XCMS. Features with a missing rate greater than 50% within sample groups were removed. Missing values were imputed using one-fifth of the minimum value when the blank-value proportion was greater than 50%, whereas K-nearest-neighbor (KNN) imputation was applied when the blank-value proportion was below 50%. Peak areas were subsequently corrected using support vector regression (SVR). Metabolite features with an identification composite score ≥ 0.5 and a coefficient of variation (CV) &lt; 0.3 in quality-control samples were retained for subsequent analysis.</p>	<p>Metabolites were extracted from serum or brain tissue samples prior to LC-MS analysis. Detailed metabolite extraction and sample preparation procedures will be provided according to the experimental protocol.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Data were acquired separately in positive and negative ionization modes using full-scan MS combined with data-dependent MS/MS acquisition with dynamic exclusion. The scan range was m/z 75–1000 for both MS1 and MS2. The MS1 and MS2 resolutions were 35,000 and 17,500, respectively. The spray voltage was 3.5 kV in positive ion mode and 3.2 kV in negative ion mode. The sheath gas and auxiliary gas settings were 30 and 5 arbitrary units, respectively. The ion transfer tube temperature was 320 °C and the vaporizer temperature was 300 °C. Normalized collision energies of 30, 40 and 50 were used. The AGC targets were 1 × 10^6 for MS1 and 2 × 10^5 for MS2.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification and annotation were performed by matching the processed LC-MS features against an in-house metabolite database, integrated public metabolite databases, and prediction databases. Features were evaluated using a composite identification score, and metabolites with an identification score ≥ 0.5 and a coefficient of variation (CV) &lt; 0.3 in quality-control samples were retained. Metabolites were annotated based on the available chromatographic and mass spectrometric information, including m/z, retention time, and MS/MS information where available.</p>

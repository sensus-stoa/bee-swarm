Study Title	ZEB1 Maintains Energy Metabolism to Sustain Cardiac Function Through Synergistic Regulation with TEAD1
Study Description	Metabolic reprogramming plays a critical role in heart failure (HF), but its regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1), a transcription factor initially identified as a regulator of epithelial-mesenchymal transition, has recently been implicated in metabolic regulation. Here, we show that ZEB1 expression is reduced in failing cardiomyocytes. Cardiomyocyte-specific deletion of Zeb1 leads to progressive HF with impaired glycolysis and fatty acid metabolism. Mechanistically, ZEB1 interacts with TEA domain transcription factor 1 (TEAD1) to activate metabolic genes, including pyruvate kinase muscle isozyme 1 (PKM1) and acetyl-CoA acyltransferase 2 (ACAA2), which regulate glycolysis and fatty acid β-oxidation, respectively. Loss of Acaa2 aggravates cardiac hypertrophy and stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Acetate supplementation restores cardiac function in Zeb1-deficient mice, whereas ZEB1 overexpression improves pressure overload- and doxorubicin-induced HF. These findings identify the ZEB1-TEAD1 axis as a regulator of cardiac metabolic homeostasis and a potential therapeutic target for HF.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Heart tissues were collected from cardiac-specific Zeb1 knockout (Zeb1 cKO) mice (n = 9) and Zeb1 fl/fl littermate controls (n = 6). Following collection, heart tissues were placed in individually labeled sample containers using clean laboratory instruments to minimize cross-contamination between samples. Tissues were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after collection and subsequently stored at -80°C until metabolomic sample preparation. No chemical stabilizers or preservatives were added during tissue storage. Sample identity and genotype were documented throughout collection, storage, and subsequent processing. Samples were maintained under consistent frozen-storage conditions prior to metabolite extraction.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using a SCIEX ExionLC AD UPLC system with two complementary chromatographic methods. For reversed-phase separation in both positive and negative ion modes, a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) was used. Mobile phase A consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. The gradient program was 0.0 min, 5% B; 2.0 min, 20% B; 5.0 min, 60% B; 6.0 min, 99% B; 7.5 min, 99% B; 7.6 min, 5% B; and 10.0 min, 5% B. For HILIC separation in negative ion mode, a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH HILIC column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) was used. Mobile phase A consisted of 80% acetonitrile, 10% water, and 10% methanol containing 20 mM ammonium formate, adjusted to pH 10.6 with ammonia, whereas mobile phase B consisted of 40% acetonitrile and 60% water containing 20 mM ammonium formate, adjusted to pH 10.6 with ammonia. The HILIC gradient program was 0.0 min, 5% B; 2.0 min, 20% B; 3.5 min, 70% B; 6.5 min, 95% B; 7.5 min, 95% B; 7.6 min, 5% B; and 10.0 min, 5% B. For both chromatographic methods, the flow rate was 0.4 mL/min and the column temperature was maintained at 40°C. Eluted metabolites were subsequently introduced into the mass spectrometer by electrospray ionization for MS/MS analysis.</p>	<p>Raw LC-MS/MS data were acquired using Analyst software (version 1.6.3). MRM chromatographic peaks were integrated and processed using MultiQuant software. Peak areas corresponding to individual metabolite-specific MRM transitions were extracted and used for relative metabolite quantification. Quality-control samples were used to monitor analytical stability and reproducibility. The resulting processed data matrix contained metabolite peak-area values for each biological sample and pooled QC sample.</p>	<p>Frozen heart tissues were homogenized using a tissue grinder at 30 Hz for 20 s. Subsequently, 400 μL of 70% methanol containing internal standards was added for metabolite extraction. Samples were vortexed/shaken at 2500 rpm for 5 min and incubated on ice for 15 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, 300 μL of the supernatant was collected and stored at -20°C for 30 min. Samples were then centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 3 min at 4°C. Finally, 200 μL of the resulting supernatant was transferred to autosampler vials for UPLC-MS/MS analysis. Pooled quality-control (QC) samples were prepared and analyzed to monitor analytical stability and reproducibility throughout the metabolomic analysis.</p>	<p>Metabolomic analysis was performed using an ABI SCIEX 6500 QTRAP LC-MS/MS system equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Metabolites separated by reversed-phase HSS T3 chromatography were analyzed in both positive and negative ion modes, whereas metabolites separated by HILIC chromatography were analyzed in negative ion mode. Targeted metabolite detection and relative quantification were performed using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Precursor ions were selected in Q1, subjected to collision-induced dissociation, and characteristic product ions were monitored in Q3. Declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) were optimized individually for metabolite-specific MRM transitions rather than applying uniform values to all metabolites. ESI source parameters, including ion spray voltage, source temperature, curtain gas, ion source gas 1, and ion source gas 2, were optimized separately for positive- and negative-ion acquisition to achieve stable electrospray ionization and reproducible metabolite detection. Data were acquired using Analyst software (version 1.6.3). Metabolite identification was based on chromatographic retention time and characteristic precursor/product ion transitions by comparison with an in-house metabolite database. MRM chromatographic peaks were integrated and processed using MultiQuant software, and integrated peak areas were used for relative metabolite quantification.</p>	<p>Metabolites were identified and annotated based on chromatographic retention time and characteristic mass-spectrometric information, including precursor/product ion transitions, by comparison with an in-house metabolite database. Metabolite annotation was further supported, where available, by public metabolite databases including HMDB, KEGG, PubChem and ChEBI. For quantitative analysis, characteristic precursor-to-product ion transitions were monitored using MRM, and chromatographic peak areas were integrated using MultiQuant software.</p>

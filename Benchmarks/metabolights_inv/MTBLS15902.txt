Study Title	Dolphin semen metabolome characterization and relationships with sperm quality
Study Description	<p><strong>Introduction</strong></p><p>Identification and quantification of metabolites in semen can augment assisted reproductive technologies by indicating reproductive health and fertility. Species-specific semen metabolites associated with improved semen quality remain largely unknown in exotic species due to limited sample availability.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Objectives</strong></p><p>Study objectives were to establish metabolome profiles of common bottlenose dolphin (<em>Tursiops truncatus</em>) semen and seminal plasma and identify metabolites associated with semen quality.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Methods</strong></p><p>Semen was collected from sexually mature common bottlenose dolphins (aged 17–46 years) trained to perform voluntary semen collection behavior. One to five semen samples were collected from each of four dolphins in managed care, resulting in 12 ejaculates for metabolomic analyses. Semen quality parameters were measured including volume, pH, and sperm concentration, motility, kinematics, and viability. Metabolites in whole semen and sperm-free seminal plasma were analyzed using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid mass spectrometry. Metabolome profile variability was assessed using principal component analyses with permutational multivariate analysis of variance and volcano plots with t-tests. Metabolomic pathway analyses were conducted using a species-specific systems biology markup language (SBML) metabolite model. Correlations between semen quality parameters and normalized metabolite peak areas were assessed using repeated-measures correlation.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Results</strong></p><p>An untargeted analysis identified 2,198 metabolites in dolphin semen and seminal plasma. Biological matrix, individual variation, and collection date contributed to differences in metabolome profiles. The relative abundances of 353 metabolites differed significantly between semen and seminal plasma (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05). Targeted analysis detected eight hormones in semen and/or seminal plasma. Pathway enrichment analysis indicated inter-individual variation including enriched nucleotide metabolism, protein degradation, and transport reaction pathways. Two individuals exhibited enriched inflammatory, reactive oxygen species detoxification, and antioxidant metabolism pathways, suggesting oxidative stress. A significant correlation (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0005) occurred between 98 distinct metabolites and semen quality parameters.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Conclusion</strong></p><p>This study provides the first semen metabolome profile for any marine mammal species and identifies novel metabolomic biomarkers of dolphin semen quality.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Semen was collected from four sexually mature common bottlenose dolphins in managed care previously trained in voluntary semen collection using manual stimulation. Dolphins were trained to invert their bodies and hold their ventrum above the water’s surface where trainers induced erection using tactile stimulation. The penis tip was rinsed with a buffered solution upon full erection (225-245 mOsm and pH 7.3-7.4; O’Brien &amp; Robeck, 2006). The penis tip was directed into a sterile plastic bag for semen collection. A total of 13 semen samples were collected over a six-week period with one to three semen samples collected from each male. Semen samples were collected around the same time daily with a maximum of one sample collected per dolphin per day. Semen samples were maintained in -80ºC storage for 130-170 days. Samples were removed from -80ºC storage, held at -20ºC for ~24 hours, then held at 4ºC for ~24 hours to gradually temperature-adjust semen prior to processing.</p>	<p>Semen and seminal plasma analytes were assessed with an ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). An ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 reversed-phase column (130 Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm; Waters™ Corporation, Milford, MA) was used. The mobile phases of the analysis employed UHPLC-grade Milli-Q water with 0.1% formic acid (eluent A) and UHPLC-grade acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (eluent B). Analyte separation occurred at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, with a 7 min re-equilibration period between samples. The mobile phase gradient used 5% eluent A and 95% eluent B from 0 to 2 min, ramped to 65% B for 18 min, ramped to 100% B for 1 min, and held at 100% B for 3 min. . The UHPLC used an injection volume of 5 µL. All negative-mode analyses used a locking solution (85% CH3CN, 15% H2O, 0.08% NH4OH, 0.001% negative-locking solution) controlled by an external pump (Dionex AXP-MS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) at a flow rate of 0.1 mL/min.</p>	<p>Untargeted metabolite identification occurred using Compound Discoverer (v3.3) software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data normalization was conducted via the Systematic Error Removal using Random Forest (SERRF) algorithm to attenuate instrumental drift, inter-run discrepancies, and systematic technical biases. Intra- and inter-day precision were monitored through the injection of pooled quality control (QC) samples at intervals of 10 samples. SERRF employs a random forest architecture to capture and rectify complex, correlated systematic errors utilizing the variance derived from these QC injections. In alignment with our prior findings, this normalization effectively constrained technical variation, yielding an average relative standard deviation (RSD) of 5% and facilitating robust analytical reproducibility. Missing values were subsequently imputed using the post-alignment Fill Gaps function within Compound Discoverer software, applying a mass tolerance of 5 ppm and a signal-to-noise threshold of 1.5. Prior to further statistical evaluation, metabolites exhibiting missing values in 25% or more of the samples were discarded.</p>	<p>After thawing, all tubes were gently inverted three times and three 1 mL semen subsamples were collected. All tubes were then vortexed for 10 sec to resuspend sperm and a second set of three 1 mL semen subsamples were collected. The first set of 1 mL subsamples were centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min at 500 g to separate sperm from seminal plasma, yielding sperm-free seminal plasma for analyte analysis. Seminal plasma was visually inspected to verify the absence of sperm. The second set of 1 mL subsamples were not centrifuged and were used for whole semen analysis. Semen samples were processed in a randomized order. Aliquots of 400 µL pre-chilled 70:30 (v/v) acetonitrile: water (LC-MS Optima grade, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) extraction solvent were combined with either 200 µL semen in lysing matrix tubes (1.4 mm Zirconium Beads, OPS Diagnostics, Lebanon, NJ) for semen analyte extraction or 200 µL sperm-free seminal plasma in microcentrifuge tubes for seminal plasma analyte extraction. Aliquots of 600 µL of extraction solvent were added to three lysing matrix tubes and three microcentrifuge tubes, which served as blank controls for each treatment. All tubes were vortexed at 3,200 g (Fixed Speed Vortex Mixer, VWR®, Radnor, PA) for 10 sec. Sperm in lysing matrix tubes were homogenized in a bead beater (Precellys® Evolution, Bertin Technologies, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France) for 60 sec, with 30 sec intervals and a 30 sec rest between cycles, at a speed of 5,000 g. After homogenization, lysing matrix tubes were vortexed for 10 sec and all tubes were sonicated for 20 min at 30ºC (Branson M2800, Branson Ultrasonics™, Brookfield, CT). All tubes were centrifuged (Centrifuge 5424 R, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at 4ºC at 12,000 g for 10 min, and 350 µL supernatant was transferred to pre-chilled high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) vials. Fresh extraction solvent was added to the original lysing matrix and microcentrifuge tubes, and the homogenization, vortexing, sonicating, and centrifuging steps described above were repeated. An additional 350 µL aliquot of supernatant was transferred to the HPLC vials to give a final volume of 700 µL extracted solvent. Semen samples were diluted with chilled extraction solvent to a final concentration of 96 x 106 sperm/mL. Analytes were extracted and analyzed in triplicate for each semen sample. Aliquots of 20 uL of each sample were combined to create a pooled quality control. Blank controls were created for semen and seminal plasma using the same respective extraction procedures without the samples. All extracts were stored at -20ºC until instrumental analysis.</p>	<p>An Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) was used. The Orbitrap full scan specified a mass range of 85-700 m/z. MS2 fragmentation scans were collected using the Quadrupole (0.7 m/z isolation window) with a stepped-energy collision-induced dissociation (CID) method. All samples and blank controls were run in positive and negative modes. The pooled quality control was run every ten samples in both modes to quantify mass spectrometer performance over time.</p>	<p>After data normalization, Compound Discoverer (v3.3) software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to generate putative molecular compositions for each metabolite using retention time, accurate mass measurements (mass error &lt; 2 ppm), peak signal intensity (&gt; 5,000), and MS2 fragmentation spectra. Detected features were matched against in-house mass databases generated in mzVault and external databases, including mzCloud (Thermo Fisher Scientific), Human Metabolome Database (HMDB; Wishart et al., 2007), Metabolika (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and ChemSpider (Pence &amp; Williams, 2010). Each metabolite annotation was manually reviewed. Positive identifications were confirmed only when at least three MS2 fragment ions matched the fragmentation pattern of a corresponding pure analytical standard. Metabolite classes were assigned for compounds with significant differential expression between matrices and individuals using HMDB (Wishart et al., 2007), PubChem (Kim et al., 2025), LIPID MAPS (Liebisch et al., 2020), ClassyFire (Feunang et al., 2016), and ChEBI (Degtyarenko et al., 2008) databases. Any compounds not listed in the databases were labeled “Unknown.” A list of targeted hormone standards was selected based on the results of untargeted analysis and hormones identified in the semen/seminal plasma of terrestrial artiodactyls and humans in published literature. Hormone standards were purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI), Cerilliant (Round Rock, TX), Fisher Scientific (Hampton, NH), LGC Standards (Manchester, NH), and Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) and possessed ≥ 95% purity. Non-labeled standards for the 27 selected hormones were dissolved in methanol and diluted to concentrations ranging from 0.0001 to 500 ppb to generate calibration curves for quantification. Extraction performance was evaluated using three replicates of a single semen sample, a single seminal plasma sample, and three blanks which were spiked with known concentrations of isotopically labeled standards to achieve a final concentration of 20 ppb and processed according to the analyte extraction protocol (Section 2.4). Recovery was calculated as the percentage of isotopically labeled hormone recovered following extraction. Precision was determined as the relative standard deviation of replicate recoveries. Data were processed using Skyline software (MacCoss Lab, University of Washington). Metabolite limits of detection (LOD) were determined based on reference standard retention times, peak signal intensities (&gt; 1,000) with S/N &gt;3, and mass errors (&lt; 3 ppm for positive mode without locking solution, &lt; 2 ppm for negative mode with locking solution). Peaks with &lt; 5 scans were designated as noise and excluded from subsequent analyses. Metabolite limits of quantification (LOQ) were determined based on LOD requirements and matching MS2 fragmentation to non-labeled analytical standards.</p>

Study Title	Fecal metabolic changes in mice before Salmonella infection
Study Description	With the monophasic variant of Salmonella Typhimurium (S. 4,[5],12:i-) emerging as one of the most prevalent serotypes, multidrug resistance has become an increasingly serious clinical challenge. Conventional antibiotic therapy is further constrained by adverse effects, including resistance selection, disruption of the gut microbiota, and organ toxicity. These limitations have driven growing interest in host-directed therapies (HDT) that strengthen intrinsic immune defenses and promote tissue repair to achieve sustained and balanced infection control. Here, to identify key metabolites that enhance host immunity against Salmonella infection, we compared gut metabolite profiles between non-diarrheal and diarrheal mice following S. 4,[5],12:i challenge. It revealed that diarrheal mice exhibited significantly elevated levels of 1-methylhistidine (1-MH) compared with non-diarrheal mice, indicating a marked alteration in histidine metabolism. Consistently, transcriptomic profiling together with targeted energy metabolomics indicated that histidine broadly activates immune-related programs and anti-infective responses. Mechanistically, histidine promotes M1 macrophage polarization through PI3K/AKT/mTOR/HIF-1α-dependent glycolytic reprogramming, thereby enhancing macrophage activation and controlling Salmonella infection. Collectively, our findings identify L-histidine supplementation as a promising host-directed therapeutic strategy against drug-resistant Salmonella infection.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Fecal samples were collected from 12 male BALB/c mice before infection with 1000000000 CFU of Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:-. Based on the subsequent development of diarrheal symptoms following infection, the mice were divided into diarrheal and non-diarrheal groups. The pre-infection fecal samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently subjected to untargeted metabolomic analysis by Panomix Biotech Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China).</p>	<p>LC–MS analysis was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X Fourier transform mass spectrometer (UHPLC–Q Exactive HF-X; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Mobile phase A consisted of 95% water and 5% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid, while mobile phase B consisted of 47.5% acetonitrile, 47.5% isopropanol, and 5% water containing 0.1% formic acid. The injection volume was 3 μL, and the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C.</p>	<p>Raw LC–MS data files were directly imported into Progenesis QI v3.0 (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) without prior format conversion.</p>	<p>The collection and analysis of samples were adhered to the manufacturer’s standard protocol. Metabolites were extracted from samples using 80% methanol, followed by vortexing, centrifugation, and lyophilization.</p>	<p>LC–MS analysis was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X Fourier transform mass spectrometer (UHPLC–Q Exactive HF-X; Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Data were acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes over an m/z range of 70–1050. The sheath gas and auxiliary gas flow rates were set to 50 and 13 arbitrary units (arb), respectively. The heater temperature was maintained at 425 °C, and the capillary temperature was set to 325 °C. The spray voltages were 3.5 kV in positive ion mode and 3.5 kV in negative ion mode. The S-Lens RF level was set to 50. The normalized collision energies (NCEs) were set to 20, 40, and 60%. The mass resolution was set to 60,000 for full MS scans and 7,500 for MS/MS scans.</p>	<p>Raw data were imported into Progenesis QI v3.0 (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) for data preprocessing, including baseline filtering, peak detection, peak integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment. A data matrix containing information on retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and peak intensity was subsequently generated. Metabolite annotation was performed in Progenesis QI by matching the MS and MS/MS spectral information of detected features against metabolite databases. The mass tolerance for MS matching was set to &lt;10 ppm, and metabolite identification was further evaluated based on MS/MS spectral matching scores. The databases used for metabolite annotation primarily included the Human Metabolome Database (http://www.hmdb.ca/), METLIN (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and an in-house metabolite database.</p>

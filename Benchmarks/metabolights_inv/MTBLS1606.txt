Study Identifier	MTBLS1606
Study Title	Integrated genomic and metabolomic profiling of ISC1, an emerging Leishmania donovani population in the Indian subcontinent
Study Description	<p><em>Leishmania donovani</em> is the responsible agent for visceral leishmaniasis (VL) in the Indian subcontinent (ISC). The disease is lethal without treatment and causes 0.2 to 0.4 million cases each year. Recently, reports of VL in Nepalese hilly districts have increased as well as VL cases caused by <em>L. donovani</em> from the ISC1 genetic group, a new and emerging genotype. In this study, we perform for the first time an integrated, untargeted genomics and metabolomics approach to characterize ISC1, in comparison with the Core Group (CG), main population that drove the most recent outbreak of VL in the ISC. We show that the ISC1 population is very different from the CG, both at genome and metabolome levels. The genomic differences include SNPs, CNV and small indels in genes coding for known virulence factors, immunogens and surface proteins. Both genomic and metabolic approaches highlighted dissimilarities related to membrane lipids, the nucleotide salvage pathway and the urea cycle in ISC1 versus CG. Many of these pathways and molecules are important for the interaction with the host/extracellular environment. Altogether, our data predict major functional differences in ISC1 versus CG parasites, including virulence. Therefore, particular attention is required to monitor the fate of this emerging ISC1 population in the ISC, especially in a post-VL elimination context.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Linked cross omic data sets:</strong></p><p>Raw sequencing data associated with this study are available in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA): accession number <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/browser/view/PRJNA419531' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>PRJNA419531</a>.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>9 <em>Leishmania donovani</em> cloned from 7 Nepalese and 2 Indian clinical isolates were analyzed in this study (see <strong>Table S1.1</strong> in the paper associated with this study). Among these are 5 Indian subcontinent <strong>ISC1 strains</strong> (<strong>BPK026/0</strong>, <strong>BPK604/0</strong>, <strong>BPK612/0</strong>, <strong>BPK519/12</strong>, <strong>BPK406/6</strong>) and 2 Nepalese (<strong>BPK275/0</strong>, <strong>BPK282/0</strong>) as well as 2 Indian (<strong>BHU568/0</strong>, <strong>BHU573/0</strong>) strains from the <strong>Core Group (CG)</strong>. Promastigotes of all strains were grown in modified Eagle's medium (Invitrogen) supplemented with 20% (v/v) heat inactivated fetal calf serum (PAA Laboratories GmbH, Linz, Austria) pH 7.5 at 26 °C. At each passage, all parasite lines were inoculated to a starting concentration of 5 x 10^5 parasites/ml and growth was followed for 2 passages enabling synchronized sampling of the different cultures and minimizing the effect of differences in growth rate between strains. For each strain, 4 growth replicates were cultured for metabolomics analysis and 1 for genome sequencing.</p>	<p>Metabolites were extracted in stationary growth phase according to a previously published method<strong>[1]</strong>. Initially, the parasites were quenched for 20 s in their culture flasks to 0 °C in a bath containing a mixture of dry ice/ethanol. Secondly, a volume containing 4 x 10^7 parasites was aliquoted, pelleted and washed 3 times with 1 ml of ice-cold (0 °C) phosphate buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4; Invitrogen) &amp; centrifugation (20,800 x g, 0 °C, 3 min). Metabolites were then extracted with 200 µl chloroform/methanol/water 20/60/20 (v/v/v) during 1 h in a Thermomixer (1400 rpm, 4 °C; Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany). Finally, the metabolite extract was separated from cell debris by centrifugation (20,800 x g, 0 °C, 3 min) and vials with the supernatant(metabole extract) stored at -80 °C.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> t'Kindt R, Scheltema RA, Jankevics A, Brunker K, Rijal S, Dujardin JC, Breitling R, Watson DG, Coombs GH, Decuypere S. Metabolomics to unveil and understand phenotypic diversity between pathogen populations. PLoS Negl Trop Dis. 2010 Nov 30;4(11):e904. doi:10.1371/journal.pntd.0000904. PMID:21152055.</p>	<p>Samples were analyzed with LC-MS using an Orbitrap Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) coupled to a 2.1 mm ZIC-HILIC column at <strong>Glasgow Polyomics</strong> (University of Glasgow, Scotland) (Sequant), exactly as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, elution of the guard column (20 mm by 2.1 mm inside diameter [i.d.], 5 μm; Merck/SeQuant) and Zic-HILIC high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) column (150 mm by 2.1 mm i.d., 3.5 μm, 100 Å; Merck/SeQuant) was carried out with a gradient of <strong>(A)</strong> 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile and <strong>(B)</strong> 0.1% formic acid in water. The flow rate was 100 μl/min, with an injection volume of 10 μl. Gradient elution was performed starting with 80% solvent <strong>A</strong>. Within a 6 min time interval, solvent <strong>B</strong> was increased to 40% and maintained for 12 min, followed by an increase to 90% within 4 min. This was maintained for 2 min, after which the system returned to the initial solvent composition in 2 min. The whole system was allowed to re-equilibrate under these conditions for 14 min.</p><p><br></p><p>Both negative and positive ion modes were run in parallel with rapid polarity switching. Several quality control samples were run at the start, middle and end of the LC-MS run to aid accurate metabolite identification and verify LC-MS stability<strong>[2]</strong>. These included: <strong>1)</strong> Authentic standard mixes containing in total 217 metabolites (50-400 Da) representing a wide array of metabolic classes and pathways. <strong>2)</strong> An amino acid standard mix (Sigma Product No. A9906). <strong>3)</strong> Serial dilutions (Undiluted, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/16) of a pooled sample of all extracts to filter out spurious signals that do not follow the dilution trend<strong>[3]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Berg M, García-Hernández R, Cuypers B, Vanaerschot M, Manzano JI, Poveda JA, Ferragut JA, Castanys S, Dujardin JC, Gamarro F. Experimental resistance to drug combinations in Leishmania donovani: metabolic and phenotypic adaptations. Antimicrob Agents Chemother. 2015 Apr;59(4):2242-55. doi:10.1128/AAC.04231-14. PMID:25645828.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Berg M, Vanaerschot M, Jankevics A, Cuypers B, Breitling R, Dujardin JC. LC-MS metabolomics from study design to data-analysis - using a versatile pathogen as a test case. Comput Struct Biotechnol J. 2013 Feb 6;4:e201301002. doi:10.5936/csbj.201301002. PMID:24688684.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Jankevics A, Merlo ME, de Vries M, Vonk RJ, Takano E, Breitling R. Separating the wheat from the chaff: a prioritisation pipeline for the analysis of metabolomics datasets. Metabolomics. 2012 Jun;8(Suppl 1):29-36. doi:10.1007/s11306-011-0341-0. PMID:22593722.</p>	<p>High-resolution mass measurements were obtained with an Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) at the <strong>Scottish Metabolomics Facility</strong> (<strong>Glasgow Polyomics</strong>, University of Glasgow, Glasgow, Scotland) using fast polarity switching for simultaneous measurements in positive and negative ion electrospray mode. The electrospray ionization (ESI) source voltage was optimized to 4.5 kV and 3.5 kV (for the positive and negative modes, respectively), and capillary voltage was set to 40 V and 30 V (for the positive and negative modes, respectively). The source temperature was set to 275 °C, and the sheath and auxiliary gas flow rates were set to 40 and 5 machine-specific units, respectively. Full-scan spectra were acquired over an m/z-range of 70 to 1,400 Da, with the mass resolution set to 50,000 full width at half maximum (FWHM). All spectra were collected in continuous single mass spectrometry (MS) mode.</p>	<p>Data analysis was performed using the <strong>XCMS 1.42.0[1]</strong> and <strong>mzMatch 1.0.1[2]</strong> packages in <strong>R</strong> as described previously<strong>[3]</strong>. In summary, <strong>mzXML</strong> files were first subjected to retention time alignment using <strong>ObiWarp[4]</strong> and peak detection with the <strong>centWave algorithm[5]</strong>. Corresponding peaks from the 4 biological replicates were subsequently combined and filtered on a maximum allowed reproducibility standard deviation (RSD) of 0.5. Peaksets from all samples were then combined and filtered further, requiring for each peak a minimal scaled <strong>CoDA-DW quality value</strong> of 0.8 (<strong>mzMatch</strong> noisefilter), detection in at least 3 out of 4 replicates and a minimal peak intensity of 3000. The <strong>gapfiller tool</strong> was used to fetch peaks from the <strong>mzXML</strong> files that were missing in 1 or more samples and derivative peaks were annotated<strong>[6]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Smith CA, Want EJ, O'Maille G, Abagyan R, Siuzdak G. XCMS: processing mass spectrometry data for metabolite profiling using nonlinear peak alignment, matching, and identification. Anal Chem. 2006 Feb 1;78(3):779-87. doi:10.1021/ac051437y. PMID:16448051.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Scheltema RA, Jankevics A, Jansen RC, Swertz MA, Breitling R. PeakML/mzMatch: a file format, Java library, R library, and tool-chain for mass spectrometry data analysis. Anal Chem. 2011 Apr 1;83(7):2786-93. doi:10.1021/ac2000994. PMID:21401061.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Berg M, Vanaerschot M, Jankevics A, Cuypers B, Maes I, Mukherjee S, Khanal B, Rijal S, Roy S, Opperdoes F, Breitling R, Dujardin JC. Metabolic adaptations of Leishmania donovani in relation to differentiation, drug resistance, and drug pressure. Mol Microbiol. 2013 Oct;90(2):428-42. doi:10.1111/mmi.12374. PMID:24020363.</p><p><strong>[4]</strong> Prince JT, Marcotte EM. Chromatographic alignment of ESI-LC-MS proteomics data sets by ordered bijective interpolated warping. Anal Chem. 2006 Sep 1;78(17):6140-52. doi:10.1021/ac0605344. PMID:16944896.</p><p><strong>[5]</strong> Tautenhahn R, Böttcher C, Neumann S. Highly sensitive feature detection for high resolution LC/MS. BMC Bioinformatics. 2008 Nov 28;9:504. doi:10.1186/1471-2105-9-504. PMID:19040729.</p><p><strong>[6]</strong> Scheltema R, Decuypere S, Dujardin J, Watson D, Jansen R, Breitling R. Simple data-reduction method for high-resolution LC-MS data in metabolomics. Bioanalysis. 2009 Dec;1(9):1551-7. doi:10.4155/bio.09.146. PMID:21083103.</p>	<p>Peaks were then putatively identified allowing 2 ppm of mass deviation using sequentially the <strong>LeishCyc database[1]</strong>, <strong>LipidMAPS[2]</strong>, <strong>KEGG[3]</strong>, the peptide database included in <strong>mzMatch</strong> and the <strong>Human Metabolome Database[4]</strong>. Each time, only unmatched peaks were aligned against the next database, reducing the number of potential identifications to the most likely candidates<strong>[5]</strong>. Peaks of which the intensity did not follow the dilution pattern in the pooled sample dilution series (<strong>Pearson correlation</strong> &gt;0.8 or <strong>p-value</strong> &gt;.05) were removed from the analysis with the <strong>mzMatch Dilution Trend Filter tool[6]</strong>. The remaining peaks were normalized for total ion count and visually verified before exporting them to a <strong>csv</strong> file containing all peak heights, retention times and relevant database annotations.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Doyle MA, MacRae JI, De Souza DP, Saunders EC, McConville MJ, Likić VA. LeishCyc: a biochemical pathways database for Leishmania major. BMC Syst Biol. 2009 Jun 5;3:57. doi:10.1186/1752-0509-3-57. PMID:19497128.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Fahy E, Sud M, Cotter D, Subramaniam S. LIPID MAPS online tools for lipid research. Nucleic Acids Res. 2007 Jul;35(Web Server issue):W606-12. doi:10.1093/nar/gkm324. PMID:17584797.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Kanehisa M, Furumichi M, Tanabe M, Sato Y, Morishima K. KEGG: new perspectives on genomes, pathways, diseases and drugs. Nucleic Acids Res. 2017 Jan 4;45(D1):D353-D361. doi:10.1093/nar/gkw1092. PMID:27899662.</p><p><strong>[4]</strong> Wishart DS, Tzur D, Knox C, Eisner R, Guo AC, Young N, Cheng D, Jewell K, Arndt D et al. HMDB: the Human Metabolome Database. Nucleic Acids Res. 2007 Jan;35(Database issue):D521-6. doi:10.1093/nar/gkl923. PMID:17202168.</p><p><strong>[5]</strong> Scalbert A, Brennan L, Fiehn O, Hankemeier T, Kristal BS, van Ommen B, Pujos-Guillot E, Verheij E, Wishart D, Wopereis S. Mass-spectrometry-based metabolomics: limitations and recommendations for future progress with particular focus on nutrition research. Metabolomics. 2009 Dec;5(4):435-458. doi:10.1007/s11306-009-0168-0. PMID:20046865.</p><p><strong>[6]</strong> Jankevics A, Merlo ME, de Vries M, Vonk RJ, Takano E, Breitling R.

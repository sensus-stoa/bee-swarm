Study Identifier	MTBLS16117
Study Title	Pyruvate availability governs adaptive antibiotic resistance in Staphylococcus aureus
Study Description	Adaptive resistance enables bacteria to survive antibiotic stress before stable resistance emerges, yet the metabolic requirements that permit this transitional state remain unclear. Using vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (VISA) evolution as a model, we identify pyruvate availability as metabolic checkpoint for adaptive antibiotic resistance. Adaptive laboratory evolution shows that SceD, but not the related lytic transglycosylase IsaA, is strongly induced and supports efficient progression toward the VISA phenotype. Genomic, transcriptomic, and [U-¹³C] glucose-tracing analyses show that sceD deletion redirects adaptive evolutionary trajectories and exposes a bottleneck at the phosphoenolpyruvate-to-pyruvate node while leaving upstream glycolysis largely intact. This bottleneck limits pyruvate-derived anaplerosis, nucleotide metabolism, UDP-GlcNAc biosynthesis, and energy balance. Rescue experiments show that pyruvate and pyruvate-derived anaplerotic intermediates, rather than upstream carbon sources, peptidoglycan turnover products or nucleobases, restore adaptive capacity. Genetic disruption of pyk nearly abolishes adaptive evolution, while exogenous pyruvate restores or enhances adaptation. SceD interacts with pyruvate kinase (Pyk) and modulates Pyk enzymatic activity, providing one route by which envelope stress is coupled to pyruvate metabolism. Pyruvate dependence extends across antibiotic classes, establishing pyruvate availability as a convergent metabolic requirement for adaptive resistance.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Overnight cultures of S. aureus strains (WT and Δ<em>sceD</em>) were prepared in BHI medium. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended to an OD600 of 0.01 in fresh chemically defined medium (CDM) supplemented with uniformly labeled [U-¹³C] glucose as the sole carbon source, as used for stable isotope-based glucose tracing analysis (46). Cultures were incubated at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm, and growth was monitored by measuring OD600 every 15 min. At late exponential phase (OD600 ≈ 1.0), cells were rapidly quenched and collected for metabolite extraction. Metabolites were extracted using cold methanol/chloroform and analyzed by UHPLC-HRMS (Ultimate 3000/Q Exactive, Thermo Scientific) on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column. Mass isotopomer distributions (MIDs) were derived from negative-mode HESI-MS data according to established principles for interpreting ¹³C metabolite labeling patterns</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters Corporation) for hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC). Mobile phase A consisted of 90% acetonitrile in water, and mobile phase B consisted of water; both contained 15 mM ammonium acetate at pH 9. The following linear gradient was used: 0–1 min, 10% B; 1–9 min, 10–25% B; 9–10.2 min, 25–50% B; 10.2–12 min, 50% B; 12–12.1 min, 50–10% B; and 12.1–14 min, 10% B. The flow rate was 0.30 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL.</p>	<p>Raw UHPLC-HRMS/MS data were inspected using Xcalibur software (version 4.0.27.19; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Chromatographic peaks corresponding to the target metabolites and their isotopologues were integrated, and the integration results were manually reviewed. The resulting peak areas were exported for subsequent isotope-labeling analysis. Natural isotope abundance correction was applied to the isotopologue peak areas using theoretical calculations. For each metabolite, the corrected area of each isotopologue was divided by the sum of the corrected areas of all its isotopologues and multiplied by 100 to obtain the mass isotopomer distribution (MID), expressed as a percentage.Please update this protocol description</p>	<p>Bacterial samples were mixed with internal standard, cold enzyme inhibitor, cold methanol, and zirconia beads, and homogenized by cryogenic grinding at 50 Hz for 1 min. After adding chloroform, the mixture was vortexed and centrifuged (14,000 rcf, 4 °C, 10 min). The upper aqueous phase was collected, gently dried under nitrogen, and reconstituted in 50 µL of 50% acetonitrile for instrumental analysis.'</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using a Q Exactive hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source operated in negative-ion mode. The spray voltage was 4,000 V. The capillary temperature and probe heater temperature were both set to 320 °C. The sheath gas and auxiliary gas flow settings were 35 and 10 arbitrary units, respectively, and the S-lens RF level was 50. Full-scan MS data were acquired at a resolving power of 70,000 FWHM at m/z 200, with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 3 × 10^6. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) was used to acquire MS/MS spectra from up to eight precursor ions per scan cycle. HCD collision energy settings of 15, 30, and 45 were used. MS/MS spectra were acquired at a resolving power of 17,500 FWHM, with an AGC target of 1 × 10^5.</p>	<p>The analysis focused on predefined target metabolites involved in central carbon and nucleotide sugar metabolism. Samples were analyzed by UHPLC-HRMS/MS using negative-ion full-scan MS and data-dependent MS/MS acquisition. Target metabolites were annotated based on their mass-to-charge ratios (m/z), retention times, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns. Chromatographic and mass spectral data were inspected using Xcalibur software (version 4.0.27.19; Thermo Fisher Scientific).</p>

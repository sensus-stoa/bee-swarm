Study Title	Fecal metabolomic alterations associated with sleep deprivation in mice
Study Description	This study investigates fecal metabolomic alterations associated with sleep deprivation in mice. Fecal samples were collected from control (CON), partial sleep deprivation (PSD), and sleep deprivation (SD) groups, with six biological replicates per group. Untargeted LC-MS metabolomics was performed in both positive and negative ionization modes to characterize changes in the fecal metabolome and identify metabolites associated with sleep deprivation.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Fecal samples were collected from mice in the control (CON), partial sleep deprivation (PSD), and sleep deprivation (SD) groups on November 23, 2023, after completion of the sleep-manipulation protocol. Six biological replicates were collected from each group, resulting in a total of 18 biological samples. Immediately after collection, fecal samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at 80°C until metabolite extraction and LC–MS analysis.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system coupled to a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Metabolites were separated on a C18 reversed-phase column using a gradient of acetonitrile and water containing 0.1% formic acid. LC–MS analysis was performed in both positive- and negative-ion modes by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).</p>	<p>Raw LC–MS data from positive- and negative-ion modes were processed separately using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA), using the same general processing workflow for both acquisition modes. Data processing included baseline filtering, peak detection and integration, retention-time correction, and peak alignment to generate a feature matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and peak intensity. Missing values were subsequently filtered and imputed, followed by signal normalization and logarithmic transformation. Quality-control performance was assessed using the relative standard deviation (RSD) of features in QC samples, and variables with QC RSD values greater than 30% were excluded from downstream differential quantitative analyses.</p>	<p>Approximately 50 mg of fecal material from each sample was used for metabolite extraction. Samples were homogenized in pre-cooled methanol to precipitate proteins and extract metabolites, followed by centrifugation, and the resulting supernatants were collected for LC–MS analysis. Internal standards were used for normalization and quality-control purposes. In addition to the 18 biological samples, three pooled quality-control (QC) samples were analyzed to evaluate analytical reproducibility and instrument stability.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection was performed using a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in both positive- and negative-ion acquisition modes to maximize metabolite coverage. The mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) scan range was 50–750. MS and MS/MS information was acquired for metabolite feature detection and subsequent annotation, and the instrument was calibrated regularly to maintain mass accuracy.</p>	<p>Metabolite annotation was performed using Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) by matching MS and MS/MS information against reference metabolite databases. The principal reference resources included the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), METLIN, additional public databases, and Majorbio's in-house metabolite database. Annotation incorporated accurate mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), retention time, molecular formula, adduct information, mass error, and MS/MS fragmentation information where available. Additional functional annotation was performed using KEGG Compound and KEGG Pathway databases, and HMDB classifications were used for chemical classification of annotated metabolites.</p>

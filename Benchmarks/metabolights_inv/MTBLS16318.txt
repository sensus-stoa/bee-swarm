Study Title	Study on the Remodeling of Bile Acid Metabolic Profile and Compensatory Adaptability of the Body under High-Altitude Hypoxic Environment
Study Description	"High-altitude hypoxic exposure is often accompanied by hepatobiliary metabolic disorders, but the direction of changes in bile acid metabolism under hypoxia, the consistency across molecular levels, and their evolution with exposure duration remain unclear. In this study, a hypobaric oxygen chamber was used to simulate a rat high-altitude hypoxia model, with three groups established: low-altitude control group (LAC), high-altitude acute hypoxia group (HAH), and high-altitude chronic hypoxia group (HCH). Targeted bile acid metabolomics analysis was performed on liver, serum, and urine, integrated with DIA proteomics of liver, kidney, and intestine as well as HepaRG cell transcriptomics to screen key molecules. The key molecules were validated at the mRNA and protein levels in rat liver and HepaRG cells using qPCR, Western blot, and immunofluorescence. The results showed that the bile acid profile separated regularly with the duration and severity of hypoxia, and KEGG pathway enrichment was concentrated in four consecutive pathways: cholesterol metabolism, primary bile acid biosynthesis, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, and bile secretion. Differential bile acids had the highest proportion in urine (50%), and the sulfated bile acids TCDCA-3S and CDCA-3S were positively correlated with AST, TBIL, and IBIL. Molecular validation showed that under hypoxia, FXR and SULT2A1 were consistently downregulated at both mRNA and protein levels in rat liver and HepaRG, suggesting that hypoxia mediates a dual imbalance of """"""""enhanced synthesis and impaired detoxification"""""""" through FXR downregulation; CYP7A1 showed cross-system inconsistency, with mRNA and protein upregulated in rat liver with exposure time (not significant in the acute phase, significantly upregulated in the chronic phase), while in HepaRG, mRNA was upregulated but protein decreased, suggesting the involvement of post-transcriptional regulation. This imbalance had clear temporal characteristics: in the acute phase, the main compensation was the shift of clearance pathways to the kidney, while in the chronic phase, it shifted to decompensation due to the accumulation of disinhibition effects and persistent impairment of detoxification enzymes. Sulfated conjugated bile acids were closely related to liver injury indicators, and changes in their key metabolic enzyme SULT2A1 showed cross-species conservation, making it a promising cross-species comparable biomarker of hypoxic liver injury; the application value of targeting the FXR–bile acid axis in the prevention and treatment of high-altitude-related diseases and risk monitoring warrants further validation."

Study Protocol Description	<p>The rats were randomly divided into three groups, low-altitude control group, high-altitude acute hypoxia group, and high-altitude chronic hypoxia group. After the final exposure, all rats were fasted for 12 h with free access to water, anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 20% ethyl carbamate (1 g/kg), and blood was collected via the abdominal aorta. After standing at room temperature for 30 min, serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min; liver, kidney, and intestinal tissues were rapidly isolated, and the left liver lobe, left kidney, and duodenum were collected, rinsed with normal saline, blotted dry with filter paper, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and transferred to a 80 °C freezer for storage; meanwhile, urine was collected by bladder puncture, aliquoted, and stored at 80 °C for later use.</p>	<p>The LC analysis was performed on EXion LC Liquid chromatography (AB SCIEX, USA). An ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1×100 mm, 1.7 μm) was used, the injection volume was 5 μL, the column temperature was 40 °C, mobile phase A was 0.01% formic acid aqueous solution and B was acetonitrile, the flow rate was 0.25 mL/min, and gradient elution was performed; the gradient elution conditions were 0–4 min, 25% B; 4–9 min, 25–30% B; 9–14 min, 30–36% B; 14–18 min, 36–38% B; 18–24 min, 38–50% B; 24–32 min, 50–75% B; 32–33 min, 75–90% B; 33–35.5 min, 90–25% B. The flow rate was 0.25 mL/min.</p>	<p>MS Convert software (Proteowizard, v3.0.8789) was applied to convert “.raw” format raw data to “.mzML” format, which would be used for downstream analysis.</p>	<p>Preparation of standard solutions&nbsp;</p><p>Weigh the bile acid standard and prepare it with methanol to make a final concentration of 1000 μg/mL mixed standard stock solution, then dilute the stock solution with 30% methanol, and dilute it to ten standard. All stock solutions and working standard solutions were stored at -20.</p><p>Sample preparation</p><p>Samples were extracted in 600 μL of methanol(-20), vortex for 60 s. Centrifuge at 12,000 rpm and 4 for 10 min, take 400 μL of the supernatant and concentrate it to dryness with a vacuum concentrator. Add 100 μL of 30% methanol to reconstitute. The supernatant was filtered through 0.22μm membrane, and the filtrate was added to the LC-MS bottle.</p><p>QC Sample Preparation Method</p><p>Take a portion of the supernatant from the sample to be tested, mix it with cal5 at a 1:1 volume ratio to make the QC sample, and run it for testing to determine whether the test data quality is acceptable.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric detection of metabolites was performed on AB6500+ (AB SCIEX, USA). Mass spectrometry conditions were as follows: electrospray ionization (ESI) negative ion mode, multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) scanning, ion source temperature 500 °C, ion source voltage 4500 V, collision gas 6 psi, curtain gas 30 psi, and both nebulizer gas and auxiliary gas 50 psi.</p>	<p>Metabolites were identified by targeted LC–MS/MS on a SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ system coupled to a SCIEX ExionLC AD chromatograph, using scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (sMRM). Identities were assigned by comparing retention times and MRM transition pairs (quantifier and qualifier ions) with authentic bile-acid reference standards analysed under identical conditions, and confirmed by qualifier/quantifier ion-ratio agreement (±20%) and co-elution with isotope-labelled internal standards. The reference database was an in-house library built from authentic standards (precursor/product ions, collision energies, and retention times); this transition list is provided as supplementary material. Peak integration and quantification were performed with SCIEX quantitative analysis software (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). Reported metabolites correspond to Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) level 1 (authenticated by reference standards).</p>

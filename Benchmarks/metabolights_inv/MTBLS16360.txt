Study Title	Research Progress on the Effects of Berberine on the Characteristics of Escherichia coli Outer Membrane Vesicles
Study Description	Berberine (BBR), a natural isoquinoline alkaloid extracted from Coptis chinensis and other medicinal plants, exerts broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against Gram-negative bacteria, yet its impact on bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) remains largely unexplored. This study aimed to investigate whether BBR treatment modulates the biophysical properties, protein cargo, and metabolic composition of OMVs secreted by Escherichia coli. Log-phase E. coli cells were exposed to 55 μg/mL BBR (dissolved in DMSO) for 18 h in TB medium, with DMSO-treated cells serving as the vehicle control (n = 6 biological replicates per group). OMVs were purified from cell-free culture supernatants by sequential ultracentrifugation (150,000 × g, 70 min, 4 ℃) following 0.22 μm filtration to remove intact bacteria and debris. The size distribution and polydispersity index (PDI) of purified OMVs were characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS), total protein concentration was quantified using the BCA assay, and vesicular morphology was verified by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Untargeted metabolomics based on liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) in both positive and negative ionization modes was performed to profile the metabolite cargo of OMVs. DLS demonstrated that BBR treatment shifted the OMV size distribution toward larger diameters (approximately 120–200 nm versus 60–140 nm in DMSO controls) and markedly elevated the PDI, indicating increased vesicle size heterogeneity. TEM confirmed intact spherical bilayered vesicles in both groups, with BBR-treated samples displaying a broader size range and occasional aggregation. BCA assays revealed that BBR exposure altered OMV protein yield relative to controls. LC-MS metabolomic data acquisition has been completed in both ionization modes; downstream multivariate and univariate analyses are ongoing to identify differentially enriched metabolites and perturbed metabolic pathways in OMVs upon BBR challenge. Collectively, these preliminary findings suggest that BBR perturbs E. coli membrane homeostasis and OMV secretion, offering a novel extracellular perspective on the antibacterial mechanisms of this clinical alkaloid. Keywords: berberine; Escherichia coli; outer membrane vesicles; metabolomics; dynamic light scattering; ultracentrifugation

Study Protocol Description	<p>Escherichia coli (E. coli) cells were cultured in LB medium at 37°C with orbital shaking at 200 rpm. When the culture reached logarithmic growth phase (OD600 ≈ 0.6), cells were treated with either 55 μg/mL berberine hydrochloride (BBR55 group, n=6) or an equal volume of DMSO solvent (control group, n=6). After treatment, bacterial culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to pellet cells, followed by filtration through a 0.45 μm filter to remove remaining cell debris. Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) were isolated from the cell-free supernatant by ultrafiltration and/or ultracentrifugation. The OMV pellets were washed with PBS and stored at -80°C until metabolite extraction.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters) using two UHPLC systems: Agilent 1290 Infinity LC and Thermo Vanquish UHPLC. Column temperature was maintained at 25°C, flow rate at 0.5 mL/min, injection volume at 2 μL. The mobile phase A was 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide in water, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The gradient elution program: 0–0.5 min 95% B; 0.5–7 min linear decrease to 65% B; 7–8 min to 40% B; 8–9 min hold at 40% B; 9–9.1 min back to 95% B; 9.1–12 min re-equilibration. Samples were kept at 4°C in the autosampler.</p>	<p>Raw MS data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard MSConvert. Peak picking, alignment, and retention time correction were performed using XCMS software (centWave m/z=10 ppm, peakwidth=c(10,60), bw=5, mzwid=0.025, minfrac=0.5). Isotope and adduct annotation was performed using CAMERA. Metabolite identification was achieved by matching accurate mass (&lt;10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra against an in-house database authenticated with reference standards. Data preprocessing included filtering features with &gt;50% missing values, KNN imputation, and RSD&gt;50% feature filtering. Multivariate statistical analysis (PCA, OPLS-DA) was performed using the R package ropls with Pareto scaling. Model validation used 7-fold cross-validation and permutation testing. Differentially expressed metabolites were defined by VIP&gt;1 and Student's t-test p&lt;0.05.</p>	<p>OMV samples were thawed slowly at 4°C. Pre-chilled methanol/acetonitrile/water (2:2:1, v/v/v) was added to each sample, followed by vortex mixing and low-temperature ultrasonication for 30 min. The mixture was incubated at -20°C for 10 min to precipitate proteins, then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and dried in a vacuum centrifuge. For LC-MS analysis, the dried extract was reconstituted in 100 μL acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v), vortexed, and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C before injection. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes of supernatant from all samples and analyzed at regular intervals (every 5 samples) throughout the analytical run to monitor system stability and reproducibility.</p>	<p> Metabolites were detected on two complementary high-resolution MS platforms:</p><p>(1) Agilent 1290 UHPLC coupled to AB Sciex TripleTOF 6600 with ESI source operated in both positive and negative ionization modes. Gas1=60 psi, Gas2=60 psi, curtain gas=30 psi, source temperature=600°C, ISVF=±5500 V. TOF MS scan range m/z 60–1000 Da, accumulation time 0.20 s/spectrum; MS/MS in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode, collision energy 35±15 eV, DP ±60 V, 10 candidate ions per cycle, exclusion of isotopes within 4 Da.</p><p>(2) Thermo Vanquish UHPLC coupled to Orbitrap Exploris 480 with ESI source in positive (3500 V) and negative (2800 V) modes. Gas1=50, Gas2=2, source temperature=350°C. Full scan m/z 70–1200 Da at resolution 60,000, accumulation time 100 ms; data-dependent MS/MS at resolution 60,000, dynamic exclusion 4 s.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed by comparing accurate precursor m/z values (&lt;10 ppm mass error) and MS/MS fragmentation spectra against a commercial in-house standard database (Bioyousee/Biotree database) built with authenticated reference standards. Putative identification was assigned at Level 2 (library matching) based on matching MS/MS spectra. Isotopic and adduct annotations (M+H, M-H, M+Na, M+NH4, etc.) were assigned using CAMERA. A total of 1,137 metabolites were putatively annotated across both positive and negative ionization modes. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to map differential metabolites to canonical metabolic pathways.</p>

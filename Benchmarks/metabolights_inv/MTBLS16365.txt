Study Title	Gut Microbiota and Metabolomic Profiles Associated with Growth Differences in Medium- and Slow-Growing Yellow-Feathered Broilers
Study Description	This study aimed to investigate the metabolic pathways and microbial factors potentially associated with the growth differences between medium-growing and slow-growing yellow-feathered broilers. We integrated cecal metagenomic data, duodenal 16S rRNA sequencing data, and multi-tissue metabolomic data to characterize differences in microbial composition, metabolic profiles, and functional pathways between two chicken groups exhibiting distinct growth phenotypes. Furthermore, a core differential bacterial species was isolated, identified, and characterized in vitro.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Four types of samples were collected for metabolomic analysis in Nanning, Guangxi, China. These included bacterial strain culture supernatants, duodenal contents, plasma, and breast muscle tissues.</p><p><br></p><p>For bacterial strain metabolomic analysis, bacterial strains were cultured in March 2026. Following cultivation, the bacterial cultures were centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were collected for metabolomic analysis. The collected supernatants were immediately stored at 80°C until further analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>For chicken metabolomic analysis, duodenal contents, blood samples, and breast muscle tissues were collected from the experimental broilers in Nanning, Guangxi, China. Duodenal contents were collected from the intestinal tract, and breast muscle samples were obtained from the breast muscle tissue. Blood samples were collected and processed to obtain plasma. All collected biological samples were stored at 80°C until metabolomic analysis.</p>	<p>Separation was performed using a Thermo Vanquish ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. For positive ion mode, the mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A2) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B2), with a gradient elution program of: 0–1 min, 10% B2; 1–5 min, 10%–98% B2; 5–6.5 min, 98% B2; 6.5–6.6 min, 98%–10% B2; 6.6–8 min, 10% B2. For negative ion mode, the mobile phases consisted of 5 mM ammonium formate in water (A3) and acetonitrile (B3), with a gradient elution program of: 0–1 min, 10% B3; 1–5 min, 10%–98% B3; 5–6.5 min, 98% B3; 6.5–6.6 min, 98%–10% B3; 6.6–8 min, 10% B3.</p>	<p>The raw mass spectrometry data acquired in both positive and negative ion modes were converted into the mzXML format using the MSConvert tool in ProteoWizard software (version 3.0.8789) for subsequent peak detection, filtering, and alignment using the XCMS package in R.</p>	<p>Following sample preparation, data acquisition was performed on a liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry platform.</p>	<p>The operating parameters were set as follows: spray voltage, 3.50 kV (positive) and -2.50 kV (negative); sheath gas, 40 arb; auxiliary gas, 10 arb; and capillary temperature, 325 °C. Full-scan MS spectra were acquired with a resolution of 60,000 over a mass range of m/z 100–1000. HCD was employed for MS/MS secondary fragmentation with a collision energy of 30% and a resolution of 15,000, selecting the top 4 ions for fragmentation with dynamic exclusion enabled. The raw mass spectrometry data were converted to mzXML format using the MSConvert tool in Proteowizard software (v3.0.8789). Peak detection, filtering, and alignment were performed using the R XCMS package (v3.12.0) with parameters set as bw = 2, ppm = 15, peakwidth = c(5, 30), mzwid = 0.015, mzdiff = 0.01, and method = 'centWave'. Blank samples were analyzed in parallel for blank correction, and total peak area normalization was applied to eliminate systematic errors. Metabolites were identified by matching accurate masses (ppm &lt; 30), molecular formula predictions, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns against public databases and our in-house library.</p>	<p>Metabolites were identified by matching the accurate mass measurements (mass error &lt; 30 ppm), predicted molecular formulas, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns against publicly available metabolomics databases and an in-house spectral library. Both positive and negative ionization modes were considered during metabolite annotation. The identified metabolites were subsequently annotated and mapped to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database for pathway enrichment analysis.</p>

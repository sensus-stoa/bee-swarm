Study Title	Temperature-dependent remodeling of the excretory-secretory metabolome of Anisakis pegreffii third-stage larvae
Study Description	Temperature is a major environmental factor influencing the physiology and metabolic activity of parasitic nematodes. Anisakis pegreffii is a marine zoonotic parasite of public health relevance whose third-stage larvae (L3) are exposed to varying thermal conditions during their life cycle, potentially affecting their behaviour and interactions with host tissues. However, the impact of temperature on the excretory–secretory (ES) metabolome of Anisakis larvae remains poorly understood. Here, we used an untargeted ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–high-resolution accurate-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRAM MS) approach to characterize metabolites released by A. pegreffii L3 larvae cultured at three selected temperatures: 13 °C, 19 °C, and 25 °C. A total of 84 annotated metabolites were included in the untargeted metabolomic dataset, revealing temperature-associated differences in their extracellular abundance. At 13 °C, the ES metabolomic profile was characterized by higher relative levels of selected amino acids and nucleotide-related intermediates, potentially consistent with a more resource-conserving metabolic configuration. In contrast, the metabolomic signature at 25 °C showed higher relative abundance of selected purine metabolites and pantothenate, suggesting possible differences in nucleotide turnover and cofactor-related metabolism. At the intermediate thermal condition (19 °C), the ES metabolome displayed a transitional pattern between those observed at 13 °C and 25 °C. Overall, these findings indicate that temperature is associated with changes in the composition of the ES metabolome of A. pegreffii L3 larvae, potentially reflecting differences in metabolic activity across thermal conditions. This study represents the first investigation of temperature-dependent changes in the ES metabolome of A. pegreffii L3 larvae, providing an initial characterization of their extracellular metabolic profiles and a basis for investigating the potential relevance of parasite derived metabolites to host interactions and seafood safety.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Third-stage larvae (L3) of Anisakis were collected from the visceral cavities of two European hake (Merluccius merluccius) caught in the Adriatic Sea off San Benedetto del Tronto, Italy. Larvae were washed and pooled before being distributed into nine independent cultures, each containing 30 larvae in 4 mL of sterile filtered phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Three independent cultures were incubated at each temperature (13, 19, and 25 °C) for 24 h in a humidified atmosphere. Following incubation, larvae were removed and the excretory-secretory (ES)-containing supernatants were collected for metabolomic analysis. Each culture supernatant represented one biological replicate.</p>	<p>Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) was performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Five microliters of each extracted sample were injected onto a Thermo Accucore C18 reversed-phase column (150 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 µm particle size). Chromatographic separation was carried out at a column temperature of 30 °C and a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min.</p><p><br></p><p>For positive ionization mode, mobile phase A consisted of ultrapure water containing 0.1% formic acid, while mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. A linear gradient from 0% to 100% mobile phase B was applied over 19.5 min, followed by a return to 95% mobile phase A within 0.1 min and a 10.4 min post-run equilibration period.</p><p><br></p><p>For negative ionization mode, the same chromatographic gradient was used, with mobile phase A consisting of ultrapure water containing 0.1% ammonium acetate and mobile phase B consisting of acetonitrile containing 0.1% ammonium acetate. The UHPLC system was coupled online to a high-resolution Orbitrap mass spectrometer.</p>	<p>Raw UHPLC-HRAM-MS data files from biological replicates were exported in mzXML format and processed using MAVEN version 8.1. Extracted ion chromatograms were generated for metabolite feature detection, peak alignment, and integration. The resulting integrated peak areas were used as measures of relative metabolite abundance across samples.</p><p><br></p><p>Metabolomic data were subsequently analyzed using R version 4.4.2 within the RStudio environment and MetaboAnalyst version 6.0. Prior to statistical analysis, metabolite intensities were normalized by the median intensity of each sample, followed by autoscaling using MetaboAnalyst 6.0.</p><p><br></p><p>Hierarchical clustering analysis was performed to visualize metabolite abundance patterns using heatmaps. Pairwise comparisons between temperature conditions (13 °C, 19 °C, and 25 °C) were performed using unpaired Student's t-tests, applying a nominal significance threshold of p &lt; 0.05 and a fold-change threshold of 1.5. Volcano plots and box plots were generated to visualize differential metabolite abundance.</p>	<p>The collected excretory-secretory (ES)-containing culture supernatants were subjected to metabolite extraction using cold methanol and chloroform at a 3:2 (v/v) ratio. Samples were vortexed and sonicated for 20 min, followed by overnight incubation at −20 °C. Subsequently, samples were kept at 4 °C for 20 min and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected and dried to obtain visible pellets. The dried extracts were reconstituted in 0.1 mL of water containing 5% formic acid and transferred into glass autosampler vials for UHPLC-HRAM-MS analysis.</p>	<p>High-resolution accurate-mass spectrometry (HRAM-MS) was performed using an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled online to a Vanquish UHPLC system. Mass spectrometric analyses were conducted in both positive and negative ionization modes.</p><p><br></p><p>Data were acquired in full MS scan mode at a mass resolution of 60,000 over an m/z range of 80–1,200.</p><p><br></p><p>The mass spectrometer was calibrated before each analysis using positive- and negative-ion calibration mixtures (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) to maintain sub-ppm mass accuracy.</p><p><br></p><p>Biological samples were analyzed in two consecutive acquisition sessions corresponding to positive and negative ionization modes. Blank injections were included, and sample injection order was randomized to minimize systematic bias.</p><p><br></p><p>Instrumental performance was evaluated using a mixture of four reference standards analyzed at the beginning and end of each acquisition session, with polarity switching. The coefficients of variation for positive-ion measurements ranged from 2.03% to 8.94% for peak areas and from 0.09% to 0.93% for retention times.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed using an untargeted UHPLC-HRAM-MS approach. Raw mass spectrometry data were converted to mzXML format and processed using MAVEN version 8.1. Extracted ion chromatograms were generated for feature detection, peak alignment, and integration.</p><p><br></p><p>Metabolites were putatively annotated by accurate-mass matching against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, applying a mass tolerance of 2 ppm between experimentally observed and theoretical m/z values.</p><p><br></p><p>A total of 86 metabolites were putatively annotated across the three experimental temperature conditions (13 °C, 19 °C, and 25 °C). Integrated chromatographic peak areas were used to estimate relative metabolite abundance across biological replicates.</p>

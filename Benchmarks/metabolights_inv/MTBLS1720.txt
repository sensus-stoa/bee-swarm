Study Identifier	MTBLS1720
Study Title	Viral infection of algal blooms leaves a unique metabolic footprint on the dissolved organic matter in the ocean
Study Description	<p>Algal blooms are hotspots of primary production in the ocean, forming the basis of the marine food web and fueling the dissolved organic matter (DOM) pool. Marine viruses are key players in controlling algal bloom demise, thereby diverting algal biomass from higher trophic levels to the DOM pool, a process termed the ‘viral shunt’. To decode the metabolic footprint of the ‘viral shunt’ in the marine environment, we induced a bloom of&nbsp;<em>Emiliania huxleyi</em>&nbsp;and followed its succession using an untargeted exometabolomics approach. Here, we show that algal bloom succession induces dynamic changes in the exometabolic landscape. We discovered a set of novel chlorine-iodine-containing metabolites that were induced by viral infection and released during bloom demise. These metabolites were further detected in virus-infected oceanic&nbsp;<em>E. huxleyi</em>&nbsp;blooms.&nbsp;Therefore, we propose that halogenation with both chlorine and iodine is a distinct hallmark of the virus-induced DOM of&nbsp;<em>E. huxleyi</em>, providing insights into the metabolic consequences of the ‘viral shunt’ for marine DOM.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Mesocosm:</strong></p><p>The mesocosm experiment AQUACOSM VIMS-Ehux was carried out between 24th&nbsp;May (day 0) and 16th&nbsp;June (day 23) 2018 in Raunefjorden at the Marine Biological Station Espegrend, Norway (60°16′11N; 5°13′07E) as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. 4 light-transparent enclosure bags&nbsp;were filled with non-filtered surrounding fjord water (day -1; pumped from 5 m depth) and continuously mixed by aeration (from day 0 onwards). Each bag was supplemented with nutrients at a nitrogen to phosphorous ratio of 16:1 (1.6 µM NaNO3&nbsp;and 0.1 µM KH2PO4&nbsp;final concentration) on days 0-5 and 14-17, whereas on days 6, 7 and 13 only nitrogen was added. Nutrient concentrations were measured daily<strong>[2]</strong>.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>Water samples were collected daily (07:00 AM) from each bag and the surrounding fjord, which served as environmental reference. Water samples were collected in 10-20 l carboys (rinsed with &lt;100 kDa filtered seawater) from approximately 1 m depth using a peristaltic pump at ca. 5 l/min and a 200 µm nylon pre-filter. Samples were kept at 4 °C until further processing. To collect dissolved organic matter of &lt;0.22 µm particle size, water samples were filtered gently, acidified, and extracted by solid phase extraction (SPE). Glassware and chemically resistant equipment were used whenever possible and cleaned with HCl (1% or 10%) and Deconex 20 NS-x (Borer Chemie, Zuchwil, Switzerland) to reduce contaminations. On day 5, no water samples were extracted for DOM analysis. Water samples were first gravity-filtered over 25 µm pre-cleaned stainless steel filters (47 mm, Sinun Tech, Barkan, Israel). Filtrates were then filtered gently by vacuum (&lt;400 mbar under-pressure) over pre-combusted GF/A filters (≥5 h at 460 °C; 47 mm, GE Healthcare Whatman, Buckinghamshire, UK), and finally over 0.22 µm pre-cleaned hydrophilic PVDF filters (&lt;600 mbar under-pressure; 47 mm, Durapore, Merck Millipore, Cork, Ireland). Per sample, 1 l of filtrate was collected in a glass bottle and supplemented with 5 µl internal standard solutions containing&nbsp;N-hexanoyl-L-homoserine lactone-d3&nbsp;(0.2 µg/µl in methanol) and indole-3-acetic-2,2-d2&nbsp;acid (1 µg/µl in water). Filtrates were incubated 2-3 h at 4 °C in the dark, and then acidified to pH 2 using 10% HCl<strong>[3]</strong>.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NA-VICE cruise:</strong></p><p>Water samples of natural&nbsp;<em>E. huxleyi</em>&nbsp;blooms were collected during the NA-VICE cruise as described previously<strong>[4]</strong>. Samples (1-1.5 l) were pre-filtered through 200 µm mesh and collected on 0.8 µm hydrophilic polycarbonate filters (47 mm, Millipore). The filters were then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until processing 7 years post collection.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Vardi A, Haramaty L, Van Mooy BA, Fredricks HF, Kimmance SA, Larsen A, Bidle KD. Host-virus dynamics and subcellular controls of cell fate in a natural coccolithophore population. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A. 2012 Nov 20;109(47):19327-32. doi:10.1073/pnas.1208895109. PMID: 23134731.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Vincent F et al. PMID:29531367.</p>	<p><strong>Mesocosm:</strong></p><p>Metabolites were extracted from each 1 l filtrate using hydrophilic-lipophilic balanced SPE cartridges (Oasis HLB, 500 mg, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) as follows: cartridges were conditioned (6 ml methanol), equilibrated (6 ml 0.01 N HCl), and then loaded by gravity with the acidified samples (1.5-2.5 h). The cartridges were then washed (18 ml 0.01 N HCl), dried completely using a vacuum pump, and gravity-eluted with 5 ml methanol into 4 ml glass vials. In total, 110 biological samples were collected. Eluates were kept at -80 °C, dried under a flow of nitrogen (TurboVap LV, Biotage, Uppsala, Sweden) within 1.5 months after collection, and stored at -80 °C until further processing.</p><p><br></p><p>Dry eluates (n = 110) were randomized and divided into 3 batches with approximately 40 samples each batch including blanks and internal standard QC samples. Randomization was performed automatically using an in-house R script with the following constraints: the total number of biological samples per batch was either 36 or 37, of which 7 or 8 samples were randomly sampled from the pool of fjord samples and the remaining samples were randomly sampled from the pool of mesocosm bag samples. Every experimental sampling day was represented at least once in each analytical batch. Per batch, samples&nbsp;were thawed, re-dissolved in 310 μl methanol:water (1:1, ν:ν) containing as injection standards tryptophan-d5&nbsp;(2.1 µg/ml) and caffeine-d9&nbsp;(1.5 µg/ml), vortexed, sonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 3,200 x g for&nbsp;10 min&nbsp;at 4 °C. The&nbsp;supernatants were transferred to 200&nbsp;μl glass inserts in autosampler vials and directly used for LC-MS analysis. A pooled QC sample was generated by combining aliquots of 10 µl from all biological samples of the first batch. For each batch, a new aliquot was transferred into an injection vial.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p><strong>NA-VICE cruise:</strong></p><p>Extraction was performed as described previously<strong>[1]</strong> with slight modifications. Briefly, filters were extracted with 3 ml pre-cooled (-20 °C) methanol:methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE, 1:3, ν:ν) solution containing N-hexanoyl-L-homoserine lactone-d3&nbsp;(0.33 µg/ml) and indole-3-acetic-2,2-d2&nbsp;acid (1.67 µg/ml) as internal standards. The samples were shaken for 30 min at 4 °C and sonicated for 30 min. The samples were then supplemented with 1.5 ml water:methanol (3:1, ν:ν) solution, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged for 10 min at 3,200 x g and 4 °C. The upper organic phase was removed (1.5 ml) and the remaining aqueous phase was re-extracted with 1.5 ml pre-cooled MTBE to further reduce lipophilic metabolites. Finally, the aqueous phase (1.5 ml) was transferred into 2 ml centrifuge tubes, centrifuged at 28,000 x g, and the supernatant was dried under a flow of nitrogen (TurboVap LV) followed by lyophilization (Gamma 2-16 LSCplus, Martin Christ, Osterode am Harz, Germany). The dried extracts were kept at -80 °C until analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>Dry samples were randomized automatically using an in-house R script without any constrains. Samples were processed as described above.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Schleyer G, Shahaf N, Ziv C, Dong Y, Meoded RA, Helfrich EJN, Schatz D, Rosenwasser S, Rogachev I, Aharoni A, Piel J, Vardi A. Briefly, metabolites were separated using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100&nbsp;×&nbsp;2.1 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters) attached to a VanGuard Pre-column (5&nbsp;×&nbsp;2.1 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters) with a column oven temperature of 35 °C and a gradient of 5-100% acetonitrile at a flow of 0.3 ml/min&nbsp;leading to 40 min total run time. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in either acetonitrile:water (5:95,&nbsp;ν:ν, mobile phase A) or acetonitrile (mobile phase B). The chromatographic gradient was set to linear from 100% to 72% mobile phase A over 22 min and from 72% to 30% mobile phase A over 13.5 min, after which the column was first washed with 100% mobile phase B for 2 min and then returned to initial conditions (100% mobile phase A) and equilibrated for 1.5 min (40 min total run time). The PDA detector was set to 200-600 nm. A divert valve (Rheodine) excluded 0-1.2 min and 35-40 min from injection to the mass spectrometer.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NA-VICE cruise:</strong></p><p>An aliquot of 3 µl was analyzed as described above.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong>&nbsp;Shahaf N, Rogachev I, Heinig U, Meir S, Malitsky S, Battat M, Wyner H, Zheng S, Wehrens R, Aharoni A. PMID:27571918.</p>	<p><strong>Mesocosm:</strong> </p><p>Eluting compounds were detected using a SYNAPT G2 HDMS Quadrupole Time-of-Flight mass spectrometer (Waters). The ESI source was set to 140 °C source and 450 °C desolvation temperature, 1.5 kV capillary voltage, and 20 eV or 27 eV cone voltage (positive or negative ionization mode, respectively), using nitrogen as desolvation gas (800 l/h) and cone gas (53 l/h). The mass spectrometer was operated in separate acquisitions in full scan MSE&nbsp;positive and negative resolution mode (26,000 at&nbsp;m/z&nbsp;556) over a mass range of 50-1600 Da alternating with 0.1 min scan time between low- (1 eV collision energy) and high-energy scan function (collision energy ramp of 10-45 eV in positive and 10-40 eV in negative ionization mode). LC-MS analyses were performed over 2 weeks with about 120 injections per batch in the positive and negative MS ionization mode including blanks, internal standard QC samples, authentic standard mixes, reference material, and aliquots of the pooled QC sample.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NA-VICE cruise:</strong></p><p>Samples were analyzed as described above over 2 days in 1 batch.</p>	<p>All LC-MS files were converted from Waters RAW binary files to open-format ‘<strong>mzXML</strong>’ files using the command line version of the ‘<strong>msconvert</strong>’ utility as part of the ProteoWizard toolkit<strong>[1]</strong>. Conversion parameters were set as follows: 64 bit numeric accuracy, ‘<strong>zlib</strong>’ compression enabled, and ‘<strong>scanEvent</strong>’ filter set to ‘<strong>1</strong>’ corresponding with the MS1 acquisition channel.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>All LC-MS/MS files were converted from Waters RAW binary files to open-format ‘<strong>netCDF</strong>’ files using DataBridge (MassLynx version 4.1, Waters).</p><p><br></p><p>Refs:</p><p>[1]&nbsp;Chambers MC, Maclean B, Burke R, Amodei D, Ruderman DL, Neumann S, Gatto L, Fischer B, Pratt B et al. For a few metabolites, collision energy was set to 20 eV or 30 eV to improve fragmentation. Manually curated MS/MS spectra were used to annotate fragments and neutral losses and for the prediction of molecular formulas using SIRIUS 4.0.1<strong>[1]</strong>. Full identification of the metabolites was not accomplished and therefore no data for identified metabolites is provided.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NA-VICE cruise:</strong></p><p>MS/MS analyses were performed in negative ionization mode as described above for selected chloro-iodo metabolites using an injection volume of 4 µl.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong>&nbsp;Böcker S, Dührkop K.

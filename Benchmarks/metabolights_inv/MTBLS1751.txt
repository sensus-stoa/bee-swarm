Study Identifier	MTBLS1751
Study Title	Resource Partitioning of Diatom Metabolites that Support Bacterial Heterotrophy in the Ocean (UPLC-MS assay)
Study Description	<p>The communities of marine bacteria that assemble around living microphytoplankton are predictably dominated by three taxonomic groups, each of which specializes in different components of the available metabolite pool: Rhodobacterales transport small and polar metabolites, Flavobacteriia use carbohydrate-rich polymers, and Gammaproteobacteria use compounds from both classes. The consistent ecological pattern involving these three taxa is widespread throughout the surface ocean, yet whether it reflects the outcome competition or niche partitioning of the phytoplankton-derived compounds is not clear. Addressing this question requires better knowledge than currently exists of the metabolites that link microbial autotrophs and heterotrophs in the surface ocean. Here we used two untargeted biological screening strategies that leverage bacterial proficiency for scavenging dilute substrates from chemically complex mixtures to characterize metabolite uptake by heterotrophic bacteria. In the first, expression patterns of transporters and diagnostic catabolic genes were analyzed in model marine bacteria grown individually in co-culture with the diatom Thalassiosira pseudonana. In the second, the ability of bacteria to draw down exometabolites from the culture medium was detected by novel approaches in mass spectrometry (MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis. These methods identified diverse chemical currencies mediating carbon transfer including low molecular weight metabolites (nucleosides, amino acids, organic acids, monomeric sugars, peptides, and sulfonates) and components of polysaccharides (chrysolaminarin, chitin oligomers, and alginate-like oligosaccharides). Bacterial utilization of bioreactive metabolites in the absence of competition indicated low resource overlap among strains and a dominance of resource partitioning over resource competition among the bacterial groups that process a major fraction of marine net primary production.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>UPLC-MS assay</strong> is reported in the current study <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS1751' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MTBLS1751</strong></a></p><p><strong>NMR assay</strong> is reported in <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS1544' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MTBLS1544</strong></a></p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Metabolites in the filtrate were derivatized with benzoyl chloride<strong>[1]</strong> by modification of methods<strong>[2][3]</strong>. Each sample was combined with 300 µl sodium carbonate, 30 µl sodium hydroxide, and 200 µl working reagent (5% benzoyl chloride in acetone V:V). Samples were vortexed for 5 min and then acidified with concentrated phosphoric acid to pH 2-3 (15 µl). The acetone was evaporated using a vacufuge (Eppendorf), and an equivalent volume of Milli-Q water was added to replace the acetone. Samples were extracted using solid phase extraction Bond Elut PPL cartridges (Agilent, 1 g/6 ml) that were conditioned by rinsing with 1 cartridge volume of methanol, followed by 4 cartridge volumes of acidified deionized water (pH 2-3, HCl). Following conditioning, 1 ml of the aqueous phase of each sample was loaded onto each cartridge by gravity and. eluted with methanol (6 ml). Eluents were dried down (vacufuge), reconstituted in 95:10 water:acetonitrile (100 µl), and stored at 4 °C until analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Widner et al. in prep</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Oehlke J, Brudel M, Blasig IE. Benzoylation of sugars, polyols and amino acids in biological fluids for high-performance liquid chromatographic analysis. J Chromatogr B Biomed Appl. 1994 Apr 22;655(1):105-11. doi:10.1016/0378-4347(94)00067-0. PMID:8061817.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Wong JM, Malec PA, Mabrouk OS, Ro J, Dus M, Kennedy RT. Benzoyl chloride derivatization with liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry for targeted metabolomics of neurochemicals in biological samples. J Chromatogr A. 2016 May 13;1446:78-90. doi:10.1016/j.chroma.2016.04.006. PMID: 27083258</p>	<p>Liquid chromatography was performed on a UHPLC system (Vanquish Binary pump and autosampler) using a reverse phase column (Waters Acquity HSS T3 column (2.1 x 100 mm, 1.8 μm) with a Vanguard pre-column at 40 °C). The mobile phase was (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min. The gradient conditions were 0-0.5 min (1% B), 2 min (10% B), 2-5 min (10% B), 7 min (25% B), 7-9 min (25% B), 12.5 min (50% B), 13 min (95% B), 13-14.5 min (95% B). Separate injections of 5 μl each were made for positive and negative ion modes.</p>	<p>Samples were analyzed in both negative and positive ion mode on an ultrahigh resolution tribrid mass spectrometer, the Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following heated electrospray ionization (H-ESI). The Lumos was operated in full-scan MS mode (175 to 1500 m/z scan range) with data-dependent tandem mass spectrometry, guided by a list of user-defined parent ions with a retention time window for each (full MS/ddMS2 with inclusion list). In this targeted quantitation approach, data is collected in full-scan mode in the Orbitrap analyzer. When any of the parent ions on the inclusion list are detected within the retention time window, MS/MS scans are automatically acquired and then the instrument resumes full-scan operation. All data were collected in profile mode.</p>	<p>Sample and standard peak areas were integrated on the RAW XCalibur files using Skyline<strong>[1][2]</strong>, and post-Skyline processing was done in MATLAB. Standard curves were calculated using at least 5 standards, where standard curves were prepared in L1 medium and ranged from 0.5 to 500 ng/ml. Standard curve R squared values were required to be greater than 0.92 for metabolite quantification. Concentrations were corrected for matrix effects using the analytical recovery of a standard mix spiked into each sample. Because of this correction, we are reporting 'adjusted concentration' for metabolites. The units are nM. </p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Pino LK, Searle BC, Bollinger JG, Nunn B, MacLean B, MacCoss MJ. The Skyline ecosystem: Informatics for quantitative mass spectrometry proteomics. Mass Spectrom Rev. 2020 May;39(3):229-244. doi:10.1002/mas.21540. PMID:28691345</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Henderson CM, Shulman NJ, MacLean B, MacCoss MJ, Hoofnagle AN. Skyline Performs as Well as Vendor Software in the Quantitative Analysis of Serum 25-Hydroxy Vitamin D and Vitamin D Binding Globulin. Clin Chem. 2018 Feb;64(2):408-410. doi:10.1373/clinchem.2017.282293. PMID:29203474</p>	<p>Metabolites were identified by comparison to known standards using retention time, parent mass-to-charge value, and at least one fragment mass-to-charge value.</p>	<p>An axenic Thalassiosira pseudonana 1335 culture (National Center for Marine Algae) was grown in 500 ml of L1 +Si medium<strong>[1]</strong> in a 600 ml vented tissue culture flask at 18 °C under 16 h light at 160 µmol photons/m^2/s and 8 h dark. After the diatom cultures had been growing for 7 d, bacteria pre-grown in 1/2 YTSS medium at 30 °C (Ruegeria pomeroyi DSS-3 (treatment A) and Stenotrophomonas sp. SKA14 (treatment D)) or 1/10 YTSS medium at 25 °C (Polaribacter dokdonensis (treatment E)) were washed 5 times in sterile L1 medium and inoculated into the diatom cultures and glucose controls at ~10^6 (Stenotrophomonas sp. and P. dokdonensis) or ~10^7 (R. pomeroyi) cells/ml. All co-cultures were in triplicate. Axenic T. pseudonana and unamended L1+Si medium were maintained and sampled alongside the co-cultures as controls (treatment X and C). Samples were collected for mass spectrometry metabolite analysis at 8, 24, and 49 h time points and from uninoculated L1 medium as a blank, i.e. sample A1_24 is treatment A (R. pomeroyi + DSS-3, replicate 1, 24 h timepoint). The filtrate was stored frozen at -20 °C. </p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Guillard RRL &amp; Hargraves PE. (1993) Stichochrysis immobilis is a diatom, not a chrysophyte, Phycologia, 32:3, 234-236, doi:10.2216/i0031-8884-32-3-234.1 </p>

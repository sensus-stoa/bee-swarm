Study Title	Migratory Zooplankton Excreta and its Influence on Prokaryotic Communities
Study Description	<p>Particulate organic matter (fecal pellets) from zooplankton has been demonstrated to be important nutrient sources for the pelagic prokaryotic community.&nbsp;Significantly less is known about the chemical composition of the dissolved organic matter (DOM) produced by these eukaryotes and its influence on pelagic ecosystem structure. Zooplankton migrators, which daily transport surface-derived compounds to depth, may act as important vectors of limiting nutrients for mesopelagic microbial communities. In this role, zooplankton may increase the DOM remineralization rate by heterotrophic prokaryotes through the creation of nutrient rich “hot spots” that could potentially increase niche diversity. To explore these interactions, we collected the migratory copepod Pleuromamma xiphias from the northwestern Sargasso Sea and sampled its excreta after 12-16 h of incubation. We measured bulk dissolved organic carbon, dissolved free amino acids via high performance liquid chromatography and dissolved targeted metabolites via quantitative mass spectrometry (UPLC-ESI-MSMS) to quantify organic zooplankton excreta production and characterize its composition. We observed production of labile DOM, including amino acids, vitamins and nucleosides. Additionally, we harvested a portion of the excreta and subsequently used it as the growth medium for mesopelagic (200m) bacterioplankton dilution cultures.&nbsp;In zooplankton excreta treatments we observed a four-fold increase in bacterioplankton cell densities that reached stationary growth phase after five days of dark incubation. Analyses of 16s rDNA amplicons suggested a shift from oligotrophs typical of open ocean and mesopelagic prokaryotic communities to more copiotrophic bacterial lineages in the presence of zooplankton excreta. These results support the hypothesis that zooplankton and prokaryotes are engaged in complex and indirect ecological interactions, broadening our understanding of the microbial loop.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Copepods (<em>Pleuromamma xiphias</em>) and incubation water were collected aboard the RV <em>Atlantic Explorer</em> on September 7-10th 2016 and July 9-10th 2017 in the vicinity of the Bermuda Atlantic Time series Station (BATS) (31° 40’N, 64° 10’W). Zooplankton were captured with a custom 1 m Reeve net with 20 l cod end after 22:00 (local time) from 100-150 m. <em>Pleuromamma </em>were picked from these samples and visually identified to species, and rinsed 3x with filtered seawater before initiation of the experiments. Water for the experiments was collected from the mesopelagic zone (200 m) using Niskin bottles and gravity filtered through a 142 mm 0.2 μm Omnipore filter (Millipore).</p><p><br></p><p>After cleaning, copepods were incubated in 2 (September 2016, cruise AE1620) or 3 (July 2017, cruise AE1712) replicates of 1000 ml 0.2 µm-filtered water in 2 l polycarbonate Biotainers (Nalgene). The number of copepods in the zooplankton treatments ranged between 42 and 58 individuals per replicate. Control treatments comprised exclusively of 0.2 µm-filtered seawater were incubated and processed at the same time as treatments that included zooplankton (AE1620-A and B for 2016 and AE1712-A, B, and C for 2017). Copepods were incubated for 12.5-16 h in the dark at in situ temperatures (AE1620-C and D and AE1712-D, E, and F for 2016 and 2017, respectively). At the conclusion of the experiment, copepods were removed using an 80 µm Nytex screen. An 800 ml aliquot from each bottle was filtered through a 0.2 µm Teflon filter (Omnipore, Millipore Sigma) placed in an acid-cleaned (10% HCl) Teflon filter holder.</p>	<p>The filtrate was then acidified to pH ~3 using HCl and loaded onto a methanol-rinsed solid phase extraction cartridge (1 g, 6 ml, Agilent Bond Elut PPL) using a vacuum pump as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. The PPL cartridges were rinsed with acidified (pH ~3, 0.01 M HCl) MilliQ water, and metabolites were eluted in 6 ml of methanol. Extracts were evaporated to near dryness using a Vacufuge (Eppendorf) and reconstituted in 250 µl of 95:5 (v/v) water/acetonitrile with deuterated biotin (0.05 mg/ml). Samples were stored at -20 °C until analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Longnecker K. Dissolved organic matter in newly formed sea ice and surface seawater. Geochim Cosmochim Acta 171, 39-49 (2015). doi:10.1016/j.gca.2015.08.014.</p>	<p>Ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) was performed using an Accela Open Autosampler and Accela 1250 Pump, Thermo Scientific<strong>[1]</strong>. The reverse phase column (Waters Aquity HSS T3, 2.1 x 100 mm, 1.8 μm) was equipped with a Vanguard pre-column and heated to 40 °C. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The flow rate was maintained at 0.5 ml/min. The gradient began at 1% B for 1 min, increased to 15% from 1 to 3 min, then increased to 50% B from 3 to 6 min, and then increased to 95% B from 6 to 9 min. The mobile phase was maintained at 95% B until 10 min and then decreased to 1% B from 10 to 10.2 min and held at 1% B for the remainder (12 min total run time). Samples were run in both positive and negative ion modes using a 5 μl injection for each.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Kido Soule MC, Longnecker K, Johnson WM, Kujawinski EB, Environmental metabolomics: Analytical stategies. Mar. Chem. 177, 374-387 (2015). doi:10.1016/j.marchem.2015.06.012.</p>	<p>Samples were analyzed in both negative and positive ion mode on a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (TSQ Vantage, Thermo Scientific) following heated electrospray ionization (h-ESI) and operated under the selected reaction monitoring (SRM) mode<strong>[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Kido Soule MC, Longnecker K, Johnson WM, Kujawinski EB, Environmental metabolomics: Analytical stategies. Mar. Chem. 177, 374-387 (2015). doi:10.1016/j.marchem.2015.06.012.</p>	<p>Raw data files were converted to mzML format using msConvert<strong>[1]</strong>, and MAVEN<strong>[2][3]</strong> was used to identify and integrate peaks for all samples and standards. Metabolite concentrations were calculated using a standard curve of at least 5 points, and concentrations were corrected for the efficiency of the solid phase extraction step using previously published data<strong>[4]</strong>. Metabolites with extraction efficiencies less than 2% are not considered quantifiable and were excluded from the dataset.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Chambers MC, Maclean B, Burke R, Amodei D, Ruderman DL, Neumann S, Gatto L, Fischer B, et al. A cross-platform toolkit for mass spectrometry and proteomics. Nat Biotechnol. 2012 Oct;30(10):918-20. doi:10.1038/nbt.2377. PMID: 23051804.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Clasquin MF, Melamud E, Rabinowitz JD. LC-MS data processing with MAVEN: a metabolomic analysis and visualization engine. Curr Protoc Bioinformatics. 2012 Mar;Chapter 14:Unit14.11. doi:10.1002/0471250953.bi1411s37. PMID:22389014.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Melamud E, Vastag L, Rabinowitz JD. Metabolomic analysis and visualization engine for LC-MS data. Anal Chem. 2010 Dec 1;82(23):9818-26. doi:10.1021/ac1021166. PMID:21049934.</p><p><strong>[4]</strong> Johnson WM, Kido Soule MC, Kujawinski EB. Extraction efficiency and quantification of dissolved metabolites in targeted marine metabolomics. Limnology and Oceanography: Methods 15, 417-428 (2017).doi:10.1002/lom3.10181.</p>	<p>Metabolites were identified by their unique retention time, parent m/z, and product m/z in comparison to authentic standards. 2 SRM transitions were monitored for quantification and confirmation.</p>

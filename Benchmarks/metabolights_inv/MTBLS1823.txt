Study Title	Metabolic cooperation and spatiotemporal niche partitioning in a kefir microbial community; Interaction between the kefir isolates Lactococcus lactis and Acetobacter (spent-medium assay, measured untargeted (FIA-qTOF) and targeted (HILIC, LC-MS)
Study Description	<p>Microbial communities often undergo intricate compositional changes yet also maintain stable coexistence of diverse species. The mechanisms underlying long-term coexistence remain unclear, as system-wide studies have been largely limited to engineered communities, ex situ adapted cultures, or synthetic assemblies. Here we show how kefir, a natural milk-fermenting community of prokaryotes and yeasts, realises stable coexistence through spatiotemporal orchestration of species and metabolite dynamics. During milk fermentation, kefir grains (a polysaccharide matrix synthesized by kefir microbes) grow in mass but remain unchanged in composition. In contrast, the milk is colonized in a sequential manner in which early members open metabolic niches for followers. Through metabolomics and large-scale mapping of inter-species interactions, we show how microbes poorly suited for milk survive in, and even dominate the community, through metabolic cooperation and uneven partitioning between grain and milk. Overall, our findings reveal how inter-species interactions partitioned in space and time lead to stable coexistence.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Linked metabolomics studies:</strong></p><p><a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS1829' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>MTBLS1829</a> Kefir fermentation curve (FIA-MS)</p><p><a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS1830' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>MTBLS1830</a> Interaction between the kefir isolates Lactococcus lactis and Acetobacter fabarum (GC-MS)</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Linked cross omic data:</strong></p><p>Genomes of isolated kefir species are available in the <a href='https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>NCBI database</a> under the accession: PRJNA375758 (bioproject ID: 375758).</p><p>Metatranscriptomic sequencing reads can be accessed from <a href='www.ebi.ac.uk/ena' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>ENA</a> under the project id PRJEB37001.</p><p>Genome-scale metabolic models for kefir bacteria can be found at github.com/cdanielmachado/kefir_models.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><u>Primary fermentation</u></p><p>L. lactis spent-whey was produced by L. lactis (strains SB-17, SB-261 and SB-352) fermentation for 96 hours or by L. lactis (strain SB-150 due to slower fermentation by this strain) for 168 hours at 30˚C without shaking. Sampling was carried out at different time points during milk fermentation by L. lactis, including pre-innoculation (milk only), 0, 24, 48, 72 and 96 hours after inoculation with an additional 168 hour time point for SB-150.</p><p><br></p><p><u>Secondary fermentation</u></p><p>At 96 or 168 hours whey was harvested by centrifugation for 40 min at 7000 rpm and filter sterilization (0.22 µM pore size). Each of the four L. lactis spent-wheys were then inoculated with one of four Acetobacter isolates (A. fabarum: internal stock IDs SB-290, SB-373 and A. ghanensis: internal stock IDs SB-354, SB-380) or mock (no species) inoculated. Sampling was carried out at 0, 24, 48 and 72 hours of growth at 30 ˚C for extracellular metabolomics analysis. </p>	<p>Fermented milk (500 µl) was centrifuged for 1 min at max speed. Supernatant (50 µl) were added to 950 µl 80 ˚C H2O and incubated for 3 min at 80 ˚C and 700 rpm. The samples were then centrifuged for 10 min at 0 ˚C at maximum speed. The supernatant was harvested and ten times diluted for measurement.</p>	<p><u>FIA-MS</u></p><p>Metabolite extracts were injected into an Agilent 6550 time-of-flight mass spectrometer (ESI-iFunnel Q-TOF, Agilent Technologies) operated at 4 Ghz high resolution, in negative mode, with a mass / charge (m/z) range of 50-1,000. The mobile phase was 60:40 isopropanol:water (v/v) and 1 mM NH4F at pH 9.0 supplemented with hexakis (1H, 1H, 3H- tetrafluoropropoxy)phosphazine and 3-amino-1-propanesulfonic acid for online mass correction. Details in detailed in Fuhrer et al, 2011 [1].</p><p><br></p><p>References:</p><p>[1] Fuhrer T,&nbsp;Heer D,&nbsp;Begemann B,&nbsp;Zamboni N. High-throughput, accurate mass metabolome profiling of cellular extracts by flow injection-time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Anal Chem. 2011 Sep 15;83(18):7074-80 doi: 10.1021/ac201267k.</p>	<p><u>FIA-MS</u></p><p>Metabolite extracts were injected into an Agilent 6550 time-of-flight mass spectrometer (ESI-iFunnel Q-TOF, Agilent Technologies) operated at 4 Ghz high resolution, in negative mode, with a mass / charge (m/z) range of 50-1,000. The mobile phase was 60:40 isopropanol:water (v/v) and 1 mM NH4F at pH 9.0 supplemented with hexakis (1H, 1H, 3H- tetrafluoropropoxy)phosphazine and 3-amino-1-propanesulfonic acid for online mass correction.</p><p><br></p><p><u>LC-MS</u></p><p>Metabolites were detected on a 5500+ QTRAP triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer in positive mode with MRM scan type (AB Sciex, Foster City, CA).&nbsp;</p><p><br></p>	<p>Data was transformed using in house pipeline. For details see Fuhrer et al, 2011 [1]. In brief, All steps of data processing and analysis were performed with Matlab R2010b (The Mathworks, Natick) using functions embedded in the Bioinformatics, Statistics, Database, and Parallel Computing toolboxes. These were embedded on a largely automated IT system comprising an application server and a relational database hosting both data and metadata and distributing time-demanding processing tasks on a high performance computer cluster. For more details on data processing, ion annotation, and statistical analysis, please refer to Supporting Information [1].</p><p><br></p><p>Reference:</p><p>[1] Fuhrer T,&nbsp;Heer D,&nbsp;Begemann B,&nbsp;Zamboni N. High-throughput, accurate mass metabolome profiling of cellular extracts by flow injection-time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Anal Chem. 2011 Sep 15;83(18):7074-80 doi: 10.1021/ac201267k.</p>	<p><u>FIA-MS</u></p><p>Matching accurate mass as detailed in Fuhrer et al, 2011 [1]. Possibly exchanged metabolites were identified by selecting ions that showed an increase during L. lactis growth (log2(FC) ≥ 1) and a decrease during Acetobacter growth (log2(FC) ≤ 1).</p><p><br></p><p><u>LC-MS</u></p><p>MRM with authentic standards as detailed in Link et al, 2015 [2].</p><p><br></p><p>References:</p><p>[1] Fuhrer T,&nbsp;Heer D,&nbsp;Begemann B,&nbsp;Zamboni N. High-throughput, accurate mass metabolome profiling of cellular extracts by flow injection-time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Anal Chem. 2011 Sep 15;83(18):7074-80 doi: 10.1021/ac201267k.</p><p>[2] Link H,&nbsp;Fuhrer T,&nbsp;Gerosa&nbsp;L,&nbsp;Zamboni&nbsp;N,&nbsp;Sauer&nbsp;U. Real-time metabolome profiling of the metabolic switch between starvation and growth. Nat Methods. 2015 Nov;12(11):1091-7. doi: 10.1038/nmeth.3584.</p>	<p><u>LC-MS</u></p><p>Metabolite extract (5 µl) was analysed on a Sciex 5500+ QTRAP triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer using an Agilent HILIC Plus RRHD column (100 mm × 2.1 mm × 1.8 µm). The gradient of mobile phase A (10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) was as follows: 0 min, 90% B; 2 min, 40% B; 3 min, 40% B; 5 min, 90% B; 6 min, 90% B. The flow rate was held constant at 400 µl/min. A titration of pure mixed amino acid calibration standard was also included (measurements-22 to -30).</p>

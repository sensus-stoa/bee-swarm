Study Identifier	MTBLS1887
Study Title	Sequential lipidomic, metabolomic, and proteomic analyses of serum, liver, and heart tissue specimens from peroxisomal biogenesis factor 11α knockout mice
Study Description	Peroxisomes are versatile single membrane-enclosed cytoplasmic organelles, involved in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid metabolism and diverse other metabolic processes. Peroxisomal disorders result from mutations in Pex genes-encoded proteins named peroxins (PEX proteins) and single peroxisomal enzyme deficiencies. The PEX11 protein family (α, β, and γ isoforms) plays an important role in peroxisomal proliferation and fission. However, their specific functions and the metabolic impact caused by their deficiencies have not been precisely characterized. To understand the systemic molecular alterations caused by peroxisomal defects, here we utilized untreated peroxisomal biogenesis factor 11α knockout (Pex11α KO) mouse model and performed serial relative-quantitative lipidomic, metabolomic, and proteomic analyses of serum, liver, and heart tissue homogenates. We demonstrated significant specific changes in the abundances of multiple lipid species, polar metabolites, and proteins and dysregulated metabolic pathways in distinct biological specimens of the Pex11α KO adult mice in comparison to the wild type (WT) controls. Overall, the present study reports comprehensive semi-quantitative molecular omics information of the Pex11α KO mice, which might serve in the future as a reference for a better understanding of the roles of Pex11α and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of peroxisomal biogenesis disorders.

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Materials </strong></p><p>Ammonium bicarbonate, ammonium formate, bovine serum albumin, dithiothreitol (DTT), iodoacetamide (IAA), methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE), thiourea, and urea were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). LC–MS grade solvents acetonitrile (ACN), methanol (MeOH), water (H2O), 2-propanol/isopropyl alcohol (IPA), and formic acid were procured from Honeywell Riedel-de Haën (Seelze, Germany). The synthetic lipid internal standards were procured from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL, USA). Sequencing grade modified trypsin was obtained from Promega Corporation (Mannheim, Germany) and RapiGest SF surfactant from Waters Corporation (Milford, MA, USA). Bradford reagent was purchased from Bio-Rad Laboratories (CA, USA) and ZipTipC18 from Merck Millipore (MA, USA). </p><p><br></p><p><strong>Pre‑analytics </strong></p><p>Pathogen-free <strong>C57BL/6&nbsp;J mice</strong> were obtained from the central animal facility of the Justus Liebig University Giessen, Germany. They were kept on a normal laboratory diet and water and maintained under standard environmental conditions. The generation, breeding, and basal characterization of <strong>Pex11α KO </strong>animals were described in details earlier<strong>[1]</strong>. Adult 3-mth-old male mice (<strong>WT control</strong> and homozygous <strong>Pex11α KO</strong>) were narcotized using 3% isofuran and sacrificed by cervical dislocation. For the isolation of <strong>heart </strong>and <strong>liver</strong>, the animals were perfused with phosphate buffer saline through the left ventricle to remove the blood. <strong>Liver </strong>and <strong>heart </strong>tissues were dissected, snap-frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen, and stored at−80&nbsp;°C until further processing. Blood was extracted by cardiac puncture immediately following the cervical dislocation and left standing for 30&nbsp;min at 37&nbsp;°C to separate the <strong>serum </strong>from the red blood cells. Then, the samples were spun for 10&nbsp;min at 1500×g at 4&nbsp;°C, and <strong>serum </strong>was collected and stored at−80&nbsp;°C until further use. The animal experiments were performed&nbsp;in accordance with the German Animal Welfare Law and recommendations of institutional animal welfare ofcers. All experiments were approved by the German Government Commission of Animal Care&nbsp;(Justus Liebig University internal classification: JLU-Nr.: 616_M, Project ID: 1016 Peroxisomen).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Li X, Baumgart E, Dong GX, Morrell JC, Jimenez-Sanchez G, Valle D, Smith KD, Gould SJ. PEX11alpha is required for peroxisome proliferation in response to 4-phenylbutyrate but is dispensable for peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha-mediated peroxisome proliferation. Mol Cell Biol. 2002 Dec;22(23):8226-40. doi: 10.1128/MCB.22.23.8226-8240.2002. PMID: 12417726; PMCID: PMC134051.</p>	<p><strong>Liquid–liquid extraction </strong></p><p>10 mg of the snap-frozen liver and heart tissues from three biological replicates of WT control (Pex11α+/+) and homozygous Pex11α KO (Pex11α−/−) mice were homogenized in phosphate bufer saline solution (200&nbsp;µl) using a tissue homogenizer (Retsch GmbH, Germany) with zirconia beads for 1&nbsp;min at 20&nbsp;Hz at 4&nbsp;°C. 200&nbsp;µl of liver or heart tissue homogenates and/or 80&nbsp;µl of serum were transferred into respective glass vials and subjected to liquid–liquid extraction as reported earlier<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, 750&nbsp;µl of icecold methanol (LC–MS grade) containing the following lipid standard mix: 56&nbsp;pmol phosphatidylethanolamine (PE 17:0/14:1); 52.5&nbsp;pmol phosphatidylglycerol (PG 17:0/14:1); 82.5&nbsp;pmol phosphatidylinositol (PI 17:0/14:1); 90&nbsp;pmol phosphatidylserine (PS 17:0/14:1); 56&nbsp;pmol phosphatidylcholine (PC 17:0/14:1); 47.5&nbsp;pmol lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC 17:1); 16.6&nbsp;pmol lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI 17:1); 84.6&nbsp;pmol lysophosphatidylserine (LPS 17:1); 50&nbsp;pmol lysophosphatidylglycerol (LPG 17:1); 42.8&nbsp;pmol lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE 17:1); 69.6&nbsp;pmol sphingomyelin (SM d18:1/17:0); 50&nbsp;pmol cholesteryl ester (ChE 19:0); 90.4&nbsp;pmol ceramide (Cer d18:1/17:0); 77.6&nbsp;pmol hexosylceramide (HexCer d18:1/12:0); 30&nbsp;pmol triglyceride (TG 17:0/17:0/17:0) were added to the homogenate samples and vortexed vigorously for 1&nbsp;min. After that, 2.5&nbsp;ml of icecold MTBE (anhydrous, 99.8%) were added to each vial and vortexed for 1&nbsp;h at room temperature. Later, 625&nbsp;µl of ice-cold water (LC–MS grade) were added for phase separation, vortexed for 1&nbsp;min, and centrifuged at 4,000&nbsp;g for 15&nbsp;min at 4&nbsp;°C. After centrifugation, the upper non-polar (lipids) and lower polar (metabolites) phases were separately collected, transferred to fresh vials, dried out in vacufuge concentrator (ambient temperature), and stored at -80&nbsp;°C until further analysis. The lower sediment (protein) pellets were resuspended in 250&nbsp;µl of lysis buffer containing 0.1% RapiGest, 1&nbsp;M urea, 0.2&nbsp;M thiourea, and 70&nbsp;mM dithiothreitol in 50&nbsp;mM of ammonium bicarbonate buffer solution. The resuspended solution was vortexed vigorously followed by centrifugation for 10&nbsp;min at 14,000xg at 4&nbsp;°C and the supernatants were collected and stored at -80&nbsp;°C until further processing.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Breitkopf SB, Taveira MO, Yuan M, Wulf GM, Asara JM. Serial-omics of P53-/-, Brca1-/- Mouse Breast Tumor and Normal Mammary Gland. Sci Rep. 2017 Nov 6;7(1):14503. doi: 10.1038/s41598-017-15132-y. PMID: 29109428; PMCID: PMC5674068.</p>	<p><strong>Lipids</strong></p><p>Dried lipid extracts were reconstituted in 100 µl of ACN:IPA:H2O (65:30:5 v/v) buffer and analysed using a hybrid quadrupole orbital trapping mass spectrometer (Q Exactive; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Briefly, 10 µl of reconstituted lipid extract were injected into a Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and separation was performed using a reversed-phase ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 1.8 µm column (100 X 2.1 mm, Waters) with a flow rate of 250 µl/min. The binary solvent system consisted of mobile phase A (H2O:ACN, 40:60 v/v with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid) and mobile phase B (IPA:ACN, 90:10 with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid). Lipids were eluted with a 28 min linear gradient of 30 to 100% mobile phase B (30% – 43% over 2 min, 43% – 55% from 2 – 2.1 min, 55% – 65% from 2.1 – 12 min, 65% – 85% from 12 – 18 min, 85% – 100% from 18 – 20 min, maintained 100% B for 5 min, followed by an immediate drop back to 30%, which is held constant up to min 28 of each run). The column temperature was set to 50 °C and autosampler to 15 °C.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p><strong>Polar Metabolites</strong></p><p>Dried polar metabolite extracts were reconstituted in 100 µl of 15% methanol and a 10 µl injection volume was used. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a reversed-phase ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 1.8 µm column (100 X 2.1 mm, Waters) at a flow rate of 300 µl/min of a binary solvent system consisted of mobile phase A (100% water with 0.1% formic acid) and mobile phase B (100% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). Polar metabolites were eluted with a 20 min linear gradient of 1 to 99% mobile phase B (maintained 1% B for 1.5 min, then 1 – 99% from 1 - 15 min, maintained 99% B for 2 min, followed by drop back to 1% in 0.3 min, which is held constant up to min 20 of each run). In the case of serum metabolite analysis, mobile phase B was replaced by 100% methanol with 0.1% formic acid and a linear gradient of 1 to 95% in 14 min (1% – 15% over 1 min, 15% – 35% from 1 – 4 min, 35% – 95% from 4 – 7 min, maintained 95% B for 2 min, followed by drop back to 1% in 1 min, which is held constant up to min 14 of each run). The column and autosampler compartments were maintained at 40 °C and 4 °C, respectively.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Proteins</strong></p><p>Peptide digests (2.5 μg) were separated using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with Kinetex C18 reversed-phase 2.6 µm column (100 X 2.1 mm, 100 A°, Phenomenex). The sample was loaded onto the column with 97% of mobile phase A (100% water, 0.1% formic acid) and 3% of mobile phase B (100% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) at 250 μl/min flow rate. Peptides were eluted with a 120 min linear gradient of 3 to 50% mobile phase B (maintained 3% for 5 min, then 3% – 40% from 5 – 95 min, 40% – 50% from 95 – 105 min, 50% – 98% from 105 – 108 min, maintained 98% B for 2 min, followed by drop back to 3% in 2 min, which is held constant up to min 120 of each run). The column temperature was set to 40 °C and autosampler at 4 °C.</p>	<p><strong>Lipids</strong></p><p>The heated electrospray ionization source (HESI II) was used with the following conditions: sheath gas: 35; auxiliary gas: 12; sweep gas: 1; spray voltage (KV): 4.35 in positive- and 4.00 in negative-ion mode; capillary temperature: 330 °C; S-Lens RF: 55; and auxiliary heater temperature: 325 °C. Lipidomic datasets were acquired in both positive- and negative-ion modes in a data-dependent manner using the top-15 method (ddMS2-top15) with the following acquisition settings. In full scan MS, scan range: 200-1800 <em>m/z</em>; resolution: 70,000; AGC target: 1e^6; and maximum injection time 250 ms. In the case of data-dependent MS/MS, mass resolution: 35,000; AGC target: 1e^5; maximum injection time: 75 ms; dynamic exclusion: 8 s; and stepped normalized collision energy (HCD) values of 25, 30 in positive-ion mode and 20, 30 and 40 in negative-ion mode. In addition, in positive-ion mode an internal lock mass (<em>m/z</em> 391.28421) and exclusion list were considered.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Polar Metabolites</strong></p><p>The heated electrospray ionization source (HESI II) was used with the following conditions: sheath gas: 42; auxiliary gas: 11; sweep gas: 0; spray voltage (KV): 4.00 in positive- and 3.50 in negative-ion mode; capillary temperature: 300 °C; S-Lens RF: 55; and auxiliary heater temperature: 310 °C. Metabolomic datasets were acquired in both positive- and negative-ion modes in a data-dependent manner using the top-10 method (ddMS2-top10) with the following acquisition settings. In full scan MS, scan range: 67-1000 <em>m/z</em>; resolution: 70,000; AGC target: 1e^6; and maximum injection time 120 ms. In the case of data-dependent MS/MS, mass resolution: 17,500; AGC target: 1e^5; maximum injection time: 80 ms; dynamic exclusion: 10 s; and stepped normalized collision energy (HCD) values of 30, 35 in positive-ion mode and 25, 30 and 35 in negative-ion mode. In addition, in positive-ion mode an internal lock mass (<em>m/z</em> 391.28421) was considered.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Proteins</strong></p><p>The heated electrospray ionization source (HESI II) was used with the following conditions: sheath gas: 30; auxiliary gas: 8; sweep gas: 0; spray voltage (KV): 4.20; capillary temperature: 320 °C; S-Lens RF: 55; and auxiliary heater temperature: 200 °C. Proteomic datasets were acquired in positive-ion mode in a data-dependent manner using the top-10 method (ddMS2-top10) with the following acquisition settings. In full scan MS, scan range: 350-1800 <em>m/z</em>; resolution: 70,000; AGC target: 1e^6; and maximum injection time 120 ms. In the case of data-dependent MS/MS, mass resolution: 17,500; AGC target: 1e^5; maximum injection time: 120 ms; dynamic exclusion: 15 s; and normalized collision energy value (HCD) of 30. In addition, an internal lock mass: (<em>m/z </em>391.28421); charge state: 2; charge exclusion of 1 and unassigned values were considered.</p>	<p>No data transformation was performed in this study.</p>	<p><strong>Lipids</strong></p><p>High-resolution and accurate LC-MS/MS lipidomic datasets were analysed using the <strong>LipidSearch </strong>(version <strong>4.2.23</strong>) software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for the identification and alignment<strong>[1]</strong>. The following parameters were used for search: Database – General and HCD; Search Type - Product; Experiment Type - LC-MS; Parent Tolerance - 0.1 Da; NL/Prec Tol - 0.5 Da; Precursor Tolerance - 5.0 ppm; Product Tolerance – 5.0 ppm; Merge Range (Min) - 0.0; Minimal Peak Width (min) - 0.0; Threshold Type - Relative; Product Ion - 1.0%; m-score threshold - 2.0; Recalculate Isotope - ON; R.T. Interval (min) - 0.0; Execute Quantitation - ON; <em>m/z</em> Tolerance - −5.0/+5.0; Tolerance Type - ppm; R.T. range (min) - −0.5/+0.5; Top rank filter - ON; Main Node Filter - Main Isomer Peak; m-Score Threshold (Display) - 5.0; C-Score Threshold (Display) - 2.0; Fatty Acid Priority (FA Priority) - ON; ID Quality Filter (A, B, C, D). Adducts of +H, +NH4, +Na, +K, +H-H20, +H-2H20, +2H and –H, +HCOO, -2H were applied for positive- and negative-ion modes respectively. The following parameters were used for alignment: Search Type - Product; Experiment Type – LC-MS; Alignment Method - Mean; R.T. Tolerance - 0.25; Calculate Unassigned Peak Area - ON; Filter Type - New Filter; Top rank Filter -ON; Main Node Filter – All isomer peaks; m-score threshold - 5.0; ID Quality - A, B, C, D. All lipid species aligned using the <strong>LipidSearch </strong>software were filtered with predefined filtering criteria to remove false positives, and manually curated after computational analysis, being either accepted, rejected, or reassigned.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Polar Metabolites</strong></p><p>Metabolomic datasets of both positive- and negative-ion modes were processed independently using the<strong> Progenesis QI</strong> (version 2.4) data analysis software (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle upon Tyne, UK). The detailed workflow for data processing and analysis included data import, retention time (RT) alignment, experimental design setup, peak picking, deconvolution, identification of compounds, normalization and relative quantification<strong>[2]</strong>. The adduct ions of each feature (<em>m/z</em>, RT) were deconvoluted and identified primarily using an in-house developed metabolite database, <strong>EMBL-MCF</strong> (<a href='http://curatr.mcf.embl.de' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>http://curatr.mcf.embl.de</a>), <strong>MassBank of North America</strong> (<a href='https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/</a>), <strong>m/z cloud </strong>(<a href='https://www.mzcloud.org/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.mzcloud.org/</a>) mass spectral libraries using precursor ion accurate mass (MS) and fragmentation (MS/MS) patterns.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Proteins</strong></p><p>Proteomic datasets were processed using untargeted label-free processing and consensus workflows of<strong> Proteome Discoverer</strong> (version <strong>2.2.0.388</strong>) software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). MS data files were searched against <strong>UniProt </strong>Mus musculus protein database (downloaded on 15th September 2019, containing 17422 target sequences and 9908993 residues) using Sequest HT search algorithm. The search was performed with peptide precursor and fragment mass tolerance of 10 ppm, 0.02 Da respectively and trypsin as a protease with two missed cleavages and 1% false discovery rate (FDR). Search criteria also included carbamidomethylation of cysteine (57.021 Da) as fixed modification, N-terminal acetylation (42.011 Da) and methionine oxidation (15.995 Da) as variable modifications. Unique and razor peptides were considered, precursor abundance values were normalized with total peptide amount and replicate-based resampling imputed missing values. Abundance ratios (KO/WT) were calculated using the pairwise ratio method and significant values were obtained from ANOVA hypothesis test (based on the background population of proteins or peptides). The common external protein contaminant list (<strong>MaxQuant database</strong>) was used to mark contaminants in the result file.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Li Z, Lai ZW, Christiano R, Gazos-Lopes F, Walther TC, Farese RV Jr. Global Analyses of Selective Insulin Resistance in Hepatocytes Caused by Palmitate Lipotoxicity.

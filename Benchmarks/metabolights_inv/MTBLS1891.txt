Study Identifier	MTBLS1891
Study Title	Trans-tissue molecular coordination in high-intensity physical activity
Study Description	High-intensity physical activity has major impacts in different parts of the body. However, the regulatory coordination between different tissues to support the high physical demands is poorly understood. Here we performed a multi-omics analysis of blood plasma, urine and saliva from a group of firefighters that underwent a 45-minute intense exercise session. The plasma analysis showed signatures of tissue damage and acute repair response. This is accompanied by lipolysis, glycolysis and amino acid degradation towards supplying the high-energetic demands. Concomitantly, the urine analysis showed a strong regulation of the renin-angiotensin system towards excretion of catabolites, reabsorption of nutrients and balance of the body fluids. The saliva showed a decrease in inflammatory molecules and increase in antimicrobial peptides, possibly towards improving the circulatory and respiratory capacity. Overall, our data show signatures of coordinated action between different organs towards supplying the demands of high-intensity physical activity.

Study Protocol Description	<p> A total of 13 male firefighter volunteers with average age of 25±3 years old and average body mass index of 26.3±3.3 before exercise were recruited. The volunteers underwent an exercise session consisted of hiking outdoors over hilly terrain in Santa Clarita, CA, with full wildland firefighter gear that weighed from 9 to 20 kg. Volunteers’ blood was collected by phlebotomy and immediately placed on ice prior and at most 10 minutes after the exercise. Blood was drawn by professional phlebotomists from different arms for baseline and after exercise sampling to avoid local responses related to the drawing process. The skin surface was wiped with antiseptic tissue before the puncture to minimized contamination. Blood was drawn into one 6-mL ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-coated vacuum tubes (BD Vacuntainer), mixed by inverting the tube 8-10 and immediately placed on ice. Plasma was then separated by centrifugation at 1,000 x g for 10 min at room temperature. Collected plasma were visually inspected for coagulation and hemolysis, collected into 0.5-mL aliquots, and stored within 1 h of blood drawing. For the saliva collection, volunteers rinsed their mouths 3 times for 30 seconds each with water and then dripped the saliva directly into the tube without spitting to avoid contamination with other fluids of the respiratory and gastroesophageal systems and placed on ice. Urine was collected by directly urinating into tubes and placed on ice. Immediately after collection, plasma, saliva, and urine samples were stored at -80 ºC. Sample collection, preparation and instrument run orders were randomized to minimize confounding factors.</p>	<p>Biofluids were transferred into 2-mL Sorenson MμlTI™ SafeSeal™ tubes (Sorenson BioScience, Inc., Salt Lake City, UT) with heavy isotope internal standard mix, GC-IS (1 mg/mL in water each 2H4-malonic acid, 2H4-succinic acid, 2H5-glycine, 2H4-citric acid, 13C6-fructose, 2H5-tryptophan, 2H4-lysine, 2H7-alanine, 2H35-stearic acid, 2H5-benzoic acid, 2H15-octanoic acid): plasma - 1:1 (v;v, sample:GC-IS), urine - 4:1 (v;v, sample:GC-IS). Urine was further treated with 1 mg/mL urease at 37°C with mild shaking (500 rpm) for 30 mins and incubated on ice for 1 min. Plasma sample was also spiked with 10 μL SPLASH mix (in methanol). A 2:1 (v:v) chloroform/methanol mix was added to the samples to make a ratio of 8:4:3 (v:v:v) chloroform/methanol/water. Samples were then vortexed and incubated on an ice block for 5 min. The layers were separated by centrifugation at 12,000 xg for 10 min at 4ºC. For plasma samples, 66.7 μL (from a total 500 μL extraction volume) of the lower lipid layer transferred to a glass autosampler vial and dried in a centrifugal vacuum concentrator. The upper liquid layer and the rest of the bottom layer was transferred to another autosampler vial for metabolites and dried. For the other biofluids, upper and lower phases were collected into separated files. The dried metabolites were capped and stored at -20 ºC for metabolomics analysis. The dried lipids had 500 μL 2:1chloroform:methanol added, capped and stored for lipidomics analysis. The protein pellet was washed with 1 mL of ice-cold methanol and centrifuged to pellet, supernatant removed and allowed to dry in a fume hood. Proteins were dissolved in 8 M urea prepared in 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0 and concentration was measured by BCA Protein Assay (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA). Disulfide bonds were reduced for 1 h at 37 °C with 5 mM dithiothreitol (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) from a 500 mM stock solution. Reduced cysteine residues were alkylated by adding 500 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) to a final concentration of 10 mM and incubating in the dark at 25 ºC for 45 min. Samples were diluted 4 fold with 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0 to and digested with lysyl-C endopeptidase (Wako) at 1:50 enzyme to substrate ratio at 25 ºC for 2 h. The same amount of sequencing-grade modified trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI) was added to the samples for 14 h incubation at 25 °C. The reaction was stopped by acidifying the samples with 100% formic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) to a final concentration of 1% formic acid, extracted in C18 SepPak cartridges (Waters, Milford, MA) and dried in a centrifugal vacuum concentrator (ThermoFisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA).</p><p><br></p><p>Metabolites were also extracted with methanol for a more comprehensive coverage. Plasma and urine samples were spiked with GC-IS as described above. Urine was also treated with urease. Eight volumes of ice-cold methanol were added, and samples were vortexed for 10 sec and placed in ice blocks for 10 min and vortexed for 3 min. Centrifuged at 15,000 xg at 4 ºC for 10 min and the supernatant was transferred to labeled autosampler vials and dried in a centrifugal vacuum concentrator. Dried metabolites were capped and stored at -20 ºC for further metabolomics analysis.</p>	<p>GC-MS Metabolomics: Samples were run in an Agilent GC 7890A using a HP-5ms GC (0.25 µm, 0.25 mm x 30 m; Agilent Technologies) column coupled with a single quadrupole MSD 5975C (Agilent Technologies). The GC temperature gradient started at 60 °C, with a hold of temperature for 1 min after injection, followed by increase to 325 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min and a 5-minute hold at this temperature.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>LC-MS Lipidomics: Lipids were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. The lower organic layer of the Folch extraction was reconstituted in MeOH and analyzed using a Waters NanoAquity UPLC system (Waters column, HSS T3 1.0 mm × 150 mm × 1.8 μm particle size). Unique lipid species were separated chromatographically over a 90 min gradient elution (mobile phase A: ACN/H2O (40:60) containing 10 mM ammonium acetate; mobile phase B: ACN/IPA (10:90) containing 10 mM ammonium acetate) at a flow rate of 30 μl/min.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Dautel SE, Kyle JE, Clair G, Sontag RL, Weitz KK, Shukla AK, et al. Lipidomics reveals dramatic lipid compositional changes in the maturing postnatal lung. Sci Rep. 2017;7:40555. DOI 10.1038/srep40555.</p>	<p>GC-MS Metabolomics: Samples were run in a single quadrupole MSD 5975C (Agilent Technologies). Scan m/z range : 50-500. Electron impact at eV 70.</p><p><br></p><p>LC-MS Lipidomics: Lipids were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, a Velos-ETD Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA). Data dependent scan events occurred both in the ion trap (collision-induced dissociation, CID) and Orbitrap (HCD) using normalized collision energy (NCE) of 30 and 35 arbitrary units, respectively, in the same run as outlined previously. Scan events were between the mass ranges of m/z 200–2000 for both positive and negative ionization. A full scan event occurring in the Orbitrap with a resolving power of 60,000. The full scan event is followed by MS/MS of the top 6 ions, alternating between HCD (resolving power of 7500) and CID, respectively, with an isolation width of 2 and dynamic exclusion of 60 sec. The ion time for the full scan MSn was 500 ms, for MS/MS in the Orbitrap (HCD) 1000 ms, and MS/MS in the ion trap (CID) 50 ms. The automatic gain control for the full scan was 1,000,000, for HCD MS/MS 50,000, and for CID MS/MS 10,000.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Dautel SE, Kyle JE, Clair G, Sontag RL, Weitz KK, Shukla AK, et al. Lipidomics reveals dramatic lipid compositional changes in the maturing postnatal lung. Sci Rep. 2017;7:40555. DOI 10.1038/srep40555.</p>	<p>GC-MS raw data files were processed using the Metabolite Detector software. Retention indices of detected metabolites were calculated based on the analysis of a FAMEs mixture, followed by their chromatographic alignment across all analyses after deconvolution. LC-MD raw data: Aligned features were manually verified and peak apex intensity values were exported for statistical analysis.</p>	<p>GC-MS Metabolomics: Identification of molecules were done by matching against the FiehnLib library with additional in-house entries and the NIST17/Wiley 11 GC-MS spectral databases.</p><p>LC-MS Lipidomics: Identifications were manually validated based on the MS/MS spectra (diagnostic and corresponding acyl chain fragments), the precursor isotopic profile, extracted ion chromatogram, mass measurement accuracy and elution time.</p>

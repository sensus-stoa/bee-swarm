Study Identifier	MTBLS1925
Study Title	EMT alterations in the solute carrier landscape uncover SLC22A10/A15 imposed vulnerabilities in pancreatic cancer
Study Description	<p>The involvement of membrane-bound solute carriers (SLCs) in neoplastic&nbsp;transdifferentiation&nbsp;processes is poorly defined. Here, we examined changes in the SLC landscape during epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of pancreatic cancer cells. We show that two SLCs from the organic anion/cation transporter family, SLC22A10 and SLC22A15, favor EMT via&nbsp;interferon&nbsp;(IFN)&nbsp;α&nbsp;and γ signaling activation of receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1) expression. In addition, SLC22A10 and SLC22A15 allow tumor cell accumulation of&nbsp;glutathione&nbsp;to support EMT via the IFNα/γ-ROR1 axis. Moreover, a pan-SLC22A inhibitor lesinurad reduces EMT-induced metastasis and&nbsp;gemcitabine&nbsp;chemoresistance to prolong survival in mouse models of pancreatic cancer, thus identifying new vulnerabilities for human PDAC.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>PANC-1</strong> (human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma) cells were transduced with lentiviral particles harboring the DNAs for overexpression (OE) of vector, <strong>SLC22A10</strong> and <strong>SLC22A15</strong> (pLenti-III-Blank,&nbsp;pLV-puro-SLC22A10-t1-Myc and pLV-puro-SLC22A15-Myc), for 48 h. Stable clones of <strong>pLV-Vector</strong>, <strong>pLV-SLC22A10-OE</strong> and <strong>pLV-SLC22A15-OE</strong> in PANC-1 background were obtained after puromycin selection of the above transduced cells.</p><p><br></p><p>Samples for metabolomics study were prepared based on previously published methods <strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, vector-transduced and SLC22A10- and SLC22A15-overexpressing clones were seeded at 1 x 10^6 cells per 60 mm Petri dish (n = 5) and grown to 80% confluence. The culture media were changed 12 h prior to metabolite extraction.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Liu X, Ser Z, Cluntun AA, Mentch SJ, Locasale JW. A strategy for sensitive, large scale quantitative metabolomics. J Vis Exp. 2014 May 27;(87):51358. doi:10.3791/51358. PMID:24894601.</p>	<p>The growth media was aspirated, the plates were placed on dry ice, 2 mL of prechilled 80% methanol (-80 °C) was added to the cells, and the plates were kept at -80 °C for 15 min. Cells and extraction solvent were then harvested using a cell scraper, collected in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 20,000 × g, 4 °C for 10 min. Solvent in the supernatant was evaporated in a speed vacuum concentrator at room temperature. Dried metabolites were reconstituted in 100 µL of water containing 0.1% formic acid and centrifuged at 20,000 × g, 4 °C for 10 min.</p>	<p>Cleared samples were filled into glass autosampler vials for Q-TOF LC-MS/MS analyses. LC-MS/MS analyses were performed on an Agilent 6545 Q-TOF (Santa Clara, CA) and an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC system. A total of 5 µL of each sample was separated on a Kinetex PS C18 column (2.6 µm, 100 Å, 100 × 2.1 mm, Phenomenex). The mobile phase used for the chromatographic separation consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), and the flow rate was 200 µL/min. The chromatographic gradient conditions were optimized as follows: 0-3 min of 2% B, 11.5&nbsp;min of 45% B, 13 min of 90% B, 20 min of 90% B, 25 min of 2% B and 30 min of 2% B.&nbsp;</p>	<p>Data were acquired in positive electrospray ionization mode. The mass range was set from 50 to 1700 m/z. The ion source parameters and the mass spectrometry operating conditions were as follows: gas temperature, 250 °C; source gas flow, 8&nbsp;L/min; nebulizer, 25&nbsp;psi; sheath gas temperature, 350 °C; sheath gas flow, 10&nbsp;L/min; capillary voltage, 3,500&nbsp;V (positive ion mode); and nozzle voltage, 500&nbsp;V (positive ion mode). Features were selected using <strong>data-dependent acquisition</strong> (DDA), in which the top 5 ions at a given time point were retained, used to generate a mass spectral profile, and then excluded for a 30 s window.</p>	<p>Raw data files were transformed into <strong>.mzML</strong> files for subsequent analysis following the procedure for 'Mass Spectrometry File Conversion' described at <strong>GNPS</strong> documentation (<a href='https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/fileconversion' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/fileconversion</a>).</p>	<p>Features were identified using <strong>Scaffold Elements v2.1.1</strong>. Significant features after <strong>familywise error rate (FWER)</strong> correction were retained for downstream analyses. Features were identified by searching against the <strong>METLIN</strong> Metabolite and Chemical Entity Database, <strong>NIST</strong> database and <strong>HMDB</strong> metabolite library. Data were filtered based on an ID-score &gt;= 0.8 and a log10 intensity &gt;= 6. Tentatively identified metabolites were imported into <strong>MetaboAnalyst[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Chong J, Soufan O, Li C, Caraus I, Li S, Bourque G, Wishart DS, Xia J. MetaboAnalyst 4.0: towards more transparent and integrative metabolomics analysis. Nucleic Acids Res. 2018 Jul 2;46(W1):W486-W494. doi:10.1093/nar/gky310. PMID:29762782.</p>

Study Identifier	MTBLS1956
Study Title	Cell-to-cell heterogeneity drives host-virus coexistence in a bloom-forming alga
Study Description	<p>Algal blooms drive global biogeochemical cycles of key nutrients in the oceans and serve as hotspots for biological interactions. The massive spring blooms of the cosmopolitan coccolithophore <em>Emiliania huxleyi </em>(<em>E. huxleyi</em>) are often infected by the lytic <em>Emiliania huxleyi</em> specific virus (EhV) which is a major mortality agent triggering bloom demise. Nonetheless, the multi-annual 'boom and bust' pattern of<em> E. huxleyi</em> suggests that mechanisms of coexistence are essential for these host-virus dynamics. To investigate host-virus coexistence, we developed a new model system from an <em>E. huxleyi</em> culture which recovered from viral infection. The recovered population coexists with the virus, as host cells continue to grow in parallel to viral production. By applying a single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH) approach to quantify the fraction of infected cells and assessing infection-specific lipid biomarkers, we identified a small subpopulation (5-7% of cells) that was infected and produced new virions, whereas the majority of the host population could resist infection. To further assess population heterogeneity, we generated monoclonal strain collections using single-cell sorting and subsequently phenotyped their susceptibility to EhV infection. This unraveled a substantial cell-to-cell heterogeneity across a continuum of susceptibility to resistance, suggesting that infection outcomes may vary depending on the individual cell. These results add a new dimension to our understanding of the complexity of host-virus interactions that are commonly assessed in bulk and described by binary definitions of resistance or susceptibility. We propose that phenotypic heterogeneity drives <em>E. huxleyi-</em>EhV coexistence and may potentially provide the coexisting strain an ecological advantage by killing competing susceptible strains.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Cultures of&nbsp;the virus-susceptible strain <strong><em>E. huxleyi&nbsp;</em>CCMP 2090</strong>, the monoclonal resistant isolate <strong>2090-BD5</strong> and the coexisting resistant culture <strong>2090-Rec</strong> (recovered following infection with the virus EhV-201) were analyzed for their cellular lipid composition with and without addition of EhV-201 in 3 technical replicates (except for 2090-BD5, for which 2 replicates were analyzed). Cultures were grown in 1 L filtered seawater (FSW) with modified K/2 and the antibiotics ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and kanamycin (50 μg/mL) at 18 °C with a 16:8 h light:dark cycle and a light intensity of 100 μmol photons/m^2/s. Samples of <strong style='font-style: inherit;'>2090</strong>, <strong style='font-style: inherit;'>2090-BD5</strong> and <strong style='font-style: inherit;'>2090-Rec</strong> were collected during exponential growth phase, while <strong style='font-style: inherit;'>2090</strong> inoculated with <strong style='font-style: inherit;'>EhV-201</strong> was collected during culture lysis at 3 dpi. The samples (150-200 mL of each culture, equivalent to ~1-3 x 10^8&nbsp;cells per sample)&nbsp;were collected by vacuum filtration onto pre-combusted glass microfiber filters (grade GF/A, 47 mm in diameter,&nbsp;GE Healthcare&nbsp;Whatman),&nbsp;plunged into liquid nitrogen, lyophilized to dryness and stored at -80 °C until extraction.</p>	<p>Lipid extraction was performed as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>&nbsp;with slight modifications. Briefly, filters were placed in 15 mL glass tubes and extracted with <strong>3 mL</strong> of a pre-cooled (-20 °C) <strong>methanol:methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE, 1:3, ν:ν)</strong> solution, containing a sphingolipid standard mixture with <strong>146 nM of the glucosyl(β) ceramide d18:1/c12:0</strong>, which was used as <strong>internal standard</strong>. The samples were shaken for 30 min at 4 °C and sonicated for 30 min. The samples were then supplemented with <strong>1.5 mL water:methanol (3:1, ν:ν)</strong> solution, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged for 10 min at 3200 x g and 4 °C to obtain phase separation. The upper organic phase (1.5 mL) was transferred to 2 mL centrifuge tubes and dried under a flow of nitrogen&nbsp;(TurboVap LV, Biotage, Uppsala, Sweden). The polar phase was re-extracted with <strong>1.5 mL</strong> of <strong>MTBE</strong>. The upper organic phase (2.5 mL) was combined with the organic phase from the first extraction and dried under a flow of nitrogen. The samples were&nbsp;stored at -80 °C until UPLC-HRMS analysis. One blank filter was processed as extraction <strong>blank</strong>. Samples were thawed, re-dissolved in 200 μL acetonitrile:isopropanol (7:3) with 1% 1 M ammonium acetate and 0.1% acetic acid (LC-MS mobile phase B), vortexed, sonicated for 10 min&nbsp;and centrifuged at 20,800 x g for&nbsp;10 min&nbsp;at 10 °C. The&nbsp;supernatants were transferred to 200&nbsp;μL glass inserts in autosampler vials and directly used for LC-MS analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Schleyer G, Kuhlisch C, Ziv C, Ben-Dor S, Malitsky S, Schatz D, Vardi A. 2023. Lipid biomarkers for algal resistance to viral infection in the ocean. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A. 2023 Jul 4;120(27):e2217121120. doi:10.1073/pnas.2217121120. PMID:37364132.</p>	<p>An aliquot of 1 µL was analyzed using UPLC coupled to a photodiode detector (<strong>Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class System</strong>) and a quadrupole time-of-flight (QToF) mass spectrometer (SYNAPT G2 HDMS, Waters) as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, the chromatographic separation was performed on an <strong>ACQUITY UPLC BEH C8 (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm x 100 mm; Waters)</strong> column attached to an <strong>ACQUITY UPLC BEH C8 VanGuard Pre-column (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm x 5 mm; Waters)</strong>. Mobile phase A consisted of water with 1% 1 M ammonium acetate, 0.1% acetic acid. Mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile:isopropanol (7:3) with 1% 1 M ammonium acetate, 0.1% acetic acid. The column was maintained at 40 °C and the flow rate of the mobile phase was 0.4 mL/min. The chromatographic gradient was set as follows: 1 min, 45% A; 4 min, 35% A; 12 min, 11% A, 15 min, 0% A, after which the column was first washed with 100% mobile phase B for 4 min and then returned to initial conditions over 0.5 min and equilibrated for 2.5 min (22 min total run time). The PDA detector was set to 210-800 nm. A divert valve (Rheodyne) excluded 0-1 min and 20-22 min from injection to the mass spectrometer.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Schleyer G, Kuhlisch C, Ziv C, Ben-Dor S, Malitsky S, Schatz D, Vardi A. Lipid biomarkers for algal resistance to viral infection in the ocean. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A. 2023 Jul 4;120(27):e2217121120. doi:10.1073/pnas.2217121120. Epub 2023 Jun 26. PMID:37364132.</p>	<p class='ql-align-justify'>Eluting compounds were detected using a <strong>Waters SYNAPT G2 HDMS</strong> Quadrupole Time-of-Flight mass spectrometer. The ESI source was set to 120 °C source and 400 °C desolvation temperature, 1.0 kV capillary voltage, and 40 eV cone voltage, using nitrogen as desolvation gas (800 L/h) and cone gas (20 L/h). Full scan and all ion fragmentation (MSE) analysis were performed in <strong>positive ionization mode</strong> (25,000 at m/z 556) over a <strong style='font-style: inherit;'>mass range of 50-1200 Da</strong> alternating with 0.1 min scan time between low (1 eV) and high (ramp of 15-35 eV) collision energy. Leucine-enkephalin was used as lock-mass reference standard. </p>	<p class='ql-align-justify'>Peak areas of indicative masses ([M+Na]+ for hGSL and vGSL species, [M+H-Sialic acid]+ for sGSL species) above a signal-to-noise threshold of 3 (limit of detection) were normalized to the internal standard and the total number of extracted cells. All LC-MS files were converted from the vendor’s format to the open-format ‘<strong>netCDF</strong>’ using ‘<strong>DataBridge</strong>’ (<strong>MassLynx version 4.1</strong>).</p>	<p class='ql-align-justify'>The lipid markers hGSLs, sGSLs and vGSLs were annotated based on their accurate mass and LC-MS/MS fragmentation pattern<strong>[1]-[3]</strong>.&nbsp;The data were analyzed and processed with <strong>MassLynx</strong> and <strong>QuanLynx (version 4.1, Waters)</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong>&nbsp;Fulton, J.

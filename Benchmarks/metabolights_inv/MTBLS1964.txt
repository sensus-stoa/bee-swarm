Study Identifier	MTBLS1964
Study Title	Insights into Metabolic Changes Caused by the Trichoderma virens-Maize Root Interaction
Study Description	The interactions of crops with root-colonizing endophytic microorganisms are highly relevant to agriculture, because endophytes can modify plant resistance to pests and increase crop yields. We investigated the interactions between the host plant <i>Zea mays</i> and the endophytic fungus <i>Trichoderma virens</i> at 5 days postinoculation grown in a hydroponic system. Wild-type <i>T. virens</i> and two knockout mutants, with deletion of the genes <i>tv2og1</i> or <i>vir4</i> involved in specialized metabolism, were analyzed. Root colonization by the fungal mutants was lower than that by the wild type. All fungal genotypes suppressed root biomass. Metabolic fingerprinting of roots, mycelia, and fungal culture supernatants was performed using ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography coupled to diode array detection and quadrupole time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometry. The metabolic composition of <i>T. virens</i>-colonized roots differed profoundly from that of noncolonized roots, with the effects depending on the fungal genotype. In particular, the concentrations of several metabolites derived from the shikimate pathway, including an amino acid and several flavonoids, were modulated. The expression levels of some genes coding for enzymes involved in these pathways were affected if roots were colonized by the ∆<i>vir4</i> genotype of <i>T. virens</i>. Furthermore, mycelia and fungal culture supernatants of the different <i>T. virens</i> genotypes showed distinct metabolomes. Our study highlights the fact that colonization by endophytic <i>T. virens</i> leads to far-reaching metabolic changes, partly related to two fungal genes. Both metabolites produced by the fungus and plant metabolites modulated by the interaction probably contribute to these metabolic patterns. The metabolic changes in plant tissues may be interlinked with systemic endophyte effects often observed in later plant developmental stages.[Formula: see text] Copyright © 2021 The Author(s). Study Protocol Description	<p>3 genotypes of the <strong><em>T. virens</em></strong> strain<strong> Gv29.8-the wild type</strong> (<strong>WT</strong>); <strong>Δ<em>tv2og1</em>[1]</strong>; and <strong>Δ<em>vir4</em></strong>, produced following the methodology previously described<strong>[2] </strong>for the deletion of <strong><em>T. virens</em> </strong>terpene synthases-were propagated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) (Difco, Fisher Scientific, Newington, NH, U.S.A.) at 25 °C under a cycle of 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness for 7 d to induce <strong>conidia </strong>formation (see <strong>Fig. S1</strong> in the paper associated with this study). <strong>Conidia </strong>were collected in sterile Nanopure water and filtered through a double layer of sterile Miracloth (Millipore Merck, Boston, MA, U.S.A.).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Seeds </strong>of<strong> <em>Zea mays</em></strong> of the hybrid line <strong>34H31 </strong>(Pioneer Brand Products, Gisborne, New Zealand) were surface sterilized with 2% (wt/vol) sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) and 70% ethanol as previously described<strong>[3]</strong>. Sterilized <strong>seeds </strong>were left untreated (as <strong>control</strong>) or inoculated with <strong>conidial suspensions</strong> (approximately 1 x 10^6 conidia/seed) of one of the genotypes of <strong><em>T. virens</em></strong> (<strong>WT</strong>, <strong><em>Δtv2og1</em></strong>, or <strong><em>Δvir4</em></strong>), and germinated for 2.5 d, as previously described<strong>[3]</strong>. The 2.5-d-old <strong>seedlings </strong>were transferred to hydroponic conditions without aeration in 50 mL tubes containing 45 mL of sterile Hoagland’s number 2 Basal Salt Mixture (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, U.S.A.) supplemented with 0.5% (wt/vol) sucrose with a piece of sterile cotton to support the <strong>seedling</strong>. <strong>Seedlings </strong>were set up in a randomized complete block design and incubated for an additional 2.5 d in a plant growth incubator (Humidity Control Versatile Environmental Test Chamber, Sanyo, Moriguchi, Japan) at 25 °C under a cycle of 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness and relative humidity of 80%.</p><p><br></p><p>For metabolic fingerprinting of maize <strong>roots</strong>, untreated plants (used as <strong>control</strong>) and plants <strong>inoculated with <em>T. virens</em> WT</strong>, <strong><em>Δtv2og1</em></strong>, or <strong><em>Δvir4</em> </strong>were grown as described above (<em>n</em> = 10 biological replicates). The whole <strong>root </strong>system was excised, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at -80 °C, freeze-dried (Thermo Savant Micro Modulyo-115; Thermo Fisher Scientific,Waltham, MA, U.S.A.), and ground to a fine powder (1600 Mini6; SPEX SamplePrep, Metuchen, NJ, U.S.A.).</p><p><br></p><p>For chemical analyses of intra- and extracellular fungal metabolites, samples of mycelium and fungal culture supernatants were collected from cultures of the different fungal genotypes (<em>n</em> = 10 biological replicates). <strong>Conidia</strong> of <strong><em>T. virens</em></strong> (<strong>WT</strong>, <strong><em>Δtv2og1</em></strong>, or <strong><em>Δvir4</em></strong>) were harvested from 7-d-old PDA plates in 5 mL of sterile Nanopure water, filtered through two layers of sterile Miracloth, and incubated in 100 mL sterile flasks with 50 mL of Hoagland’s number 2 Basal Salt Mixture supplemented with 0.5% sucrose. Flasks were incubated using a randomized complete block design at 25 °C with shaking at 150 rpm (Ratek, Victoria, Australia). After 72 h, cultures were filtered through 2 layers of sterile Miracloth to separate the <strong>mycelia </strong>from the <strong>supernatants</strong>. <strong>Mycelia </strong>samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. <strong>Supernatants </strong>were filtered through 0.2 µm membranes (GVS Filter Technology, Morecambe, U.K.) and stored at -80 °C. <strong>Mycelia </strong>and <strong>supernatant </strong>samples were freeze-dried. Fungal tissue was ground to a fine powder. Samples of <strong>uninoculated sterile culture media</strong> (<em>n</em> = 10) were additionally stored for background subtraction.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Nogueira-López G, Rostás M, Steyaert JM, Hampton J, and Mendoza-Mendoza A, unpublished</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Nogueira-Lopez G, Greenwood DR, Middleditch M, Winefield C, Eaton C, Steyaert JM, Mendoza-Mendoza A. PMID:31921006</p>	<p>For metabolic fingerprinting, 4 and 6 mg (±<strong> </strong>0.1 mg) of (colonized) <strong>root </strong>and <strong>mycelial </strong>powder, respectively, were used. For the analysis of fungal culture <strong>supernatants</strong>, the freeze-dried material generated from the 50 mL cultures was used. Samples were extracted 3-fold on ice using 3 x 150 µL (<strong>roots</strong>), 3 x 50 µL (<strong>mycelia</strong>), or 3 x 200 µl (<strong>supernatants</strong>) of 90% ice-cooled methanol (LC-MS grade; Fisher Scientific, Loughborough, U.K.) containing hydrocortisone (&gt;98%; Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany) as internal standard. Samples were vortexed for 5 min and centrifuged for 10 min (13,200 rpm, 4 °C). Supernatants were pooled and filtered using 0.2 µm filters (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, U.S.A.). For each type of sample (<strong>roots</strong>, <strong>mycelia</strong>, and fungal culture <strong>supernatants</strong>), 3 to 4 blanks were prepared.</p>	<p>Samples and blanks were analyzed using UHPLC-DAD-QTOF-MS/MS (UHPLC: Dionex UltiMate 3000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, San José, CA, U.S.A.; and QTOF: compact; Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Compounds were separated at 45 °C on a Kinetex XB-C18 column (1.7 µm, 150 x 2.1 mm, with a precolumn; Phenomenex). Separation was performed using a gradient from eluent A (0.1% formic acid [FA; eluent additive for LC-MS, approximately 98%; Sigma-Aldrich] in Millipore-H2O) to eluent B (0.1% FA in acetonitrile [LC-MS grade; Fisher Scientific]) at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min with 2% B to 30% B within 20 min, 30% B to 75% B within 9 min, followed by column washing and equilibration.</p>	<p>DAD spectra (210 to 400 nm) as well as mass line spectra (ESI- mode, 8 Hz spectra rate, 50 to 1,500 <em>m/z</em> in both MS and MS/MS mode) were recorded. The MS parameters were end plate offset 500 V, capillary voltage 3,000 V, nebulizer (N2) pressure 3 bar, N2 dry gas flow 12 L/min at 275 °C, quadrupole ion energy 4 eV, low mass 90 <em>m/z</em>, and collision energy 7 eV. MS/MS spectra were acquired in AutoMS/MS mode using N2 as collision gas and 25 eV collision energy. A sodium formate-based calibration solution was pumped into the sprayer prior to each sample for mass axis recalibration. In total, <em>n</em> = 9 mycelia and <em>n</em> = 8 fungal culture supernatant samples were measured.</p>	<p>No data transformation was supplied for this study.</p>	<p>Mass axis recalibration was done individually for each sample. Picking of molecular features and spectral background subtraction were performed using the Find Molecular Features algorithm in <strong>Compass DataAnalysis 4.4</strong> (Bruker Daltonics) with the following settings: signal-to-noise ratio 3, correlation coefficient threshold 0.75, minimum compound length 25 (<strong>roots</strong>) or 30 (<strong>mycelia </strong>and <strong>supernatant</strong>), smoothing width 7, allowing common adducts and neutral losses for bucket generation. Features were aligned across all samples (including <strong>root</strong>, <strong>mycelia</strong>, and <strong>fungal </strong>culture supernatant samples) with <strong>Compass ProfileAnalysis 2.3 </strong>(Bruker Daltonics), allowing deviations of 0.1 min (retention time) and 5 mDa (m/z), and using the <em>m/z </em>with the highest intensity as the bucketing basis. The peak heights of the features (each characterized by a retention time and<em> m/z</em> value) were normalized to the peak heights of the FA adduct of hydrocortisone. 2 filtering steps were applied separately for each data set (i.e., colonized <strong>roots</strong>, <strong>mycelia</strong>, and <strong>fungal </strong>culture supernatants). A feature was maintained in the corresponding data set, if <strong>(i)</strong> its mean intensity in at least one treatment group was more than 50 times higher than its mean intensity in the blanks and <strong>(ii)</strong> it occurred in at least half of the replicates of at least one treatment group. For <strong>supernatant </strong>samples, those features that were present in the noninoculated culture media were not further considered (151 features).</p><p><br></p><p>Data were normalized to the dry weights of the samples (except for the <strong>supernatant </strong>samples). For each data set, a PCA was performed using <strong>R 3.4.1 </strong>(R Core Team). For the PCAs, 0's were replaced by random small numbers (10_13 to 10_12) and data autoscaled (i.e., mean centering and scaling to unit variance). For further pairwise group comparisons, only those features which occurred in at least half of the samples of at least 1 of the 2 treatment groups were retained. Volcano plots were plotted in <strong>Matlab </strong>(<strong>7.10.0.499</strong>; The MathWorks, Natick, MA, U.S.A.) based on fold changes of features (i.e., mean concentrations in the treatment group divided by mean concentrations in the control group) and P values resulting from Mann-Whitney U tests. For features that only occurred in one of the treatment groups, fold changes were set to the maximum fold change (for decrease or increase) observed in the corresponding data set. To compare the numbers of features decreased versus increased in pool size, c2 tests were performed in <strong>R</strong>. Venn diagrams were generated in <strong>R </strong>using the <strong>ggplots </strong>package. For identification of metabolites in the colonized roots, the annotation tools of <strong>Data Analysis 4.4 </strong>and<strong> MetaboScape 2.0</strong> (Bruker Daltonics) were used. Molecular formulas of parent ions and fragments were generated using <strong>Smart Formula 3D</strong> and ranked according to their <em>m/z</em> and isotope pattern fit. For the most likely formulas, suggestions for structural formulas were derived from <strong>Compound Crawler </strong>and <em>in silico</em> fragmentation and matching against the <strong>MassBank of North America in MetFrag[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p>Moreover, dominant ion types, <em>m/z</em>, isotopic patterns, retention times, UV/VIS spectra, and MS/MS spectra were matched against an in-house database containing diverse primary and specialized metabolites that were measured under the same conditions. Ion types and<em> m/z </em>values of benzoxazinoids were additionally compared with those previously given<strong>[2][3]</strong>. Features were screened for MS/MS similarities to known compounds to support metabolite identifications. For this, <strong>MetFamily[4]</strong> as well as the bucket correlation statistics and the MS/MS bucket match function of <strong>MetaboScape </strong>were used. The concentrations of the identified metabolites were compared between treatment groups within each data set by Kruskal-Wallis tests followed by post hoc Kruskalmc tests in <strong>R </strong>(package <strong>pgirmess</strong>).</p><p><br></p><p>Metabolic pathways for identified metabolites were previously obtained<strong>[5][6][7]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Ruttkies C, Schymanski EL, Wolf S, Hollender J, Neumann S.

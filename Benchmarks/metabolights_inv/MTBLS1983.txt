Study Identifier	MTBLS1983
Study Title	Generation of a chromosome-scale genome assembly of the insect-repellent terpenoid-producing Lamiaceae species, Callicarpa americana
Study Description	<p><strong>BACKGROUND:</strong> Plants exhibit wide chemical diversity due to the production of specialized metabolites that function as pollinator attractants, defensive compounds, and signaling molecules. Lamiaceae (mints) are known for their chemodiversity and have been cultivated for use as culinary herbs, as well as sources of insect repellents, health-promoting compounds, and fragrance.</p><p><strong>FINDINGS:</strong> We report the chromosome-scale genome assembly of Callicarpa americana L. (American beautyberry), a species within the early-diverging Callicarpoideae clade of Lamiaceae, known for its metallic purple fruits and use as an insect repellent due to its production of terpenoids. Using long-read sequencing and Hi-C scaffolding, we generated a 506.1-Mb assembly spanning 17 pseudomolecules with N50 contig and N50 scaffold sizes of 7.5 and 29.0 Mb, respectively. In all, 32,164 genes were annotated, including 53 candidate terpene synthases and 47 putative clusters of specialized metabolite biosynthetic pathways. Our analyses revealed 3 putative whole-genome duplication events, which, together with local tandem duplications, contributed to gene family expansion of terpene synthases. Kolavenyl diphosphate is a gateway to many of the bioactive terpenoids in C. americana; experimental validation confirmed that CamTPS2 encodes kolavenyl diphosphate synthase. Syntenic analyses with Tectona grandis L. f. (teak), a member of the Tectonoideae clade of Lamiaceae known for exceptionally strong wood resistant to insects, revealed 963 collinear blocks and 21,297 C. americana syntelogs.</p><p><strong>CONCLUSIONS:</strong> Access to the C. americana genome provides a road map for rapid discovery of genes encoding plant-derived agrichemicals and a key resource for understanding the evolution of chemical diversity in Lamiaceae.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>DiTPS candidates and reference genes were transiently expressed in <em>Nicotiana benthamiana</em> leaves as previously described in detail<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, to increase product accumulation, diterpene synthases (diTPS) were co-expressed with genes from the upstream pathway providing the substrate, CfDXS and CfGGPPS (<em>Cf, Coleus forskohlii</em>)<strong>[2][3]</strong>. Cultures containing different constructs were mixed in equal ratios to yield the appropriate combinations before infiltration into 4-5 weeks old plants with <em>Agrobacterium tumefaciens</em> bearing plasmids expressing diterpene synthases. Plants were grown for an additional 5 days before metabolite extraction. Leaf samples were collected by punching out 3 leaf discs, 24 mm diameter (approx. 65-70 mg fresh weight), from an infiltrated leaf and immediately placing these in a vial containing 1 ml n-hexane.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Johnson SR, Bhat WW, Bibik J, Turmo A, Hamberger B; Evolutionary Mint Genomics Consortium, Hamberger B. A database-driven approach identifies additional diterpene synthase activities in the mint family (Lamiaceae). J Biol Chem. 2019 Jan 25;294(4):1349-1362. doi:10.1074/jbc.RA118.006025. PMID:30498089.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Andersen-Ranberg J, Kongstad KT, Nielsen MT, Jensen NB, Pateraki I, Bach SS, Hamberger B, Zerbe P, Staerk D, Bohlmann J, Møller BL, Hamberger B. Expanding the Landscape of Diterpene Structural Diversity through Stereochemically Controlled Combinatorial Biosynthesis. Angew Chem Int Ed Engl. 2016 Feb 5;55(6):2142-6. doi:10.1002/anie.201510650. PMID:26749264.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Pateraki I, Andersen-Ranberg J, Hamberger B, Heskes AM, Martens HJ, Zerbe P, Bach SS, Møller BL, Bohlmann J, Hamberger B. Manoyl oxide (13R), the biosynthetic precursor of forskolin, is synthesized in specialized root cork cells in Coleus forskohlii. Plant Physiol. 2014 Mar;164(3):1222-36. doi:10.1104/pp.113.228429. PMID:24481136.</p>	<p>Diterpenes from the leaf samples were extracted in 1 ml n-hexane with 1 mg/l 1-eicosene as internal standard (IS) at room temperature overnight in an orbital shaker at 200 rpm. Plant material was collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm and the organic phase transferred to GC vials for analysis by GC-MS.</p>	<p>GC-MS analyses were performed on an Agilent 7890A GC with an Agilent VF-5ms column (30 m x 250 µm x 0.25 µm, with 10 m EZ-Guard) and an Agilent 5975C detector. The inlet was set to 275 °C splitless injection, He carrier gas with column flow of 1 ml/min for a 1 µl injection. The oven program was 40 °C hold 1 min, 40 °C/min to 200 °C and hold 4.5 min, 20 °C/min to 240 °C, 10 °C/min to 280 °C, 40 °C/min to 320 °C hold 3 min. The detector was activated after a 4-min solvent delay. All analyses were done in duplicate.</p>	<p>This experiment utilized a single quadrupole mass analyzer (Agilent 5975C). Ions were generated using electron ionization (70 eV) and acquired 0.3 s/scan over m/z 40-400. Data acquisition was performed using MSD ChemStation Data Analysis.</p>	<p>Data files from the instrument (.D) were converted to .RAW files for analysis in MassLynx. Figs were generated by a) graphing the collection of chromatogram points (from MassLynx) in Excel and b) creating graphs of the mass spectra using a script found here (<strong>jcamp_openchrom.py</strong>, <a href='https://zenodo.org/record/1323366 .XzW8_hNKiqA' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://zenodo.org/record/1323366 .XzW8_hNKiqA</a>).</p>	<p>Chromatograms were compared between the control samples (derived from leaves expressing only a terpene precursor generating enzyme, 'DXS-GGPPS') and the experimental samples (expressing the enzyme of interest along with DXS-GGPPS). Peaks which appeared in the experimental but not the control chromatograms (between 6-14 min, the window of diterpene elution according to our temperature program) were further inspected manually by searching for any that contained the m/z 272, a characteristic diterpene peak. Potential diterpene mass spectra were compared first with published mass spectra to provide a tentative ID and then with samples from leaves expressing known enzyme combinations to confirm ID. A peak of interest was considered a match to a known enzyme product if the retention time was within 5 s and the mass spectra were the same (see <strong>Fig. S2</strong> in the paper associated with this study).</p>

Study Identifier	MTBLS2016
Study Title	Comparison of lipidome profiles of Caenorhabditis elegans—results from an inter-laboratory ring trial
Study Description	<h4>Introduction</h4>Lipidomic profiling allows 100s if not 1000s of lipids in a sample to be detected and quantified. Modern lipidomics techniques are ultra-sensitive assays that enable the discovery of novel biomarkers in a variety of fields and provide new insight in mechanistic investigations. Despite much progress in lipidomics, there remains, as for all high throughput 'omics' strategies, the need to develop strategies to standardize and integrate quality control into studies in order to enhance robustness, reproducibility, and usability of studies within specific fields and beyond.<h4>Objectives</h4>We aimed to understand how much results from lipid profiling in the model organism Caenorhabditis elegans are influenced by different culture conditions in different laboratories.<h4>Methods</h4>In this work we have undertaken an inter-laboratory study, comparing the lipid profiles of N2 wild type C. elegans and daf-2(e1370) mutants lacking a functional insulin receptor. Sample were collected from worms grown in four separate laboratories under standardized growth conditions. We used an UPLC-UHR-ToF-MS system allowing chromatographic separation before MS analysis.<h4>Results</h4>We found common qualitative changes in several marker lipids in samples from the individual laboratories. On the other hand, even in this controlled experimental system, the exact fold-changes for each marker varied between laboratories.<h4>Conclusion</h4>Our results thus reveal a serious limitation to the reproducibility of current lipid profiling experiments and reveal challenges to the integration of such data from different laboratories.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Fresh aliquots of<strong>&nbsp;C. elegans&nbsp;wild type N2 Bristol </strong>and&nbsp;<strong>daf-2(e1370)&nbsp;</strong>strains were ordered from the&nbsp;Caenorhabditis elegans&nbsp;Genetics Center (CGC), and stocks from the same plate were sent to all participating laboratories. Experiments were performed within a few wks after arrival.</p><p><strong>C. elegans</strong>&nbsp;were cultured on nematode growth medium (NGM) agar plates using&nbsp;E. coli&nbsp;OP50 as sole food source in the 4 different <strong>laboratories </strong>participating in this trial. After age synchronization by bleaching, the worms were grown until the first day of adulthood, harvested and washed. Each culture <strong>replicate </strong>contained approximately 5000 adult worms. Samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −&nbsp;80&nbsp;°C until extraction. The exact protocols from each laboratory can be found in the <strong>SI </strong>in the paper associated with this study.</p>	<p>Lipids were extracted by a modified version of a MTBE extraction originally developed by Matyash et al.&nbsp;(2008)<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, nematodes were suspended in 500 µL methanol and homogenized in a Precellys Bead Beating system. Subsequently, the samples were transferred to 4&nbsp;mL glass vials. After addition of 1.7&nbsp;mL MTBE the samples were vortexed and incubated for 60&nbsp;min at room temperature. 420 µL water was added to induce phase separation. Samples were centrifuged at RCF of 15,294 × g&nbsp;and 4&nbsp;°C for 15&nbsp;min. The upper organic phase was transferred to fresh 4&nbsp;mL glass vials and the lower phase was re-extracted with additional 650&nbsp;µL MTBE. After centrifugation, the organic phases were combined and evaporated. The residue was reconstituted in 500 µL acetonitrile/isopropanol/water (65/30/5, v/v/v) and stored in 125 µL aliquots at −&nbsp;80&nbsp;°C until analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Matyash V, Liebisch G, Kurzchalia TV, Shevchenko A, Schwudke D. Lipid extraction by methyl-tert-butyl ether for high-throughput lipidomics. J Lipid Res. 2008 May;49(5):1137-46. doi: 10.1194/jlr.D700041-JLR200. Epub 2008 Feb 16. PMID: 18281723; PMCID: PMC2311442</p>	<p><strong>Lipid analysis: UPLC-UHR-ToF–MS</strong></p><p>Lipids were analyzed as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. Separation was achieved on a Waters CORTECS UPLC C18 (150 mm x 2.1 mm ID, 1.6 µm particle size) column using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system. Composition of eluent A was 40% H2O / 60% ACN + 10 mM ammonium formate / 0.1 % formic acid, while composition of eluent B was 10% ACN / 90% iPrOH + 10 mM ammonium formate / 0.1 % formic acid. The following gradient was used: 68/32 at 0 min, 68/32 at 1.5 min, 3/97 at 21 min, 3/97 at 25 min, 68/32 at 25.1 min with a flow rate of 0.250 mL/min and a temperature of 40 °C.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Lipid analysis: UHPLC-TIMS-ToF–MS</strong></p><p>Lipids separation was achieved on a Bruker intensity C18 column (100 mm x 2.1 mm, 1.9 µm particle size) in an Elute UHPLC (both from Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Eluents were identical to above. A multistep gradient was used for elution: 0 min 40% B, 2 min 43% B, 2.1 min 50% B, 12 min 54% B, 12.1 min 70% B, 18 min 99% B, 18.1 min 40% B, 20 min 40% B.&nbsp;</p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Witting M, Maier TV, Garvis S, Schmitt-Kopplin P. Optimizing a ultrahigh pressure liquid chromatography-time of flight-mass spectrometry approach using a novel sub-2μm core-shell particle for in depth lipidomic profiling of Caenorhabditis elegans. J Chromatogr A. 2014 Sep 12;1359:91-9. doi: 10.1016/j.chroma.2014.07.021. Epub 2014 Jul 18. PMID: 25074420</p>	<p><strong>Lipid analysis: UHPLC-UHR-ToF–MS</strong></p><p>Mass spectrometric detection was performed using a Bruker maXis UHR-ToF-MS (Bruker Daltonic, Bermen, Germany) in positive ionization mode using data dependent acquisition to obtain MS1 and MS2 information. Every 10 samples a pooled QC was injected to check performance of the UPLC-UHR-ToF-MS system and the results were used for normalization. Additional runs of QC samples in negative mode were used of lipid identification.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Lipid analysis: UHPLC-TIMS-ToF–MS</strong></p><p><span class='ql-cursor'>﻿</span>Detection was carried out in positive mode using PASEF acquisition mode. The overall acquisition cycle of 0.32 s comprised one full TIMS-MS scan and two PASEF MS/MS scans with a ramp time of 100 ms and the mobility range from 0.55 – 1.9 Vs cm−2. The measured mass range comprised m/z 100 – 1350 and for fragmentation only the range of m/z 300-1350 was considered. Low-abundance precursor ions with an intensity above a threshold of 100 counts were repeatedly scheduled for fragmentation with a collision energy of 30eV until a target value of 4000 counts was reached and then dynamically excluded for 0.1 min. For acquisition, an ion charge control setting of 5 million counts was applied. The ion mobility dimension was calibrated with a linear function using the ions ESI LC/MS tuning mix (Agilent) [m/z, 1/K0: (322.0481, 0.7319 Vs cm−2), (622.0289, 0.9848 Vs cm−2), (922.0097, 1.1896 Vs cm−2)] in positive mode.</p>	<p><strong>Raw </strong>data from the UPLC-UHR-ToF-MS was processed with <strong>Genedata Expressionist for MS 13.5 </strong>(Genedata AG, Basel, Switzerland). Preprocessing steps included noise subtraction, m/z recalibration, chromatographic alignment and peak detection and grouping. Data was exported into the <strong>Genedata Expressionist for MS 13.5 Analys</strong>t statistical analysis software and as .<strong>xlsx </strong>for further investigation. Maximum peak intensities were used for statistical analysis and the data was normalized on the protein content of the sample. Intensity drift normalization based on QC samples was used to normalize for the acquisition sequence.</p>	<p><strong>Data preprocessing and statistical analysis: UHPLC-UHR-ToF–MS</strong></p><p>Lipids were first annotated on the MS1 level using <strong>LipidMAPS </strong>and an internal database of <em>C. elegans</em> lipids. Interesting lipid features were then identified using MS2 data and manual interpretation.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Data preprocessing and statistical analysis: UHPLC-TIMS-ToF–MS</strong></p><p><strong>MetaboScape 2021</strong> (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) was used to process the 4-dimensional (retention time, exact mass, mobility, intensity) <strong>raw </strong>data files. The time aligned region complete feature extraction algorithm (T-ReX 4D) extracted and aligned the features in a retention time range from 0.8 to 18&nbsp;min and a mass range from 300 to 1200&nbsp;m/z with an intensity of higher than 3000 and a minimum peak size in 4D space of 150 points across the sample intensity. For automatic recursive feature extraction with a lowered threshold of 100 points across the sample intensity the features needed to be present in 3 out of 21 samples. Features were excluded if not present after recursive extraction in 4 out of 21 samples. Subsequently, extracted features were filtered for a minimum of 80% presence per defined sample type (i.e.&nbsp;daf-2(e1370), N2 and QC). The algorithm assigned averaged PASEF MS/MS spectra to the extracted features. Masses were automatically recalibrated using sodium formate clusters infused at the end of each chromatogram via a six-port valve. For ion deconvolution, [M + H]+&nbsp;was assigned as primary ion, [M + NH4]+, [M + K]+, [M + Na]+&nbsp;as seed ions and [M + H-H2O]+&nbsp;as common ion. For MS/MS spectral library matching using the open source <strong>LipidBlast library[1],[2],[3],[4] </strong>the following maximum tolerances were applied for matching: 5.0&nbsp;ppm precursor mass, isotopic fit (mSigma) 200, MS/MS score 500, 2% CCS deviation.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Kind T, Liu KH, Lee DY, DeFelice B, Meissen JK, Fiehn O. LipidBlast in silico tandem mass spectrometry database for lipid identification. Nat Methods. 2013 Aug;10(8):755-8. doi: 10.1038/nmeth.2551. Epub 2013 Jun 30. PMID: 23817071; PMCID: PMC3731409.</p><p><strong>[2] </strong>Kind T, Okazaki Y, Saito K, Fiehn O. LipidBlast templates as flexible tools for creating new in-silico tandem mass spectral libraries. Anal Chem. 2014 Nov 18;86(22):11024-7. doi: 10.1021/ac502511a. Epub 2014 Nov 6. PMID: 25340521; PMCID: PMC4238643</p><p><strong>[3] </strong>Ma Y, Kind T, Vaniya A, Gennity I, Fahrmann JF, Fiehn O. An in silico MS/MS library for automatic annotation of novel FAHFA lipids. J Cheminform. 2015 Nov 16;7:53. doi: 10.1186/s13321-015-0104-4. PMID: 26579213; PMCID: PMC4646931</p><p><strong>[4] </strong>Tsugawa H, Ikeda K, Takahashi M, Satoh A, Mori Y, Uchino H, Okahashi N, Yamada Y, Tada I, Bonini P, Higashi Y, Okazaki Y, Zhou Z, Zhu ZJ, Koelmel J, Cajka T, Fiehn O, Saito K, Arita M, Arita M. A lipidome atlas in MS-DIAL 4. Nat Biotechnol. 2020 Oct;38(10):1159-1163. doi: 10.1038/s41587-020-0531-2. Epub 2020 Jun 15. PMID: 32541957</p>

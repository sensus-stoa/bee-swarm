Study Identifier	MTBLS2042
Study Title	Two UGT84A Family Glycosyltransferases Regulate Phenol, Flavonoid, and Tannin Metabolism in Juglans regia (English Walnut)
Study Description	We showed previously that gallic acid is produced in walnut from 3-dehydroshikimate by a shikimate dehydrogenase (JrSkDH). This study focuses on the next step in the hydrolysable tannin pathway, the formation of 1-<i>O</i>-galloyl-β-D-glucose from the phenolic gallic acid and UDP glucose by a glycosyltransferase. JrGGT1 (UGT84A73) and JrGGT2 (UGT84A74) are predicted to be two such glycosyltransferases, which we expressed in tobacco plants. GC-MS analysis of the transgenic tobacco revealed moderate, yet significant alterations in plant secondary metabolism, such as depleted phenolic acids, including gallic acid. We postulate that these effects are due to JrGGT1 and JrGGT2 activity, as JrGGT orthologs glycosylate these phenolic compounds <i>in vitro</i>. Moreover, <i>JrGGT</i> expression in tobacco caused upregulation of shikimic acid pathway metabolites and differing responses in phenylpropanoids, such as phenolic acids and flavonoids. In transcriptome analysis of walnut pellicle tissues, both <i>JrGGT</i>s showed substantial and significant expression correlations with the gallic acid-producing <i>JrSkDH</i>s and were highly coexpressed with the genetic circuits constituting the shikimic acid and phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathways. Verification of <i>JrGGT</i> gene expression by transcriptome analysis of 20 walnut tissues revealed striking similarities with that of the pellicle data, with the greatest expression in roots, wood, buds, and leaves of <i>Juglans regia</i> cv. Chandler: tissues that typically accumulate hydrolysable tannins. Like the transgenic tobacco, pellicle metabolomic analyses revealed that many phenylpropanoids correlated negatively with <i>JrGGT</i> expression, while shikimic acid pathway metabolites correlated positively with <i>JrGGT</i> expression. This research supports the hypothesis that JrGGT1 and JrGGT2 play non-trivial roles in metabolism of phenolic acids, flavonoids, and ostensibly, tannins.

Study Protocol Description	<p>To determine the role of the JrGGTs in the HT biosynthetic pathway, full-length protein-encoding regions corresponding to JrGGT1 and JrGGT2 were cloned directly from DNA extracted from Juglans regia cv. Chandler since neither gene has any introns. The coding sequence in each case is contiguous in the walnut genome. The amplified protein-coding sequences of JrGGT1 and JrGGT2 were cloned downstream from a CaMV35S regulatory sequence creating 2 binary vectors pDH17.0301 (JrGGT1) and pDH17.0401 (JrGGT2) that were used to transform N. tabacum cv. SR1 using Agrobacterium-mediated plant transformation. Transgenic tobacco lines were screened for expression of the JrGGT1 or 2 proteins using an anti-his tag antibody. 1 highest expressing transgenic line was selected, each for JrGGT1 and JrGGT2 expression. Seeds from these T0 plants, along with untransformed tobacco, were germinated on selective and nonselective media, respectively, to create plants that were grown in the greenhouse. 12 seedlings each from untransformed tobacco (grown without kanamycin), and tobacco plants overexpressing JrGGT1 and JrGGT2 were allowed to grow for 4 wk and then harvested for leaf tissue for metabolite and amino acid analysis.</p>	<p>Leaf tissue samples representing 6 independent biological replicates, each from JrGGT1- and JrGGT2-overexpressing tobacco lines, and leaf tissue samples representing 7 independent biological replicates from UT lines were sent to the West Coast Metabolomics Center (WCMC) in the Genome Center at UC Davis for primary metabolism analysis by gas chromatography/time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GCTOF). 20 ±5-mg fresh weight of tobacco leaves were harvested and frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen. Tissue was homogenized under liquid nitrogen using a Retsch mill. 2 ml of a single-phase solvent mixture of methanol/chloroform/water 2.5:1:1 v/v/v kept at −20 °C was added to the tissue and thoroughly mixed at 4 °C for 30 min to precipitate proteins and DNA/RNA and to disassociate metabolites from membrane and cell wall components. After centrifugation, the remaining pellet consisting of DNA/RNA, proteins, starch, membranes, and cell wall components was extracted in a second step with 1 ml methanol/chloroform 1:1 v/v at −20 °C. The organic solvent extracts were combined and used for metabolite analysis via GC-TOF.</p><p><br></p><p>For GC-TOF MS analysis, the organic phase was dried and dissolved in 50 ml methoxamine hydrochloride (20 mg/ml pyridine) and incubated at 30 °C for 90 min with continuous shaking. Then, 80 ml N-methyl-N-trimethylsilyl trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA) was added at 37 °C for 30 min to derivatize polar functional groups. The derivatized samples were stored at room temperature for 120 min before injection.</p>	<p>GC-TOF MS (Leco Pegasus IV GC-TOF mass spectrometer; Leco, St. Joseph, MO, United States) analysis was performed on an HP 5890 gas chromatograph with tapered, deactivated split/splitless liners containing glass wool (Agilent, Böblingen, Germany) and 1 ml split-less injection at 230 °C injector temperature. The GC was operated at a constant flow of 1 ml/min helium with a 30 m 0.25 mm id 0.25 mm RTX-5 column with 10 m integrated pre-column. The temperature gradient started at 80 °C, was held isocratic for 2 min, and subsequently ramped at 15 °C/min to a final temperature of 330 °C, which was held for 6 min.</p>	<p>Mass spectra were recorded on a Leco Pegasus IV GC-TOF mass spectrometer (Leco, St. Joseph, MO, United States). 20 spectra/s were recorded between m/z 85 and 500. Peak identification and quantification were performed using the Pegasus software package (Leco, St. Joseph, MO, United States).</p>	<p>Metabolite data were then normalized using the mTIC method by calculating the sum of all peak heights for all identified metabolites (but not the unknowns) in each sample. Such peak sums are called “mTIC” in analogy to the term TIC used in mass spectrometry (for “total ion chromatogram”), but with the notification “mTIC” to indicate that only genuine metabolites (identified compounds) were used to avoid using potential non-biological artifacts for the biological normalizations, such as column bleed, plasticizers, or other contaminants.</p><p><br></p><p>Subsequently, it was determined whether the mTIC averages are significantly different between treatment groups or cohorts. If these averages were different by p&lt;0.05, data was normalized to the average mTIC of each group. If averages between treatment groups were not different, data was normalized to the total average mTIC.</p>	<p>Reference chromatograms were defined that had a maximum of detected peaks over a signal/noise threshold of 20 and were used for automated peak identification based on mass spectral comparison to a standard <strong>NIST98 library[1]</strong>. Automated assignments of unique fragment ions for each individual metabolite were taken as default as quantifiers and manually corrected where necessary. All artifactual peaks caused by column bleeding or phthalates and polysiloxanes derived from MSTFA hydrolyzation were identified manually and removed from the results table.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> McLafferty FW, Stauffer DA, Loh SY, Wesdemiotis C. Unknown identification using reference mass spectra. Quality evaluation of databases. J Am Soc Mass Spectrom. 1999 Dec;10(12):1229-40. doi: 10.1016/S1044-0305(99)00104-X. PMID:10584326.</p>

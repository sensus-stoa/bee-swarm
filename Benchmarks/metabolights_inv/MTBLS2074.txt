Study Identifier	MTBLS2074
Study Title	Symbiotic bacteria-derived organic acids protect Delia antiqua larvae from entomopathogenic fungal infection
Study Description	<p>The protection of associated microbiota for their animal hosts against pathogen infection has been studied widely over the last 100 years. However, how those microbes protect the animal host is limitedly investigated. In former studies, body surface microbes of one insect, <em>Delia antiqua</em>, protect the insect larvae from infection with the entomopathogen <em>Beauveria bassiana</em>. By comparing the metabolites produced by microbes that protect the insect and microbes that cannot protect the insect, how the microbes protect the insect is answered. It turns out that body surface bacteria produce a metabolite cocktail that inhibit colonization of <em>B. bassiana</em>, and consequently protect the insect. This work reveals novel molecules that with antifungal activity, which may aid in discovery and expansion of new prophylactic and therapeutic natural chemicals for treating infectious diseases.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Adults of <em>D. antiqua</em> were originally collected from garlic fields in Taian City, China (N36°14', E117°25'). The fungus <em>B. bassiana</em> BB1101 was originally isolated from infected <em>D. antiqua</em> adults with the single spore method<strong>[1]</strong>, and its conidia were preserved in 25% glycerol in our laboratory at -80 °C. Bacterial strains, <em>C. freundii</em> B505 (GenBank Accession No. MF084973), <em>E. ludwigii</em> B424 (GenBank Accession No. MF084966), <em>P. protegens</em> B108 (GenBank Accession No. MF084946), <em>S. plymuthica</em> B585 (GenBank Accession No. MF084977), <em>S. maltophilia</em> B263 (GenBank Accession No. MF084954), and <em>S. faecium</em> B253 (GenBank Accession No. MF084952), which inhibit <em>B. bassiana</em> infection of <em>D. antiqua</em> larvae[2], and <em>K. oxytoca</em> B313 (GenBank Accession No. MF084958), which has no effect on the conidial germination and mycelial growth of <em>B. bassiana</em> BB1101 nor on the infection of <em>D. antiqua</em> larvae by <em>B. bassiana</em> according to a preliminary experiment (see <strong>Fig. S6</strong> of the paper associated with this study), were originally isolated from field-collected <em>D. antiqua</em> larvae via a culture-dependent method and preserved in 25% glycerol in our laboratory at -80 °C. Standard chemicals and reagents including hypoxanthine (CAS No. 68-94-0), glutaric acid (CAS No. 110-94-1), kynurenic acid (CAS No. 492-27-3), phenyllactic acid (CAS No. 828-01-3), thymine (CAS No. 65-71-4), ethylmalonic acid (CAS No. 601-75-2), adipic acid (CAS No. 124-04-9), indoleacetic acid (CAS No. 87-51-4), picolinic acid (CAS No. 98-98-6) and ketoisocaproic acid (CAS No. 816-66-0) were purchased from Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany.</p><p><br></p><p>Individual bacterial Lysogeny broth (LB) cultures (rotary shaking at 150 rpm and 28 °C for 72 h) for each strain including <em>K. oxytoca</em> B313 were centrifuged at 3,000 x g for 5 min.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Choi Y-W, Hyde KD, Ho W. 1999. Single spore isolation of fungi. Fungal Divers 3:29-38.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Zhou F, Wu X, Xu L, Guo S, Chen G, Zhang X. Repressed Beauveria bassiana infections in Delia antiqua due to associated microbiota. Pest Manag Sci. 2019 Jan;75(1):170-179. doi:10.1002/ps.5084. PMID:29797399.</p><p><br></p>	<p>20 μl of supernatant filtered with a 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filter (Cat. No. SCAA-1114, ANPEL Laboratory Technologies (Shanghai) Inc.) was mixed with 120 μl of precooled 50% methanol (4 °C for 24 h), vortexed for 1 min, and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The mixture was then stored overnight at -20 °C and centrifuged at 4,000 x g for 20 min, and the supernatants were transferred separately into new glass vials for the LC-MS analysis. LB medium was also extracted as described above and analyzed with LC-MS to subtract the background noise of LB. In addition, pooled quality control (QC) samples were also prepared by combining 10 μl of each extraction sample. 6 replicates were used for each group (B585, B263, B108, B505, B253, B424, B313, LB, and QC).</p>	<p>Each sample from all 9 groups were analyzed using a LC-MS system according to a previously published method with minor modifications<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, all chromatographic separations were performed using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (SCIEX, UK) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (100 mm x 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters, UK) for the reversed-phase separation. The column oven was maintained at 35 °C. The flow rate was 0.4 ml/min and the mobile phase consisted of solvent A (25 mM ammonium acetate) and solvent B (isopropanol:acetonitrile = 9:1 + 0.1% formic acid). Gradient elution conditions were set as follows: 0-0.5 min, 95% B; 0.5-9.5 min, 95% to 65% B; 9.5-10.5 min, 65%-40% B; 10.5-12 min, 40% B; 12-12.2 min, 40%-95% B; and 12.2-15 min, 95% B. The injection volume for each sample was 4 µl.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Tan Y, Li Y, Zhou F, Guo J, Wang T, Shi Y, Yang Y, Lu J, Pei G. Administration of a mixture of triterpenoids from yeyachun and phenolic acids from danshen ameliorates carbon tetrachloride-induced liver fibrosis in mice by the regulation of intestinal flora. J Pharmacol Sci. 2020 Jul;143(3):165-175. doi:10.1016/j.jphs.2020.04.007. PMID:32387002.</p>	<p>A high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer TripleTOF 5600 plus (SCIEX, UK) was set up according to previously published methods<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, the TripleTOF 5600 plus MS was used to detect metabolites eluted from the column in both positive and negative ion modes with the curtain gas set to 30 psi, ion source gas1 set to 60 psi, ion source gas2 set to 60 psi, and an interface heater temperature set to 650 °C. The ionspray floating voltage was set to 5000 V for positive ion mode and -4500 V for negative ion mode. The mass spectrometry data were acquired in IDA mode, and the TOF mass range was from 60 to 1200 Da. Ion survey scans were completed in 150 ms, and 12 product ions were collected if they exceeded a threshold of 100 counts/s and with a 1+ charge-state. The total cycle time was set to 0.56 s. 4 bins were summed during each scan at a pulser frequency of 11 kHz through the monitoring of the 40 GHz multichannel TDC detector with 4-anode/channel detection. Dynamic exclusion was set for 4 s. During mass spectrometry data acquisition, the mass accuracy was calibrated every 10 samples. Furthermore, to maintain the stability of the LC-MS system during the whole analysis, all samples from the 9 groups were analyzed in a random sequence, and a QC sample was analyzed every 10 samples.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Wang w, Gentu G, Zhou T, Chang C, Yin Q, Jia Y. 2019. Base on metabolomics:analysis of the internal causes of nutrient changes in Leymus chinensis at different harvest stage. Research Square doi:10.21203/rs.2.13629/v1.</p>	<p>Metabolomic data were analyzed using the XCMS software package (https://sciex.com/products/software/xcms-plus-software) according to methods published previously<strong>[1]</strong>. LC−MS raw data files were converted into mzXML format and subsequently processed by the XCMS, CAMERA and metaX toolbox R packages.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Liu YY, Chen XR, Wang JP, Cui WQ, Xing XX, Chen XY, Ding WY, God'spower BO, Eliphaz N, Sun MQ, Li YH. Transcriptomic analysis reveals flavonoid biosynthesis of Syringa oblata Lindl. in response to different light intensity. BMC Plant Biol. 2019 Nov 11;19(1):487. doi:10.1186/s12870-019-2100-8. PMID:31711412.</p>	<p>Detected ions were identified according to their retention time (RT) and m/z data. The intensities of each peak were calculated, and a three-dimensional matrix including arbitrarily assigned peak indices (retention time-m/z pairs), sample names (observations) and ion intensity information (variables) was generated. Online databases such as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) were used to annotate the metabolites by comparing the molecular mass data (m/z) of samples with those from databases. Metabolites were annotated if the mass difference between the observed metabolite and the one from the database was less than 10 ppm. Furthermore, the molecular formula of metabolites was further identified and validated by the isotopic distribution measurements. In addition, an in-house fragment spectrum library of metabolites was used to validate the metabolite identification.</p><p><br></p><p>Principal components analysis (PCA) was performed for outlier detection and batch effects evaluation with the preprocessed dataset. Supervised partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was conducted to discriminate the different variables between groups with calculated VIP values. A VIP cut-off value of 2 was used to select important features.</p><p><br></p><p>To subtract the background noise from LB medium, additional data analysis procedures were conducted (see <strong>Fig. S7</strong> in the paper associated with the study). A dataset union was created by merging metabolites from each bacterial sample group which were significantly upregulated at least two-fold compared with the LB medium (Step I in <strong>Fig. S7</strong> in the paper associated with the study). Subsequently, a more critical parameter (metabolites produced by the selected bacterial strains upregulated at least 10x compared with the B313 group) was used to ensure that the obtained metabolites were produced by the 6 strains and of high antifungal activity (Step II in <strong>Fig. S7</strong> in the paper associated with the study).</p><p><br></p>

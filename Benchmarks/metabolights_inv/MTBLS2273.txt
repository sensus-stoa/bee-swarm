Study Title	Experimental and computational approaches for deep metabolome annotation with application to the ecotoxicological model organism Daphnia magna
Study Description	<p>Identifying metabolomes with greater coverage and confidence is a critical step towards interpreting the metabolic basis of human and environmental health. Yet for over two decades the field of metabolomics has been inhibited by limited metabolite identification. To address this, we developed the Deep Metabolome Annotation (DMA) workflow. Applied to the ecological sentinel species, Daphnia magna, one pooled sample comprising ten distinct genotypes exposed to two environment conditions was systematically physicochemically-separated via solid-phase extraction, liquid- and gas-chromatography prior to extensive mass spectrometric (MS) fragmentation, generating &gt;8000 data files, and supplemented by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy. An extensive Galaxy-based computational approach was built to analyse these data, comprising over 30 tools. DMA generated&nbsp;&gt;8,000&nbsp;polar metabolite and lipid annotations in D. magna, with the data, tools and annotations disseminated freely via public data repositories and a custom web-based interface to ensure reusability and transferability.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Ten genotypically distinct strains of <em>Daphnia magna</em> were cultured under abnormal light and dark cycles and lower temperatures (Basal conditions: 20 +/- 2 °C with a 16:8 hr light:dark ratio for 48 hr; Stressed conditions: 10 +/- 1 °C and with 16:8 hr light:dark ratio for 24 hr, then 10 +/- 1 °C and with 8:16 hr light:dark ratio for a further 24 hr). The Daphnia were then flash frozen in liquid nitrogen to quench any metabolic processes and then homogenised and pooled into a single sample.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Stock cultures of Daphnia magna</strong></p><p>Stock cultures of 10 Daphnia magna isolates (see supplemental table S1) were maintained in modified high-hardness COMBO medium at 20 +/- 2 °C and under a 16:8 h light:dark cycle, at a target density of 20 female<em> D. magna </em>per 1.2 L of medium. A concentrated isolate of Chlorella vulgaris (equivalent to 0.5 mg carbon per L and cultured under non-axenic conditions in Bold’s Basal medium - see Table S3 for details) was supplied as feed material, at an equivalent daily dose of: 0.5 mL/day for days 1-2; 0.75 mL/day for days 3-7 and; 1 mL/day for days 8 onwards. Culture medium was replaced weekly, between which neonates and any unexpected male daphniids were carefully removed. Each Daphnia stock culture was derived from an existing stock culture by transferring twenty &lt; 24 h neonates (third brood or later) to freshly aerated culture medium. Under these conditions, cultures comprised almost exclusively parthenogenetic females.</p><p><br></p><p><br></p><p><strong>Culturing Daphnia magna for DMA experiments</strong></p><p><em>D. magna</em> used in DMA experiments were derived from corresponding stock cultures by transferring 20 neonates (age &lt; 24 h, third brood or later) into fresh culturing medium. These experimental cultures were maintained as per stock cultures until day 14, at which point adult D. magna were transferred to fresh medium in the absence of food. Adults were then maintained without food for 2 days, under one of two conditions, either: 1) 20 +/- 2 °C with a 16:8 h light:dark ratio for 48 h; or 2) 10 +/- 1 °C and with 16:8 h light:dark ratio for 24 h, then 10 +/- 1 °C and with 8:16 h light:dark ratio for a further 24 h. Thereafter, remaining female daphniids were collected and metabolically quenched.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Collection and metabolic quenching of D. magna</strong></p><p>To quench metabolic processes at the end of the culturing period, Daphnia were rapidly flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. To do so,<em> D. magna </em>cultures were first passed through a coarse gauze mesh to isolate D. magna from their culturing medium. D. magna, trapped on the mesh, were then blot dried with tissue paper to remove excess media and transferred to polypropylene tubes using a fine-haired paintbrush, wherein they were submerged in liquid nitrogen. Resulting samples were either used immediately or stored at -80 °C, until required.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Homogenisation of D. magna biomass</strong></p><p>Prior to metabolite extraction, D. magna samples were cryogenically cooled in liquid nitrogen and homogenised over dry ice using a ceramic mortar and pestle. Resulting homogenate was transferred to a pre-cooled 28 mL glass vial for metabolite extraction.</p><p><br></p><p>DMA samples were homogenised in 2 batches, with approximately 50% of DMA samples homogenised in each. Approximately 60% of each resulting homogenate was transferred to a single 28 mL glass vial for extraction of “polar” metabolites, with the remainder transferred to two separate 28 mL glass vials for extraction of “apolar” metabolites.</p>	<p>The pooled <em>Daphnia </em>homogenate is then used for two distinct liquid phase extractions, one for the “polar” metabolites and one for “apolar” metabolites (or more precisely the moderately polar through to highly apolar metabolites). This is followed by by 4 types of solid phase extraction (SPE), polar extracts using weak anion-exchange (WAX) and weak-cation exchange SPE cartridges (WCX) and apolar extracts using WAX and reversed-phase C18 cartridges (C18).&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Metabolite extraction from homogenised D. magna biomass</strong></p><p>Two distinct procedures were applied for the extraction of metabolites from homogenised <em>D. magna</em> biomass. The first, aimed at extracting polar through to moderately-polar metabolites, involved adding ice-cold methanol, then ice-cold water, to homogenised D. magna biomass to yield a 2.5:1 v/v methanol:water solution (account for the ca. 80% w/w water content of an adult D. magna [REF])), at 15 parts solution per 1 part biomass (v/w). The extraction vial was capped, vortex-mixed for 30 s (40 Hz, Thermo Scientific) and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatant was carefully transferred to a clean, ice-cold 28 mL glass vial. This “polar extract” was distributed equally across 1.5 mL polypropylene microfuge tubes, such that each contained a proportion of the total extract equivalent to 20 D. magna. Polar extract aliquots were dried in a SpeedVac at 35 °C and then transferred to a -80 °C freezer for storage.</p><p><br></p><p>The “apolar extraction” procedure, derived from that described by Bligh and Dyer, was applied with the aim of extracting moderately-polar through to highly-apolar metabolites. Ice-cold chloroform was added to homogenate using a graduated glass pipette, followed by ice-cold methanol (using a laboratory pipette and polypropylene tip), to give final solvent ratio of 1:1 v/v chloroform:methanol and a final extraction ratio of 30 parts extraction solution per 1 part homogenate (assumptions as per the polar extraction procedure, detailed above). The extraction vial was then capped and vortex mixed for 30 s (40 Hz), followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to an ice-cold, 28 mL glass vial using a glass Pasteur pipette. Next, ice-cold water was added to the supernatant (taking into account the approximate water content of the daphniids) to induce phase separation, with the extraction solution consisting of 1:1:0.9 v/v/v chloroform:methanol:water. After vortex mixing for 30 s, vials were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and then allowed to stand on a laboratory bench for 5 min at room temperature. Using a 500 μL glass gas-tight syringe (Hamilton), the upper (polar) and lower (apolar) layers were transferred to separate, pre-cooled 28 mL glass vials. The syringe was rinsed before and between transfer of each layer, first using 10 full volumes of methanol and then 10 full volumes of chloroform. The upper and lower layers were independently split into equivolume aliquots across glass vials and tubes, respectively, such that each contained the equivalent of 20 D. magna. The upper ‘polar’ layer aliquots were dried under vacuum at 35 °C (Speedvac, Thermo Scientific), while the lower ‘non-polar’ layer aliquots were dried under a gaseous stream of nitrogen and then capped after flushing with nitrogen gas. Dried aliquots were stored frozen at -80 °C.</p><p><br></p><p>For both the polar and apolar extraction procedures, methodological blank samples were prepared by replacing D. magna biomass with water. The volume of water added was such that it corresponded to 80% of the total weight of the extracted D. magna biomass (assuming that one adult D. magna has a wet mass of 1.5 mg).</p><p><br></p><p>For DMA samples, dried extracts resulting from the “polar” and “apolar” extraction procedures were termed “crude” extracts.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Solid phase extraction-based fractionation of metabolite extracts</strong></p><p>Solid phase extraction (SPE) was used to fractionate metabolite extracts prior to analysis. The ‘polar extract’ was fractionated over weak anion-exchange (Hypersep WAX) and weak-cation exchange (Hypersep WCX) SPE cartridges , while the ‘apolar extract’ was fractionated over weak-anion exchange (Hypersep WAX) and reversed-phase C18 cartridges (Hypersep C18). All SPE fractionation procedures were applied independently (i.e. not chained) using cartridges of 3 mL total volume and packed with 500 mg sorbent material. Solvents and solutions were passed through cartridges at approximately 1 mL/min.</p><p><br></p><p>A total of n dried extracts (maximum n = 10) were resuspended for SPE-based fractionation over a single SPE cartridge. Extracts, x, were ordered from x = 1 to x = n. In all but the final resuspension step, resuspension solvent or solution was added to x = 1, vortex mixed for 30 s and then transferred to x + 1. This procedure was repeated until x = n, at which point, the resuspended extract was transferred to a collection vial (8 mL). In the final resuspension step, all n extracts received resuspension solvent, followed by vortex mixing for 30 s and centrifugation at 3000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were transferred to the collection vial. All solvents, solutions and extracts were kept of wet ice throughout the resuspension procedure.</p><p><br></p><p>Fractions eluted from SPE cartridges were divided into equivolume aliquots, such that each contained metabolite quantities equivalent to ca. 20 D. magna. Aliquots for polar SPE extracts were transferred to polypropylene tubes and dried using a SpeedVac (Thermo Scientific) at 35 °C. Apolar fractions were transferred to 1.75 mL glass vials and dried under a gentle stream of gaseous nitrogen at room temperature. Dried fractions were stored frozen at -80 °C.</p>	<p>Three distinct LC-MS/MS methods are then applied in both positive and negative ionisation mode. Polar extracts and polar SPE fractions using an Accucore amide HILIC LC column method (AMD: Accucore 150 Amide, 100 x 2.1 mm (length x internal diameter), 2.6 Âµm particle size) and a reversed-phase Syncronis Phenyl LC column method (PHE: Syncronis Phenyl, 100 x 2.1 mm (length x internal diameter), 1.7 Âµm particle size). Apolar extracts and apolar SPE fractions using a reversed-phase Accucore C30 LC column method (C30: 100 x 2.1 mm (length x internal diameter), 2.6 Âµm particle size, 150 Angstrom pore size).</p><p><br></p><p>The Thermo Scientific Ultimate 3000 instrument was used for all chromatography.</p><p><br></p><p>LC fractionation was performed, where eluent was collected from the column into 96 well plates to allow further analysis on each fraction collected.</p><p><br></p>	<p>(Ultra)-high-performance liquid chromatography-high resolution tandem mass spectrometry (U)HPLC-HRMS was performed with Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific), fitted with a heated electrospray ionisation source for mass spectrometry analysis of eluted metabolites.</p><p><br></p><p>Initial (U)HPLC-HRMS analyses were used to create inclusion and exclusion lists (i.e. m/z features of interest) to direct the subsequent Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA) to acquire fragmentation data. In parallel, eluent from the LC columns were fraction-collected during the mass spectral acquisition, which were then subject to extensive direct infusion-high resolution mass spectrometry with multiple-stage fragmentation (DI-HRMS(MSn).</p><p><br></p><p>The DI-HRMS(MSn) was performed using an Obitrap Elite mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) using both higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD) and collision-induced dissociation (CID) at several levels of normalised collision energy (NCE). Specifically, HCD was performed at 20%, 40% and 80% NCE, followed by CID at 35% NCE with multi-stage fragmentation up to MS3. The acquisition of DI-HRMS(MSn) fragmentation data was directed via a predefined list of targeted m/z features derived from a prior DI-HRMS data acquisition on the same fraction.</p>	<p>ThermoFisher Scientific raw files were converted into .mzML files using ProteoWizard (Adusumilli et al., 2017).</p><p><br></p><p>Adusumilli R, Mallick P. Data Conversion with ProteoWizard msConvert. Methods Mol Biol. 2017;1550:339-368. doi:10.1007/978-1-4939-6747-6_23. PMID:28188540.</p>	<p>Metabolite annotations were derived from either the 'DMA Galaxy workflow', GNPS or mzCloud.</p><p><br></p><p>The “DMA Galaxy Workflow” workflow is split into 5 components: 'Data input(s)', “LC-MS &amp; LC-MS/MS data processing”, “DIMS &amp; DIMSn data processing”, “Metabolite annotation” and the “Combining” component.</p><p><br></p><p>The “LC-MS and LC-MS/MS data processing” workflow component uses the Workflow4Metabolomics XCMS Galaxy tools (Smith et al., 2006; Giacomoni et al., 2015) for chromatographic feature picking and grouping of LC-MS (MS1) features between multiple files. Galaxy wrappers of the msPurity R package (Lawson et al., 2017) are also used to flag and calculate quality measure for MS1 features (i.e. flag blank features, calculate RSD of the intensities and retention time of the MS1 data), calculate precursor ion purity of the fragmentation spectra, align fragmentation spectra to the XCMS MS1 features, filter fragmentation data based on signal to noise, average fragmentation spectra within files (intra-averaging), average fragmentation spectra across files (inter-averaging), combine the LC-MS and LC-MS/MS based analysis into a single SQLite database and create MSP files of the averaged fragmentation spectra. Additionally the CAMERA Galaxy tool (Kuhl et al., 2012; Giacomoni et al., 2015) is used as means to determine adducts and isotopes.</p><p><br></p><p>The “DIMS &amp; DIMSn data processing” workflow component uses the Galaxy wrappers for the DIMSpy tools (github.com/computational-metabolomics/dimspy-galaxy) to process the DIMS data - including averaging and grouping spectra within and across files and blank subtraction. Galaxy wrappers of the MSnPy (github.com/computational-metabolomics/msnpy-galaxy) tools are also used to process the DIMS/MS and DIMSn data - including grouping related scans, processing and averaging the m/z and intensities from each scan group, creating spectral and fragmentation trees of each group and creating MSP files of the averaged fragmentation spectra. Additionally, the msPurity Galaxy tools are used to calculate the precursor ion purity and CAMERA DIMS Galaxy tool is used as means to determine adducts and isotopes.</p><p><br></p><p>The “Metabolite annotation” workflow components use the Galaxy wrappers of msPurity for spectral matching; the Galaxy wrapper for SIRIUS-CSI:FingerID (Dührkop et al., 2019, p. 4) for predicting structure based on fragmentation trees and a machine learning model derived from public mass spectrometry datasets; and the Galaxy wrapper for MetFrag (Ruttkies et al., 2016) that annotates features using in silico prediction of mass spectrometry. Annotation of the MS1 features is also performed using the Galaxy wrapper for BEAMSpy (https://more.bham.ac.uk/beams/) - using a neutral mass lookup of each feature to a compound database (HMDB) based on a neutral mass calculated from a predefined list of adducts.</p><p><br></p><p>The “Combining” workflow component uses the msPurity combineAnnotation tools and the LC Fractionation Galaxy tool to combine all the annotations and processed data into a single SQLite database. The LC-MS/MS data is first combined using the msPurity combineAnnotation tool - where all annotations from the LC-MS/MS dataset (i.e. spectral matching, MetFrag, Sirius CSI:FingerID and MS1 neutral mass lookup) are aligned based on their InChiKeys. Scores from each approach are weighted based on predetermined weights and the annotations are ranked for each annotated feature. The “LC Fractionation” galaxy tool is then used when fractionation data is available and combines all the DIMS and DIMSn data and annotations in the same way as the combineAnnotation but additionally aligns features observed in the DIMS fractions to the LC-MS features.</p><p><br></p><p>A molecular network was created using the online workflow (https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/) on the GNPS website (http://gnps.ucsd.edu) (Wang et al., 2016). The data was filtered by removing all fragment ions within +/- 17 Da of the precursor m/z. Fragmentation spectra were window filtered by choosing only the top 6 fragment ions in the +/- 50 Da window throughout the spectrum. The precursor ion mass tolerance was set to 0.02 Da and a fragmentation fragment ion tolerance of 0.02 Da. A network was then created where edges were filtered to have a cosine score above 0.7 and more than 2 matched peaks. Further, edges between two nodes were kept in the network if and only if each of the nodes appeared in each other's respective top 10 most similar nodes. Finally, the maximum size of a molecular family was set to 100, and the lowest scoring edges were removed from molecular families until the molecular family size was below this threshold. The spectra in the network were then searched against GNPS' spectral libraries. The library spectra were filtered in the same manner as the input data. All matches kept between network spectra and library spectra were required to have a score above 0.7 and at least 2 matched peaks. The MS2LDA (Wandy et al., 2018) workflow was then performed on the networks to determine common mass motifs. To obtain ClassyFire (Djoumbou Feunang et al., 2016) compound classifications within the molecular network, MolNetEnhancer (Ernst et al., 2019) was used (the workflow incorporates in-silico structure annotations from GNPS Library Search and the Dereplicator tool (Mohimani et al., 2017).</p><p><br></p><p>The fragmentation spectra was searched again independently of the molecular network analysis against the GNPS library spectra using the online workflow (https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/) on the GNPS website (http://gnps.ucsd.edu). The precursor ion mass tolerance was set to 0.02 Da and a MS/MS fragment ion tolerance of 0.02 Da. Additional filtered any spectral matches to have an error of &lt;= 10 ppm between the library precursor m/z and the query precursor m/z and annotations were filtered to only include spectral matching results where the library and query spectra were derived from a mass spectrometer using the same ion mode.</p><p><br></p><p>All annotations derived from mzCloud were performed programmatically in batches via mzCloud using mzCloud proprietary software. Each fragmentation scan was treated individually and spectral matching was performed against the mzCloud database. The annotation results were saved as SQLite database with reference to both the query and library spectra of each annotation. The data was filtered to only include “endogenous” metabolites and only included spectral matching results with a dot product cosine score &gt; 0.7.</p><p><br></p><p>Where possible all metabolites were classified with ClassyFire.</p><p><br></p><p>The metabolite annotations detailed in the MetaboLight repository have been filtered for those that have been observed with at least two of the three fragmentation approaches, resulting in 1,350 annotations. See the D. magna manuscript for full list of annotations.</p><p><br></p><p>References:</p><p>Djoumbou Feunang, Y. et al.

Study Identifier	MTBLS2291
Study Title	Metabolome and exposome profiling of the biospecimens from COVID-19 patients in India (Blood plasma assay)
Study Description	<p><strong>INTRODUCTION:</strong>&nbsp;COVID-19 has become a global impediment by bringing everything to a halt starting from January 2020. India underwent the lockdown starting from 22nd March 2020 with the sudden spike in the number of COVID-19 patients in major cities and states. This study focused on how metabolites play a crucial role in SARSCoV-2 prognosis.</p><p><strong>MATERIALS AND METHODS:</strong> Metabolome profiling of 106 plasma samples and 24 swab samples from symptomatic patients in the Indian population of the Mumbai region was done. COVID-19 positive samples were further segregated under the non-severe COVID-19 and severe COVID-19 patient cohort for both plasma and swab.</p><p><strong>RESULTS:</strong>&nbsp;After analyzing the raw files, total 7,949 and 12,871 metabolites in plasma and swab were found. 11 and 35 significantly altered metabolites were found in COVID-19 positive compared to COVID-19 negative plasma and swab samples, respectively. Also, 9 and 23 significantly altered metabolites were found in severe COVID-19 positive to non-severe COVID-19 positive plasma and swab samples, respectively. The majorly affected pathways in COVID-19 patients were found to be the amino acid metabolism pathway, sphingosine metabolism pathway, and bile salt metabolism pathway.</p><p><strong>CONCLUSION:</strong>&nbsp;This study facilitates identification of potential metabolite-based biomarker candidates for rapid diagnosis and prognosis for clinical applications.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Blood plasma assay</strong> is reported in the current study <strong>MTBLS2291</strong>.</p><p><strong>Nasopharyngeal swab assay</strong> is reported in <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS2349' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MTBLS2349</strong></a>.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Sample and clinical details:</strong></p><p>In this study, the leftover plasma samples collected for routine hematological tests and COVID-19 RT-PCR tests were collected from Kasturba Hospital, Mumbai. All samples were collected with the approval from the Institute Ethics Committee, IIT Bombay and Institutional Review Board, Kasturba Hospital for infectious diseases. Informed consent was not required as leftover samples from the routine tests were processed for the study. Patients with confirmed COVID-19 status based on RT-PCR test results were included in the study.</p><p><br></p><p>The inclusion criteria include: RT-PCR report positive and negative; severe and non-severity decided on basis of WHO guidelines.</p><p><br></p><p>The exclusion criteria considered were: a female patient who is pregnant at enrolling; patient aged below 18.</p><p><br></p><p>As advised by the clinicians, <strong>COVID-19(+)</strong> patients were categorized into non-severe (<strong>NSC</strong>; patients with mild fatigue, fever, cough, and breathlessness with non-invasive ventilation) and severe (<strong>SC</strong>; patients with bilateral pneumonia and acute respiratory distress symptoms with mechanical ventilation support)<strong>[1]</strong>. In total, 106 plasma samples were processed for metabolome profiling. The study cohort included plasma samples from 31 patients with <strong>COVID-19(-)</strong> patients, 43 <strong>NSC</strong> patients, and 29 samples from <strong>SC</strong> patients. The age, gender distribution, hematological parameters, and biochemical parameters of the patients enrolled in the study can be found in <strong>Table 1</strong> of the paper associated with this study.</p><p><br></p><p>Samples were viral inactivated using ethanol, processed and stored at -20 °C until transportation of the samples to the IIT Bombay campus in cold storage was conducted in a month duration. The sample preparation protocols were optimized at IIT Bombay by referring to the viral inactivation method<strong>[2]</strong>. All the steps were performed in the BSL level 3 facility at the hospital.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Sample preparation:</strong></p><p>The plasma samples were prepared using the a previously described methodology<strong>[3]</strong>, overview of the workflow can be refered in <strong>Fig. 1</strong> of the paper associated with this study. Briefly, plasma samples were collected from 9 patients showing nonsevere to severe transition. For severe transitions, we analyzed multiple-time-point plasma samples, which differed from patient to patient. Leftover blood (~1 ml) was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate plasma. The separated plasma was then incubated at 56 °C for 30 min for viral inactivation and further stored at -80 °C in cryovials until further processing. Around 100 μl of plasma separated from the leftover blood sample was dispensed into a sterile tube, and 200 μl of pre-chilled absolute ethanol was added to the plasma sample.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Bankar R, Suvarna K, Ghantasala S, Banerjee A, Biswas D, Choudhury M, Palanivel V, Salkar A, Verma A, Singh A, Mukherjee A, Pai MGJ, Roy J, Srivastava A, Badaya A, Agrawal S, Shrivastav O, Shastri J, Srivastava S. Proteomic investigation reveals dominant alterations of neutrophil degranulation and mRNA translation pathways in patients with COVID-19. iScience. 2021 Mar 19;24(3):102135. doi: 10.1016/j.isci.2021.102135. Epub 2021 Feb 4. PMID: 33558857; PMCID: PMC7857979.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Shen B, Yi X, Sun Y, Bi X, Du J, Zhang C, Quan S, Zhang F, Sun R, Qian L et al. Proteomic and Metabolomic Characterization of COVID-19 Patient Sera. Cell. 2020 Jul 9;182(1):59-72.e15. doi:10.1016/j.cell.2020.05.032. PMID: 32492406.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Suvarna K, Salkar A, Palanivel V, Bankar R, Banerjee N, Gayathri J Pai M et al. A Multi-omics Longitudinal Study Reveals Alteration of the Leukocyte Activation Pathway in COVID-19 Patients. J Proteome Res. 2021 Oct 1;20(10):4667-4680. doi:10.1021/acs.jproteome.1c00215. PMID:34379420.</p>	<p>The tubes were incubated in a bio-safety cabinet for 1.5 h until ethanol was evaporated. To the semi-dried sample, 4x (400 μl) absolute methanol was added and vortexed briefly. The tube was incubated at -20 °C overnight. The next day, the sample was centrifuged at 4 °C for 30 min at 12,000 x g. The supernatant was collected in a fresh tube and stored at -20 °C. Samples were transported at 4 °C to the Proteomics Lab, IIT Bombay. A total of 250 μl of the supernatant was concentrated using a SpeedVac up to a final volume of approximately 80 μl. 50 μl of the concentrated sample was dispensed in the glass vial for the MS-based metabolomic profile run. To each glass vial containing 50 μl of the metabolite extract, 0.5 μl of reserpine (10 μg/ml) was added as an internal control for capturing instrumental variation. Vials containing metabolite extract were then placed in an autosampler for metabolite profiling.</p>	<p>The extracted samples containing internal standard were analyzed using Ultraperformance Liquid Chromatography-High-Resolution Mass Spectrometry (UPLC-HRMS) methods involving a Thermo Scientific Dionex UltiMate 3000 System coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer. The capillary, probe, autosampler and column temperatures were 340, 380, 4 and 40 °C, respectively. A C18 column, i.e., Hypersil GOLD (100 x 2.1 mm, 1.9 µm particle size, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), was used for metabolite separation using water and 100% methanol as eluents both with 0.1% formic acid added, in a gradient lasting 20 min. The gradient consecutively reached 1% of methanol at 2 min, 50% methanol at 5 min, 98% methanol at 14 min, constant at 98% until 17 min, 1% at 17.2 min, and constant at 1% methanol until 20 min with a flow rate of 0.350 ml/min.</p>	<p>Mass spectra data were collected on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive mass spectrometer in Electrospray Ionization (ESI) positive mode. Each sample was run in triplicates, where one run was set for MS/MS analysis for identification of the metabolites. The rest 2 runs for each sample were used as technical replicates for acquiring MS data of extracted metabolites. The resolution of the mass spectrometer was set at 140,000 for full MS and 17,500 for ddMS2 and scanned at a mass range 100-700 m/z. Sheath Gas flow rate at 42, Aux Gas rate at 10, and spray voltage at 3.8 kV.</p><p><br></p><p>The samples were run in batches, and each sample was analyzed in 3 technical replicates using MS only and MS2 modes. Each batch of samples had initial blank runs consisting of 50% methanol, and a single blank was run after every sample. QC control samples consisting of a pool of samples were also run after every 5 samples to check the consistency of the instrument performance.</p>	<p>Basic data analysis was done using <strong>Compound Discoverer 3.0 software</strong> (Thermo Fisher) with a threshold value of 100,000 for intensity marked as a signal. Experimental design, standard mix for instrument general quality check (QC), and internal standards for sample preparation QC were also set.</p><p><br></p><p>The internal standards were added at 2 different time points: while preparing samples to check sample preparation quality; while injecting samples in the mass spectrometer (MS) to check instrument functionality while running a particular sample.</p><p><br></p><p>After the sample run, during data analysis, the first step was to check the coefficient of variance (CV) in all the run sets, followed by CV among all the samples in a run and then PCA analysis of all QC pools run with each set. If the CV was less than 30% among the sets or individual sample runs, then the data was considered good to be taken forward (see <strong>Fig. 2</strong> of the paper associated with this study). Also, proper segregation of QC pools for all the sets represented the proper execution of the experiment (see <strong>Fig. 3</strong> of the paper associated with this study). This included the addition of various adduct ions naturally present in the plasma, which might alter the ion formation of the metabolite targets in question. Hence the most abundant adduct ions were selected for the exploration of the target metabolites.</p>	<p>Analysis of acquired data was initially performed with the <strong>Compound Discoverer 3.0 software</strong> (Thermo Fisher) for metabolite identification/quantitation, chromatography peak alignment, mass spectrum visualization, and statistical analysis. The workflow template used in <strong>Compound Discoverer 3.0</strong> includes unknown compound detection, peak alignment, predicting the compound’s composition, and database searching against <strong>ChemSpider</strong>, which comprises of <strong>BioCyc</strong>, <strong>KEGG</strong>, and <strong>Human Metabolome Database (HMDB)</strong> with a mass tolerance of 5 ppm. For compound detection signal to noise ratio (S/N) was kept as 3, and the minimum peak intensity was 10^6. To assign compound annotation on MS/MS level, 3 different data sources, such as <strong>mzCloud</strong>, <strong>ChemSpider</strong>, and <strong>Metabolika</strong>, with a mass tolerance of 5 ppm, were used<strong>[1</strong>]. All the duplicate runs were treated as individual samples in the data analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>The <strong>Compound Discoverer 3.0</strong> analyzed output had 3 types of metabolite representation:</p><p><strong>1)</strong> Metabolites with name, molecular formula, molecular mass, and retention time.</p><p><strong>2)</strong> Metabolites without a name but with the molecular formula, molecular mass, and retention time.</p><p><strong>3)</strong> Metabolites with molecular mass and retention time but no name and molecular formula.</p><p><br></p><p>No filtering was done at the beginning of the study to avoid loss of the data, and all the metabolites were given a code with the prefix <strong>Meta_XX</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p>The QC pools and internal standards were checked in different batches (see <strong>Fig. S1</strong>, <strong>A</strong>, in the paper associated with this study) to decide the normalization and transformation strategy. Spearman correlation analysis of each cohort and all the samples was performed to check the data quality, and the samples having <strong>R2</strong> above 0.5 were considered for further post-processing. Also, the CV of the internal standard was checked across the set of samples and individual samples. The samples passing CV% &lt;30 for internal standards were only used in the data analysis. The features with over 30% missing values were filtered out, and the missing value imputation was done separately for each cohort through KNN (k-nearest neighboring) in <strong>Metaboanalyst 4.0[2][3]</strong>. The data was then log-transformed and median normalized, which was followed by a 2-tailed unpaired student t-test for each pair of cohorts. The compounds having FDR adjusted p-value less than 0.05 and log2 fold change above 1.5 were considered statistically differentially expressed metabolites. The experimental MS/MS spectra of the significant metabolites were compared to available reference MS/MS spectra in <strong>METLIN</strong> and <strong>HMDB</strong> for MSI level annotation.</p><p><br></p><p>The significant metabolites were also checked for their correlation within sample cohorts and were analyzed using <strong>Cytoscape 3.8.2</strong> with <strong>Java 11.0.6</strong> application based on pathway mapping against <strong>KEGG</strong> pathway library for <em>Homo sapiens</em>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Exposome analysis:</strong></p><p>The list of all the unannotated metabolites post CD analysis was taken for exposome exploration analysis. The analysis was done for all COVID-19 patients from the 3 categories: <strong>negative</strong>, <strong>NSC</strong>, and <strong>SC</strong>. The unannotated metabolites from the list was taken and mapped on the blood exposome database. The basis of mapping exposomes was the molecular formula, which was the common parameter between the raw files and the blood exposome database. The compounds with redundant chemical formulas were removed from the database to avoid ambiguity, which lead to loss of data. Once the exposomes were discovered for each cohort of patients, then comparative analysis was done for the groups: <strong>COVID-19(+)</strong> vs. <strong>COVID-19(-)</strong>, and <strong>NSC</strong> vs. <strong>SC</strong>. Subsequently, T-test and fold change analysis was performed, and an exposome was considered statistically significant if it has p-value &lt; 0.05 and fold change &gt;1.5.</p><p><br></p><p>A detailed search was carried for all of the significantly altered exposomes to correlate them to various drugs, diseases and food habits. Relevant literatures were obtained from <strong>PubChem</strong>, <strong>PubMed</strong>, <strong>blood exposome database</strong>, <strong>US environmental protection agency website</strong> and <strong>DrugBank</strong> online. This information was correlated with the drugs and treatment administered to the patients to get an insight on how these compounds affect the biological pathways during the course of COVID 19.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Pathway analysis:</strong></p><p>The significant metabolites from both plasma and swab metabolite cohort were used to map the human metabolomics pathways. The list of the significant metabolites was subjected to <strong>Metaboanalyst 4.0</strong> under pathway analysis function. The metabolites mapped to <strong>HMDB IDs</strong> or <strong>KEGG IDs</strong> were segregated and the remaining unmapped metabolites were manually searched for their <strong>HMDB/KEGG ID</strong>. The final list of all the significant metabolites was again subjected to <strong>Metaboanalyst 4.0</strong> and pathways analysis using Fisher’s exact test as enrichment method, scatter plot for visualization, relative betweenness centrality for topology mapping, and <strong>SMPDB</strong> pathway library.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Züllig T, Zandl-Lang M, Trötzmüller M, Hartler J, Plecko B, Köfeler HC. A Metabolomics Workflow for Analyzing Complex Biological Samples Using a Combined Method of Untargeted and Target-List Based Approaches. Metabolites. 2020 Aug 25;10(9):342. doi:10.3390/metabo10090342. PMID:32854199.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Chong J, Wishart DS, Xia J. Using MetaboAnalyst 4.0 for Comprehensive and Integrative Metabolomics Data Analysis. Curr Protoc Bioinformatics. 2019 Dec;68(1):e86. doi:10.1002/cpbi.86. PMID:31756036.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Chong J, Xia J. MetaboAnalystR: an R package for flexible and reproducible analysis of metabolomics data. Bioinformatics. 2018 Dec 15;34(24):4313-4314. doi:10.1093/bioinformatics/bty528. PMID:29955821.</p>

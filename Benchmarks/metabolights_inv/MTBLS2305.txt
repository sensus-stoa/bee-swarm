Study Identifier	MTBLS2305
Study Title	Multi-omics analysis of xylem sap uncovers dynamic modulation of poplar defenses by ammonium and nitrate
Study Description	Xylem sap is the major transport route for nutrients from roots to shoots. In the present study, we investigated how variations in nitrogen (N) nutrition affected the metabolome and proteome of xylem sap and the growth of the xylem endophyte Brennaria salicis, and we also report transcriptional re-wiring of leaf defenses in poplar (Populus × canescens). We supplied poplars with high, intermediate or low concentrations of ammonium or nitrate. We identified 288 unique proteins in xylem sap. Approximately 85% of the xylem sap proteins were shared among ammonium- and nitrate-supplied plants. The number of proteins increased with increasing N supply but the major functional categories (catabolic processes, cell wall-related enzymes, defense) were unaffected. Ammonium nutrition caused higher abundances of amino acids and carbohydrates, whereas nitrate caused higher malate levels in xylem sap. Pipecolic acid and N-hydroxy-pipecolic acid increased, whereas salicylic acid and jasmonoyl-isoleucine decreased, with increasing N nutrition. Untargeted metabolome analyses revealed 2179 features in xylem sap, of which 863 were differentially affected by N treatments. We identified 124 metabolites, mainly from specialized metabolism of the groups of salicinoids, phenylpropanoids, phenolics, flavonoids, and benzoates. Their abundances increased with decreasing N, except coumarins. Brennaria salicis growth was reduced in nutrient-supplemented xylem sap of low- and high- NO<sub>3</sub> <sup>-</sup> -fed plants compared to that of NH<sub>4</sub> <sup>+</sup> -fed plants. The drastic changes in xylem sap composition caused massive changes in the transcriptional landscape of leaves and recruited defenses related to systemic acquired and induced systemic resistance. Our study uncovers unexpected complexity and variability of xylem composition with consequences for plant defenses.

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong><em>Populus tremula x Populus alba</em></strong> <em>(</em><strong><em>P. x canescens</em></strong><em>)</em>, clone INRA 717 1B4 (INRA, Nancy, France), plants were<em> in vitro</em> multiplied by micro cuttings and grown on MS medium, previously described<strong>[1]</strong>, for 4 weeks between 23-24 °C, 60-85 μE/m^2/s photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) and a 16/8 h day/night regime. Rooted plantlets were transferred to the greenhouse and further cultivated at 21 °C under ambient light with additional illumination of 150 μE/m^2/s PAR and a 16/8 h day/night cycle. They were potted into 0.65 L containing a mixture of washed and twice autoclaved sand 0.71-1.21 mm and 0.40-0.80 mm particle size (8:2, v/v) and soil at a ratio of 10:2 (v/v). For acclimation to greenhouse conditions, the plants were covered individually with a transparent plastic beaker for a week. During the acclimation phase, all plants were daily irrigated with modified Long Ashton nutrient containing 2 mM KNO3. Excess nutrient solution ran off. The acclimated plants were divided into 2 groups and watered daily with modified Long Ashton nutrient solution, containing either <strong>ammonium</strong> (2 mM NH4Cl) or <strong>nitrate</strong> (2 mM KNO3) as nitrogen (N) sources. The plants were transferred into 3 L pots&nbsp;after 4 weeks of greenhouse cultivation. After 8 weeks of ammonium or nitrate pretreatment, each group was split into 3 subgroups and supplied with 0.4 mM (<strong>low</strong>), 2.0 mM (<strong>medium</strong>) or 8.0 mM (<strong>high</strong>) NH4Cl or KNO3, respectively, for 3 weeks.&nbsp;Each of the 6 treatment groups consisted of 10-12 individual plants, which were randomized regularly.</p><p><br></p><p>After 13 weeks of greenhouse cultivation, the plants (n=10-12 per treatment) were harvested randomly within a day, starting after 3 h in the light phase. For xylem sap harvest, a modified protocol using the natural root pressure was used<strong>[2]</strong>. Immediately after removing the shoot, a strip of the top 1.5 cm of bark of the stump was removed by cutting with a razor blade through the bark and cambium and then peeling it off. The exposed wood was washed with deionized H2O and blotted with a paper towel. When the first drops of xylem sap accumulated on the cut wood surface, they were removed by washing with deionized H2O and the stump was blotted. Then, a 4 cm long piece of tight-fitting tube was slipped over the exposed wood and secured at the stump with sticky tape. The sap was pipetted into a 1.5 mL ice cold Eppendorf every 10 min using a separate glass pipette for each plant. The xylem sap was collected for exactly 4 h and the samples were stored at -80 °C.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Müller A, Volmer K, Mishra-Knyrim M, Polle A. Growing poplars for research with and without mycorrhizas. Front Plant Sci. 2013 Aug 27;4:332. doi:10.3389/fpls.2013.00332. PMID:23986772.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Schurr U. Xylem sap sampling—new approaches to an old topic.Trends in Plant Science. 1998;3(8):293-298. doi:10.1016/S1360-1385(98)01275-8.</p>	<p>Briefly, methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE), methanol and H2O based two-phase extraction<strong>[1]</strong> was used to extract polar, semipolar as well as nonpolar metabolites from the xylem sap. A total of 200 µL thawed xylem sap were added to 750 mL methanol and 2.5 mL MTBE in a Kimble glass tube. The extracts were vortexed and shaken for 1 h in the dark at 6 °C, subsequently, 600 µL H2O were added. After 10 min of incubation, samples were centrifuged at 800 x g for 15 min, resulting in the separation of an upper (nonpolar) and lower (polar) phase. Both phases were separately transferred to new glass vials. Another phase separation was done with the polar phase. The newly formed nonpolar phase was added to the previous formed nonpolar phase from the first phase separation, dried under constant N2 stream and subsequently dissolved in 0.5 mL methanol, vortexed, transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf, centrifuged for 2 min at 16,000 x g and dried under an N2 stream. Prior to analysis, the extracts were dissolved in 100 µL acetonitrile:H2O (2:3, v/v) and&nbsp;transferred into a LC glass vial. The polar phase was transferred into a 2 mL Eppendorf&nbsp;and dried under N2 stream. Samples were then dissolved in 100 µL of methanol, mixed, shaken for 5 min, centrifuged for 5 min at 16,000 x g and dried under N2 stream. Subsequently the samples were dissolved in 15 µL of methanol by shaking for 10 min. Then, 15 µL acetonitrile were added and the samples were shaken for further 10 min. After adding 100 µL of H2O, the samples were shaken for 10 min and then centrifuged for 10 min at 16,000 x g. A total of 100 µL of the samples were transferred to a glass micro vial. Samples of the polar and the nonpolar extraction phases were covered with argon and immediately used for metabolic fingerprinting analysis.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Matyash V, Liebisch G, Kurzchalia TV, Shevchenko A, Schwudke D. Lipid extraction by methyl-tert-butyl ether for high-throughput lipidomics. J Lipid Res. 2008 May;49(5):1137-46. doi:10.1194/jlr.D700041-JLR200. Epub 2008 Feb 16. PMID:18281723.</p>	<p>The analysis was performed using a ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC 1290 Infinity; Agilent Technologies) coupled to high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS 6540 UHD Accurate-Mass Q-TOF; Agilent Technologies) as described previously<strong>[1]</strong>. Solvent system for UPLC analyses with the ACQUITY HSS T3 column (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA): solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid), solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). Briefly, set the temperature of the sample manager to 10 °C and the column oven temperature to 40 °C. Set the flow rate to 0.2 mL/min and the injection volume to values between 0.5 and 2.0 μL/min. Set the PDA detector to 190-800 nm with a resolution of 1.2 nm. Use acetonitrile as strong and acetonitrile:water (1:9, v/v) as weak wash solution. Use the gradient: 0.0 min: 99% A, 1% B; 0.5 min: 99% A, 1% B; 3.0 min: 80% A, 20% B; 8.0 min: 0% A, 100% B; 10.0 min: 0% A, 100% B; 10.1 min: 99% A, 1% B; 14.0 min: 99% A, 1% B.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Feussner K, Feussner I. Comprehensive LC-MS-Based Metabolite Fingerprinting Approach for Plant and Fungal-Derived Samples. Methods Mol Biol. 2019;1978:167-185. doi:10.1007/978-1-4939-9236-2_11. PMID:31119663.</p>	<p>No data transformation was conducted. All files contain raw mass spectrometric information recorded by the HRMS instrument (6540 UHD Accurate-Mass Q-TOF, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Peak picking and alignment were performed with <strong>MassHunter Profinder B.08.00</strong> (Agilent Technologies).</p>	<p>Metabolite annotation was done by database search based on the accurate mass information. The chemical identity of compounds were determined by interpretation of HRMS/MS spectra<strong>[1][2]</strong>.&nbsp;For further data processing, statistics, visualization as well as for data mining the <strong>MarVis-Suite</strong> toolbox (<a href='http://marvis.gobics.de' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>http://marvis.gobics.de</a>) was used<strong>[3]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Abreu IN, Johansson AI, Sokolowska K, Niittylä T, Sundberg B, Hvidsten TR, Street NR, Moritz T. A metabolite roadmap of the wood-forming tissue in Populus tremula. New Phytol. 2020 Dec;228(5):1559-1572. doi:10.1111/nph.16799. Epub 2020 Aug 27. PMID:32648607.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Green KA, Berry D, Feussner K, Eaton CJ, Ram A, Mesarich CH, Solomon P, Feussner I, Scott B. Lolium perenne apoplast metabolomics for identification of novel metabolites produced by the symbiotic fungus Epichloë festucae. New Phytol. 2020 Jul;227(2):559-571. doi:10.1111/nph.16528. Epub 2020 Apr 13. PMID:32155669.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Kaever A, Landesfeind M, Feussner K, Mosblech A, Heilmann I, Morgenstern B, Feussner I, Meinicke P. MarVis-Pathway: integrative and exploratory pathway analysis of non-targeted metabolomics data. Metabolomics. 2015;11(3):764-777. doi:10.1007/s11306-014-0734-y. Epub 2014 Oct 10. PMID:25972773.</p>	<p>The extracts were analysed by UHPLC (UHPLC 1290 Infinity; Agilent Technologies) coupled to HRMS (6540 UHD Accurate-Mass Q-TOF; Agilent Technologies) in positive and negative ionization mode, as described previously<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, operate the Q-TOF MS instrument with a detection frequency of 4 GHz in the targeted MS/MS mode. Acquire the data in a mass range of 50-1700 m/z for negative and positive ESI. Set capillary voltage to 3500 V, nozzle voltage to 100 V and fragmentor voltage to 175 V, respectively. Set drying gas flow to 8 L/min and sheath gas flow to 11 L/min, respectively. Set nebulizer pressure to 35 psi, sheath gas temperature to 350 °C and gas temperature to 300 °C. Use reference mass correction with the reference mass solution.</p><p><br></p><p>Data acquisition of accurate mass data was performed either with <strong>MassHunter Acquisition</strong> <strong>B.03.01</strong> (Agilent Technologies) and analyzed with <strong>MassHunter Qualitative Analysis</strong> (Agilent Technologies).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Feussner K, Feussner I. Comprehensive LC-MS-Based Metabolite Fingerprinting Approach for Plant and Fungal-Derived Samples. Methods Mol Biol. 2019;1978:167-185. doi:10.1007/978-1-4939-9236-2_11. PMID:31119663.</p>

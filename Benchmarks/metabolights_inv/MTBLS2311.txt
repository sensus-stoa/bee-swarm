Study Identifier	MTBLS2311
Study Title	UPLC-MS-Based Serum Metabolic Profiling Reveals Potential Biomarkers for Predicting Propofol Responsiveness in Females
Study Description	<p>Although previous studies have shown that certain factors interfere with the sensitivity of propofol, the mechanisms for interindividual variability in response to propofol remain unclear. This study aimed to screen the metabolites to predict patients' sensitivity to propofol and to identify metabolic pathways to explore possible mechanisms associated with propofol resistance. Sera from 40 female patients undergoing elective hysteroscopic surgery in a prospective cohort propofol study were obtained before the administration of propofol. The patients' responsiveness to propofol was differentiated based on propofol effect-site concentration. Serum samples from two sets, a discovery set (<em>n</em> = 24) and an independent validation set (<em>n</em> = 16), were analyzed using ultraperformance liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry based untargeted metabolomics. In the discovery set, 494 differential metabolites were screened out, and then 391 potential candidate biomarkers with the area under receiver operating characteristic curve &gt;0.80 were selected. Pathway analysis showed that the pathway of glycerophospholipid metabolism was the most influential pathway. In the independent validation set, six potential biomarkers enabled the discrimination of poor responders from good and intermediate responders, which might be applied to predict propofol sensitivity. </p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Choice of Clinical Samples</strong></p><p><strong>Serum </strong>samples were collected from patients recruited in a clinical trial of female patients undergoing propofol-based intravenous anesthesia for elective hysteroscopic surgery. The detailed descriptions of the inclusion/exclusion criteria and induction of anesthesia have been published previously<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, no premedication was administrated. When a patient arrived at an operating room, 5 mL of venous blood sample was collected. Then, the serum was separated and immediately stored at −80 °C. The target effect-site concentration of propofol was initially set at 1.0 μg/mL and was gradually titrated to achieve the targeted sedative depth (Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation scale (MOAA/S) score 1) and then the propofol effect-site concentration was recorded. No co-medication was administered during the study period. A propofol dose−distribution curve was drawn based on the effect-site concentration of the participants (detailed information has been described previously)<strong>[1]</strong>. In a <strong>discovery </strong>set, 24 <strong>serum </strong>samples were collected randomly from the lowest and the top 15% of the propofol dose−distribution curve, defined as good (n = 12) and poor responders (n = 12), respectively. Moreover, an independent <strong>validation </strong>set of 16 <strong>serum </strong>samples was used to validate the candidate biomarkers, including 4 <strong>serum </strong>samples randomly selected from the lowest 15% of propofol dose− distribution curve, 8 <strong>serum </strong>samples randomly selected from the middle 70% of propofol dose−distribution curve, and 4 <strong>serum </strong>samples randomly selected from the top 15% of propofol dose− distribution curve.</p><p><br></p><p>Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University and written informed consents were obtained from each patient.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Chemicals and Reagents</strong></p><p>Methanol, formic acid, and acetonitrile were purchased from CNW Technologies GmbH (Düsseldorf, Germany). L-2-Chlorophenylalanine was obtained from Shanghai Hengchuang Bio-technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Li, R. Y.; Lin, M.; Jiang, H. Y.; Wen, S. H.; Shen, J. T.; Huang, W. Q.; Zhang, X. Y., Impact of Anxiety or Depression Symptoms on Propofol Requirements for Sedation in Females: A Prospective Cohort Study. J Clin Pharmacol 2020, 60, (10), 1376-1384.</p>	<p>A frozen <strong>serum </strong>sample was thawed at room temperature. 10 µL of internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine, 0.3mg/mL) was mixed with 100 µL of <strong>serum </strong>sample and vortexed for 10 sec. The protein was precipitated with 300 µL of methanol and acetonitrile (2/1, vol/vol) followed by vortex for 1 min, and the mixtures were extracted in an ultrasonic ice-water bath for 10 min, then left to stand at -20 °C for 30 min and centrifuged for 10 min (at 13,000 rpm, 4 °C). 300 µL of supernatant was dried, then redissolved in 400 µL of methanol and water (1/4, vol/vol) followed by vortex for 30 sec, and centrifuged for 10 min (at 13,000 rpm, 4 °C). Finally, 150 µL of supernatants were taken and filtered through 0.22 μm organic phase filters. <strong>Quality control</strong> (QC) samples were made by combining equal volumes of each extracted supernatants.</p>	<p>The serum samples were separated by a Shimadzu Nexera ultra-performance liquid chromatograph (UPLC) (Shimadzu Co., Kyoto, Japan) with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) at the column temperature of 45 °C and a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. A gradient elution of the mobile phase composed with 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile (mobile phase B) was adopted: 5 - 100% mobile phase B from 0 to 14 min; 100% mobile phase B held for 1 min; then returned to 5% mobile phase B from 15.1 to 16 min for equilibrium.</p>	<p>&nbsp;Mass spectral (MS) data were acquired from a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in full scan mode (m/z 125 to 1000) under both positive and negative ion modes. The capillary temperature was set to 320 °C, with a spray voltage of 3,500 V in the positive ion mode and of -3,000 V in the negative mode. Gas flow rates for sheath and auxiliary were set to 40 and 10 arbitrary units for the positive ion mode, and to 35 and 8 arbitrary units for the negative ion mode.</p>	<p>No data transformation information was supplied for this study.</p>	<p>The raw MS data were processed with the <strong>Progenesis QI </strong>software <strong>version 2.3 </strong>(Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle, UK).</p><p><br></p><p>Orthogonal partial least-squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) conducted in the <strong>R </strong>programming language <strong>version 4.0.3 </strong>(R Foundation for Statistical Computing, 2020) was employed to evaluate the metabolic alterations between good and poor responders. 200 iterations of response permutation tests were conducted to validate the OPLS-DA model and to prevent the model from overfitting. The variable importance for the projection (VIP) values indicating the importance of the variables to the group discrimination were obtained from OPLS-DA model. Fold change (FC) was calculated as peak area of poor responders / peak area of good responders. The two-tailed Student’s t test was performed to compare the FC and the P values were corrected by Benjamini-Hochberg’s false discovery rate (FDR). Metabolites with VIP values larger than 1.0, FC larger than 1.50 or less than 0.67, and FDR less than 0.05 were considered as differential metabolites. These criteria for differential metabolites selection are adopted in many studies<strong> [1-3].</strong> The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted using <strong>SPSS </strong>software <strong>version 25.0 </strong>(SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) to identify the potential candidate biomarkers (the area under the curve &gt; 0.8) in the discovery set and was then applied to validate potential biomarkers in the validation set, which is commonly used to assess the predictive power of potential biomarkers in Metabolomics study <strong>[4],[5]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Zhao, Y.; Nie, S.; Yi, M.; Wu, N.; Wang, W.; Zhang, Z.; Yao, Y.; Wang, D., UPLC-QTOF/MS-based metabolomics analysis of plasma reveals an effect of Xue-Fu-Zhu-Yu capsules on blood-stasis syndrome in CHD rats. J Ethnopharmacol 2019, 241, 111908.</p><p><strong>[2] </strong>Chao, Y.; Gao, S.; Li, N.; Zhao, H.; Qian, Y.; Zha, H.; Chen, W.; Dong, X., Lipidomics Reveals the Therapeutic Effects of EtOAc Extract of Orthosiphon stamineus Benth. on Nephrolithiasis. Front Pharmacol 2020, 11, 1299.</p><p><strong>[3]&nbsp;</strong>Kouassi Nzoughet, J.; Guehlouz, K.; Leruez, S.; Gohier, P.; Bocca, C.; Muller, J.; Blanchet, O.; Bonneau, D.; Simard, G.; Milea, D.; Procaccio, V.; Lenaers, G.; Chao de la Barca, J. M.; Reynier, P., A Data Mining Metabolomics Exploration of Glaucoma. Metabolites 2020, 10, (2).</p><p><strong>[4] </strong>Zeng, J.; Yin, P. Y.; Tan, Y. X.; Dong, L. W.; Hu, C.

Study Identifier	MTBLS2356
Study Title	Dissolved metabolites from the Bermuda Atlantic Time-series Study: 2016-2019
Study Description	<p>Organic carbon in seawater plays a significant role in the global carbon cycle. The concentration and composition of dissolved organic carbon, operationally defined in this project as organic carbon that passes through a 0.2 µm filter, reflect the actions of the biological community and chemical reactions that occur in seawater. Here, we repeatedly sampled the oligotrophic northwest Sargasso Sea in the vicinity of the Bermuda Atlantic Time-Series Study site (BATS) to quantitatively follow select known compounds within the pool of dissolved organic matter in the upper 1000 meters of the water column over a four-year period. Metabolite concentrations revealed patterns with depth and time with most metabolites showing surface enrichment and lower concentrations with depth. Select metabolites had a pattern of increased and decreased concentrations throughout the year, which was observed in each of the years sampled. Vitamins, including pantothenic acid, biotin, and riboflavin, presented annual increases in the winter period when mixed layer depths are deepest. Light-sensitive riboflavin also showed significant decreases during daylight hours under diel sampling. The metabolites examined in this study are all components of central carbon metabolism. By examining these metabolites at finer resolution and in a relatively long time series, we have clues on microbial actions in marine systems, data which are fundamental&nbsp;to understanding the chemical response of marine systems to future changes in climate.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Seawater from the Bermuda Atlantic Time-series Study (BATS) site (31°40′N 64°10′W) was collected during multiple cruises spanning 2016 to 2019. Seawater (4-10 L) was collected using Niskin bottles on a conductivity, temperature, and depth (CTD) profiling rosette. Seawater was filtered through a 0.2 µm filter (Omnipore, EMD Millipore), and acidified with concentrated hydrochloric acid (~pH 2-3)<strong>[1][2]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p>Date (yyyymmdd) and time (hhmm) are given as UTC time.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Dittmar T, Koch B, Hertkorn N, Kattner G. A simple and efficient method for the solid-phase extraction of dissolved organic matter (SPE-DOM) from seawater. Limnol Oceanogr Methods 2008;6:230–5. doi:10.4319/lom.2008.6.230.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Longnecker K. (2015). Dissolved organic matter in newly formed sea ice and surface seawater. Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta 171: 39-49. doi:10.1016/j.gca.2015.08.014.</p>	<p>Dissolved organic molecules were extracted from the filtrate using solid phase extraction (SPE) modified styrene-divinylbenzene polymer (Agilent Bond Elut PPL). The PPL cartridges were first rinsed with 6 ml of methanol and then the filtrate was pulled through the cartridge via PTFE tubing using a vacuum pump<strong>[1]</strong>. The cartridge was rinsed with ~24 ml of 0.01 M HCl and then allowed to dry by pulling air over the cartridge for 5 min. The sample was then eluted with 6 ml of methanol into a glass test tube and transferred with a Pasteur pipette to an 8 ml amber vial. Once samples returned to land, the methanol extracts were evaporated to near dryness using a Vacufuge (Eppendorf) and reconstituted in 250 µl of 95:5 (v/v) water/acetonitrile with isotopically-labeled standards.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Dittmar T, Koch B, Hertkorn N, Kattner G. A simple and efficient method for the solid-phase extraction of dissolved organic matter (SPE-DOM) from seawater. Limnol Oceanogr Methods 2008;6:230–5. doi:10.4319/lom.2008.6.230.</p>	<p>Chromatography was performed on a Phenomenex C18 column (Synergi Fusion, 2.1 x 150 mm, 4 µm) as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. Eluent A (Milli-Q water with 0.1% formic acid) and Eluent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) were run at 250 µl/min in the following gradient: hold at 5% B for 2 min; ramp to 65% B for 16 min; ramp to 100% B for 7 min and hold for 8 min. The column was re-equilibrated with the starting ratio of eluents for 8.5 min between sample analyses.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Kido Soule MC, Longnecker K, Johnson WM, Kujawinski EB. Environmental metabolomics: Analytical strategies. Mar Chem 2015;177:374–87. doi:10.1016/j.marchem.2015.06.029.</p>	<p>Analytes were measured using heated electrospray ionization (ESI) and a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific TSQ Vantage) operated under selected reaction monitoring mode (SRM), as described previously<strong>[1]</strong>. Quantification and confirmation SRM transitions were monitored for each analyte.Samples were run in both positive and negative ion modes using a 5 μl injection for each.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Kido Soule MC, Longnecker K, Johnson WM, Kujawinski EB. Environmental metabolomics: Analytical strategies. Mar Chem 2015;177:374–87. doi:10.1016/j.marchem.2015.06.029.</p>	<p>Raw data files were converted to <strong>mzML</strong> format using <strong>msConvert[1]</strong> prior to processing with <strong>MAVEN[2][3]</strong> to identify and integrate peaks for all samples and standards. Peaks were identified by their unique retention time, precursor m/z, and product m/z. 2 SRM transitions were monitored for quantification and confirmation. Metabolite concentrations were calculated using a standard curve of at least 5 points and were corrected for the efficiency of the solid phase extraction step using the previously published<strong>[4]</strong>. Concentrations are given in pM.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Chambers MC, Maclean B, Burke R, Amodei D, Ruderman DL, Neumann S, Gatto L, Fischer Bet al. A cross-platform toolkit for mass spectrometry and proteomics. Nat Biotechnol. 2012 Oct;30(10):918-20. doi:10.1038/nbt.2377. PMID:23051804.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Melamud E, Vastag L, Rabinowitz JD. Metabolomic analysis and visualization engine for LC-MS data. Anal Chem. 2010 Dec 1;82(23):9818-26. doi:10.1021/ac1021166. PMID:21049934.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Clasquin MF, Melamud E, Rabinowitz JD. LC-MS data processing with MAVEN: a metabolomic analysis and visualization engine. Curr Protoc Bioinformatics. 2012 Mar;Chapter 14:Unit14.11. doi:10.1002/0471250953.bi1411s37. PMID:22389014.</p><p><strong>[4]</strong> Johnson WM, Kido Soule MC, Kujawinski EB.

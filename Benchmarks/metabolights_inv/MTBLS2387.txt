Study Identifier	MTBLS2387
Study Title	NMR-based metabolic profiling of children with premature adrenarche
Study Description	<h4><strong>INTRODUCTION:</strong> Premature adrenarche (PA) for long time was considered a benign condition but later has been connected to various diseases in childhood and adulthood which remains controversial.</h4><h4><strong>OBJECTIVE:</strong> To investigate the effect of premature adrenarche on the metabolic phenotype, and correlate the clinical and biochemical data with the metabolic profile of children with PA.</h4><h4><strong>METHODS:</strong> Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based untargeted and targeted metabolomic approach in combination with multivariate and univariate statistical analysis applied to study the metabolic profiles of children with PA. Plasma, serum, and urine samples were collected from fifty-two children with Idiopathic PA and forty-eight age-matched controls from the division of Pediatric Endocrinology of the University Hospital of Patras were enrolled.</h4><h4><strong>RESULTS:</strong> Metabolomic results showed that plasma and serum glucose, myo-inositol, amino acids, a population of unsaturated lipids, and esterified cholesterol were higher and significantly different in PA children. In the metabolic profiles of children with PA and age-matched control group a gradual increase of glucose and myo-inositol levels was observed in serum and plasma, which was positively correlated their body mass index standard deviation score (BMI SDS) values respectively. Urine <sup>1</sup>H NMR metabolic fingerprint of PA children showed positive correlation and a clustering-dependent relationship with their BMI and bone age (BA) respectively.</h4><h4><strong>CONCLUSION:</strong> This study provides evidence that PA driven metabolic changes begin during the childhood and PA may has an inductive role in a BMI-driven increase of specific metabolites. Finally, urine may be considered as the best biofluid for identification of the PA metabolism as it reflects more clearly the PA metabolic fingerprint.</h4>

Study Protocol Description	<p>A total of 52 children with <strong>PA </strong>(49 girls and 3 boys) and 48 <strong>healthy </strong>children were enrolled in the study. The clinical inclusion criteria for PA were: onset of signs of adrenarche before the age of 8 years for girls and 9 years for boys and the presence of pubic and/or axillary hair. Patients with clitoris enlargement and pubertal children were excluded as well. All patients were followed for at least 6 months and none entered puberty. None of the children with the endocrinologic disorder had underline adrenal pathology as a cause of adrenarche. None of the PA children and control subjects were on any medication. Birth weight and length were recorded from the national book of child's health. The Control group consisted of girls attending our community pediatric outpatient clinic for routine health visits and screening blood tests, or our paediatric department for preoperative assessment for minor surgeries. Age, height (measured by a Harpenden stadiometer) and weight were measured, and pubertal status was clinically assessed according to Tanner stages. Bone age from the patients was assessed by the same senior pediatric endocrinologist (D. Chrysis) based on X-Rays from the left hand and wrist evaluated according to Greulich and Pyle atlas. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Patras. All children and their parents were informed, and written consent was obtained before participation in the study.</p><p><br></p><p>The 3 biofluids were collected, stored and prepared for NMR analysis according to different protocols suitable for blood derivatives and urine. The protocol adaptations applied on previously published protocols<strong>[1]</strong>. Sample assemblage was performed at the Department of Paediatric Endocrinology while storage and NMR sample preparation were carried out at the Department of Pharmacy of Patras University.</p><p><br></p><p>Fasting plasma and serum samples were withdrawn from donors using BD Vacutainer K3-EDTA spray-coated tubes and BD Vacutainer SST, respectively. Serum samples were mixed mildly, and they have been left 30 min at room temperature for the completion of the clotting cascade. All blood samples were centrifuged at 1,500 x g for 10 min at RT. Supernatant from each sample was aliquoted in 2 or 3 fractions of 600 μL. The number of fractions was determined by the initial sample quantity. Aliquots were stored in 2 mL cryovials at -80 °C.</p><p><br></p><p>Urine samples were collected in fasting state and were stored immediately at 4 °C for a maximum of 2 h before their centrifugation at 2,500 x g for 10 min at RT. Supernatant from each sample was aliquoted in 3 fractions of 1 mL and they were stored in 2 mL cryovials at -80 °C.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Bernini P, Bertini I, Luchinat C, Nincheri P, Staderini S, Turano P. Standard operating procedures for pre-analytical handling of blood and urine for metabolomic studies and biobanks. J Biomol NMR. 2011 Apr;49(3-4):231-43. doi:10.1007/s10858-011-9489-1. PMID:21380509.</p>	<p>No extractions were performed in this study.</p>	<p>NMR samples prepared by thawing aliquots of the collected and frozen fluids followed by the addition of a suitable buffer. Hence, plasma and serum samples were mixed with a buffer of pH=7.42 (0.14 M Na2HPO4, 4% NaN3 in H2O). In the buffer, 0.5 mM 4,4-dimethyl-4-silapentane-1-sulfonic acid (DSS) was also added as a chemical shift reference. Buffer used for urine samples prepared in 100% D2O and pH=7.42 (1.5 M KH2PO4, 0.05 mM DSS and 4% NaN3 2 mM). Plasma and serum samples were vortexed for 30 s and for each one a total of 300 μL were mixed with 240 μL buffer and 60 μL D2O to a total volume of 600 μL. Correspondingly, 540 μL of each urine sample were mixed with 60 μL buffer and were vortexed for 30 s. The final step before NMR experiments was the transfer of 550 μL in a 5 mm NMR tube (Bruker BioSpin srl.).</p>	<p>All samples were analyzed in a 700 MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker Avance III HD) equipped with a 5 mm cryogenically cooled TCI gradient probe and were recorded using TopSpin 3.5 pl7 software (Bruker BioSpin srl.).</p>	<p><strong>1D 1H NOESY </strong>presaturation (Nuclear Overhauser Effect SpectroscopY) pulse sequence was used to record <strong>1H NOESY</strong> spectra for blood derivatives at 310 K with 32 scans of 98.3 K data points with 9090.91 Hz spectral width. <strong>1D 1H CPMG</strong> (Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill) pulse sequence was used to record the <strong>1H CPMG</strong> spectra of the same samples at 310 K with 32 scans of 73.7 K data points with 9090.91 Hz spectral width. For urine samples, <strong>1D 1H NOESY</strong> presaturation experiment recorded at 300 K with 64 scans of 65.5 K data points with 10504.20 Hz spectral width. 2D homonuclear <strong>1H-1H J-resolved (J-res) </strong>spectra were also acquired for each plasma, serum and urine sample using 4 scans per 128 increments for F1 (spin-spin coupling constant axis) and 12.3 K data points for F2 (chemical shift axis). Spectral width used was 78.13 Hz and 11627.91 Hz for F1 and F2, respectively. Heteronuclear 2D NMR <strong>1H-13C HSQC</strong> and <strong>HMBC </strong>spectra also acquired, in order to aid, the metabolite-peak assignment procedure. For <strong>1H-13C HSQC</strong>, a carbon–proton coupling constant of 145 Hz was applied, 90° pulse lengths of 14.11 μs and 15.400 μs were selected for 1H and 13C respectively, an interscan delay of 1.5 s and a pulse recovery delay of 200 μs was used, 1024 data points were collected in F2 dimension and the spectral width used was of 11160.714 Hz for F2. For 1H-13C HMBC a carbon–proton coupling constant of 1 Hz was applied. For<strong> 1H-13C HMBC</strong>, a carbon–proton coupling constant of 1 Hz was applied with a delay for evolution of long-range couplings set at 8 Hz, an interscan delay of 1.5 s and a pulse recovery delay of 200 μs was used, with 128 scans and 2048 data points were collected in F2 and the spectral width used was of 11160.714 Hz for F2.</p>	<p>Before the statistical analysis, all NMR spectra were pre-processed in 2 steps so as to become comparable. For the same reason, were also excluded 8, non-suitable for analysis, spectra of the total of 300 (see <strong>Table 2</strong> in the paper associated with this study). 0- and first-order phase correction of spectra were applied and subsequently, all spectra were aligned using <strong>TopSpin 4.0.7</strong> (Bruker BioSpin srl.). Both procedures were completed manually. Plasma and serum spectra were calibrated on the double resonance peak of anomeric proton of a-glucose at 5.24 ppm because of its greater chemical shift stability, while urine spectra calibrated on the DSS singlet at 0.00 ppm. The NMR spectral data were converted by <strong>AMIX software</strong> (Bruker BioSpin srl.) in bucket tables. Spectral regions 0.00-10.00 ppm were segmented into integrated spectral domains (buckets) of equal width (0.02 ppm) excluding the water signal. Furthermore, resonance peaks of the anticoagulant EDTA (H-EDTA, Ca2+-EDTA, Mg2+-EDTA) were excluded from the bucketing of plasma data, while DSS and Urea resonance peaks were excluded from the bucketing of urine data. A total of 451 buckets have been created for plasma <strong>1H CPMG</strong> spectra, 463 buckets for serum<strong> 1H CPMG </strong>spectra and 423 buckets for urine<strong> 1H NOESY</strong> spectra. Pareto scaling was selected as the most suitable scaling method for the blood samples and autoscaling for urine samples.</p>	<p>The identification of metabolites was confirmed by <strong>1D 1H NOESY-PRESAT </strong>and <strong>1H CPMG </strong>NMR spectra, homonuclear 2D <strong>1H-1H J-res </strong>and heteronuclear 2D <strong>1H-13C HSQC</strong> and <strong>HMBC </strong>NMR spectra. The metabolites assignment was non-automated and was performed by the free evaluation version of <strong>Chenomx NMR Suite 8.3,</strong> the databases <strong>HMDB</strong>, <strong>BMRB</strong>, <strong>DrugBank </strong>and the available bibliography.</p>

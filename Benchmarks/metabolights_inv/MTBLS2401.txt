Study Identifier	MTBLS2401
Study Title	Investigation of Brazilian grape juice metabolomic profile changes caused by methyl jasmonate pre-harvest treatment
Study Description	<p>Grape juice is a major source of potential health-promoting bioactive polyphenols, especially for children and those who do not consume wine. Since the subtropical climate may negatively affect the concentrations of grape polyphenols, especially anthocyanins, elicitors such as methyl jasmonate (MeJa) could be used to promote polyphenol biosynthesis. This work aimed at investigating the impact of MeJa treatment on grape juice produced <em>via</em> a traditional low-cost process from two <em>Vitis labrusca</em> cultivars and in two Brazilian regions. The untargeted LC-MS analytical protocol demonstrated that Isabel Precoce juices strongly benefited from MeJa treatment, especially regarding their anthocyanic profile, regardless of the cultivation region. Known MeJa markers in wine and <em>V. vinifera</em> grapes (flavanols, flavonols and stilbenes) in this experiment had mixed behaviours depending on the region/variety/cultivation. Moreover, it was found that all the detected hydroxycinnamates were influenced by the treatment, especially the concentration of their glucosides, which was increased. Glutathione, 2-S-glutathionyl caftaric acid and indole lactic acid glucoside were identified for the first time as MeJa treatment biomarkers in grape products, indicating a possible positive effect on juice antioxidant properties.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The experimental area (<strong>Figure S1A</strong>) was composed by Isabel Precoce and Concord grapes grafted onto 1103-Paulsen into different growing regions as described previously<strong>[1]</strong>. In the southeast, the experiment was performed in Caldas (21° 55′ 23″ S and 46° 23′ 15″ W, 1.131 m altitude), in a 7-year-old vineyard of Isabel Precoce and Concord trained on a vertical shoot positioning system with a between-row and within-row spacing of 2.5 x 1.5 m. In the south, the experiments were performed in Bento Gonçalves (29° 10′ 26″ S and 51° 31′ 7″ W, 671 m altitude) in a 5-year-old commercial vineyard of Isabel Precoce grapes and a 20-year-old vineyard of Concord trained on a tendone system with a between-row and within-row spacing of 2.8 x 1.5 m. For each region/cultivar 2 blocks were used, control versus treatment. For the south region, each block comprised 10 vines and 18 vines for the southeast (<strong>Figure S1A</strong>).</p><p><br></p><p>The MeJa solution was prepared and applied (<strong>Figure S1B</strong>), as described previously<strong>[1]</strong>, at a concentration of 10 mM of MeJa dissolved in water. While pure water was used for control group. Approximately 50 mL of treated solution were applied directly into the cluster by spraying at veraison with 3 applications (on days 1, 3 and 6), plus a single application approximately 2 weeks before harvest (approximately 15,5° Brix). The treatments were applied at least 2 h before rain.</p><p><br></p><p>The cultivar was manually destemmed and processed separately. The juice was extracted with a steam juicer, as described previously<strong>[2]</strong>. For each cultivar, 10 kg of berries were placed in the extraction chamber, and the juice extraction was accomplished with water vapor for 75 min. Followed by hot bottling in sterilized glass bottles closed with screw caps (<strong>Figure S1C</strong>). The juice samples were produced from the fruits of treated (MeJa) and control groups, processing in triplicate, generating 3 lots for each block. Juice samples were stored for 6 months at -20 °C, transported to Italy at -20 °C and kept at -80 °C until analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Moro L, Da Ros A, da Mota RV, Purgatto E, Mattivi F, Arapitsas P. LC-MS untargeted approach showed that methyl jasmonate application on Vitis labrusca L. grapes increases phenolics at subtropical Brazilian regions. Metabolomics. 2020 Jan 23;16(2):18. doi:10.1007/s11306-020-1641-z. PMID:31974665.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> da Mota RV, Glória MBA, de Souza BS, Peregrino I, Pimentel RM de A, Dias FAN, de Souza LC, de Souza AL, Regina M de A. Bioactive compounds and juice quality from selected grape cultivars. Bragantia. 2017;77(1):62-73. doi:10.1590/1678-4499.2016369.</p>	<p>For the juice analysis, all the samples were analyzed according to a randomized sequence (<a href='https://www.random.org/sequences/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.random.org/sequences/</a>) to avoid bias. The juice was diluted 1:3 with Milli-Q water, centrifuged for 3 min at 9000 x g and filtered with 0.2 μm PTFE filters (Millipore) into a 2 mL amber vial before LC-MS analysis. A quality control (QC) sample was prepared as a pooled mixture of all the samples.</p>	<p>A Waters Acquity UPLC coupled with an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface to a Synapt HDMS QTOF MS (Waters, Manchester, UK) operating in W-mode and controlled by <strong>MassLynx</strong> 4.1 software was used. The LC-MS conditions were described previously<strong>[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Arapitsas P, Speri G, Angeli A, Perenzoni D, Mattivi F. The influence of storage on the “chemical age” of red wines. Metabolomics. 2014;10(5):816-832. doi:10.1007/s11306-014-0638-x.</p>	<p>A Waters Acquity UPLC coupled via an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface to a Synapt HDMS QTOF MS (Waters, Manchester, UK) operating in W-mode and controlled by <strong>MassLynx</strong> 4.1 was used. Mass spectrometric data were collected by different runs in positive and negative ESI mode over a mass range 50-2000 amu with scan duration of 0.3 s in centroid mode. The transfer collision energy and trap collision energy were set at 6 and 4 V, respectively. The source parameters were set as follows: capillary 3 kV for positive scan and 2.5 kV for negative scan, sampling cone 25 V, extraction cone 3 V, source temperature 150 ºC, desolvation temperature 500 ºC, desolvation gas flow 1000 L/h and nebulizer gas 50 L/h. External calibration of the instrument was performed at the beginning of each batch of analysis by direct infusion of a sodium formate solution (10% formic acid/0.1 M NaOH/Acetonitrile at a ratio of 1/1/8) by controlling the mass accuracy from 40 to 2000 m/z (less than 5 ppm) and mass resolution (over 14000 FWHM). LockMass calibration was applied using a solution of leucine enkephaline (0.5 mg/L, m/z 556.2771 for positive and m/z 554.2620 for negative ion mode) at 0.1 mL/min.&nbsp;</p>	<p>Raw data were collected, imported and analyzed with <strong>Progenesis QI</strong> (Version 2.0.0.0.0, nonlinear Dynamics); the workflow comprised run alignment, peak picking, deconvolution and normalization. Data processing was conducted using Waters <strong>MassLynx</strong> 4.1 and <strong>TargetLynx</strong> software (Milford, MA), limited to the first 21 min of the chromatograph, excluding clean-up and equilibration of the column before the next injection<strong>[1]</strong>. For the biomarkers and relative annotated metabolites, further statistical analysis was performed using the MetaboAnalyst online platform version 4.0 (<a href='https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/</a>) without normalization and data transformation, using Pareto scaling<strong>[2]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Arapitsas P, Ugliano M, Perenzoni D, Angeli A, Pangrazzi P, Mattivi F. Wine metabolomics reveals new sulfonated products in bottled white wines, promoted by small amounts of oxygen. J Chromatogr A. 2016 Jan 15;1429:155-65. doi:10.1016/j.chroma.2015.12.010. Epub 2015 Dec 8. PMID:26709023.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Chong J, Soufan O, Li C, Caraus I, Li S, Bourque G, Wishart DS, Xia J. MetaboAnalyst 4.0: towards more transparent and integrative metabolomics analysis. Nucleic Acids Res. 2018 Jul 2;46(W1):W486-W494. doi:10.1093/nar/gky310. PMID:29762782.</p>	<p>For biomarker detection, we performed an alignment for each cultivar and region, excluding outliers and QC samples were aligned with <strong>Progenesis QI</strong> software (Version 2.0.0.0.0, nonlinear dynamics). This reduced the probability of false negatives due to misalignment caused by the large variation between the chromatographic profiles of cultivars. Moreover, this approach facilitated discrimination between MeJa and CtRl samples.</p><p><br></p><p>For QC during the runs and data analysis, we used principal component analysis (PCA) plots generated by <strong>Progenesis QI</strong>, checking the distribution of the QC injections. Tentative biomarkers were considered as all features with maximum fold change &gt; 2 and ANOVA (p-value) &lt; 0.001.</p><p><br></p><p>Marker annotation was performed according to the 4 levels described previously<strong>[1]</strong>. The features were annotated based on an internal database<strong>[2]</strong> and external databases (<strong>HMDB</strong>, <strong>ChemSpider</strong>, <strong>KEGG</strong>, etc). Metabolite identification was performed manually by comparing retention times and accurate mass spectra (mass difference of less than 5 ppm).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Sumner LW, Amberg A, Barrett D, Beale MH, Beger R, Daykin CA, Fan TW, Fiehn O, Goodacre R, Griffin JL, Hankemeier T, Hardy N, Harnly J, Higashi R, Kopka J, Lane AN, Lindon JC, Marriott P, Nicholls AW, Reily MD, Thaden JJ, Viant MR. Proposed minimum reporting standards for chemical analysis Chemical Analysis Working Group (CAWG) Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI). Metabolomics. 2007 Sep;3(3):211-221. doi:10.1007/s11306-007-0082-2. PMID:24039616.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Shahaf N, Franceschi P, Arapitsas P, Rogachev I, Vrhovsek U, Wehrens R. Constructing a mass measurement error surface to improve automatic annotations in liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry based metabolomics. Rapid Commun Mass Spectrom. 2013 Nov 15;27(21):2425-31. doi:10.1002/rcm.6705. PMID:24097399.</p>

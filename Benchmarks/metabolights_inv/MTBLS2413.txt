Study Identifier	MTBLS2413
Study Title	LC–MS-Based Metabolomics Discriminates Premium from Standard Chilean cv. Cabernet Sauvignon Wines from Different Valleys
Study Description	<p>Cabernet Sauvignon grapes in Chile, mainly grown between the 30 °S and 36 °S, account for more than 30% of Chilean wine production, and yield wines with different characteristics which influence their quality. The aim of this study was to apply an LC-MS based metabolomic protocol to investigate the quality differentiation in a sample set of monovarietal wines from eight valleys covering 679 km of the north-south extension. All samples were produced using a standardized red winemaking process and classified according to a company categorization in two major groups: premium and standard, and each group in two subcategories. The results pointed out that N-containing metabolites (mainly small peptides) are promising biomarkers for quality differentiation. Moreover, the premium wines were characterized by higher amounts of anthocyanins and other glycosylated and acetylated flavonoids, as well as phenolic acids; standard quality wines, on the other hand, presented stilbenoids and sulfonated catabolites of tryptophan and flavanols.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Winemaking Procedure </strong></p><p>The winemaking process to produce experimental monovarietal wines was performed using a standardized red winemaking protocol in the experimental winery of the Center for Research and Innovation (CRI)—Viña Concha y Toro, Pencahue, Chile. The Cabernet Sauvignon grapes were harvested from different Chilean valleys in 2018. The basic oenological characteristics and geographic information of the vineyards are available in the Supplementary Information section (<strong>Table S1 </strong>in the paper associated with this study). For the winemaking process, 600 kg of harvested grapes were received in the experimental winery. The winemaking process was performed in 5 days. The grapes were destemmed, crushed, and transferred to a 1-ton fermentation bin, stabilized with 5 g of SO2 per hL, and adjusted to 23.5 Brix and a pH of 3.45. For alcoholic fermentation, yeast was added (20 g/hL, Maurivin PDM, AB Biotek, Peterborough, UK), together with diammonium phosphate. The fermentation was conducted at 24-26 °C. In this step, a second dose of diammonium phosphate (60 mg/L) was added. On the third day of fermentation, 8 mg/L of O2 was added, and the measure density was 1060 g/L (densimeter Alla France, Chemillé, France). The end of alcoholic fermentation on the 5th day was confirmed by measuring the residual sugar with an enzymatic D-glucose/D-fructose reagent kit in a Y15 automatic analyzer (Biosystem, Barcelona, Spain). The alcoholic fermentation process concluded with a level of residual sugar of &lt;2 g/L. The wine was transferred to stainless steel tanks of 100 L, and pH was adjusted to 3.5 before malolactic fermentation was conducted at a temperature of 20–22 °C (MF, 4 g/HL, Viniflora CH 16, Christian Hansen, Hoersholm, Denmark). Wines were kept in the tanks under 1.5 bar N2 for approximately 1 mth more, and were thereafter filtered, had their SO2 levels adjusted to 35 mg/L, were bottled in dark green glass, and were stored at 12 °C until further analysis. </p><p><br></p><p><strong>Chilean cv. Cabernet Sauvignon Wines Samples</strong> </p><p>50 monovarietal experimental wines (produced in triplicate for a total of 150 samples bottles), vinified as described in the above section, were used in the study. The wines were classified according to the company categorization, based on the historical quality of the specific vineyard and the sensory evaluation performed by winemaking experts<strong> [1] </strong>in 2 main categories: 25 wines were classified as <strong>premium (P) </strong>and 25 wines as <strong>standard (S)</strong>. The basic oenological information on the wines is provided in the in <strong>Table S2</strong> in the paper associated with this study. The premium category was divided in to <strong>“super-premium” quality (P1)</strong>, with 7 wine samples and<strong> “premium” quality (P2)</strong>, with 18 samples, whereas the standard category was divided into <strong>“standard plus” quality (S1)</strong>, with 15 samples, and <strong>“standard” quality (S2)</strong>, with 10 wine samples. The number of samples according to origin were as follows: 2 samples from <strong>Limarí</strong>, 8 samples from <strong>Maipo</strong>, 10 samples from <strong>Colchagua</strong>,&nbsp;6 samples from <strong>Cachapoal</strong>, 15 samples from <strong>Maule</strong>, 7 samples from <strong>Curicó</strong>, 1 sample from <strong>Itata</strong>, and 1 sample from <strong>Aconcagua </strong>(<strong>Figure 1</strong> in the paper associated with this study).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Schober, D.; Legues, M.; Guidez, H.; Caris Maldonado, J.C.; Vargas, S.; Gonzalez Rojas, A. How geographical origin and vineyard management influence cv. Cabernet-Sauvignon in Chile—Machine learning based quality prediction. In Proceedings of the XIIIth International Terroir Congress, Adelaide, Australia, 17–18 November 2020.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p>	<p>A previously described and robust protocol was used for the acquisition of the wine metabolomic space<strong>[1]. </strong>The wine bottles were opened in April of 2019 in a N2-environment to avoid triggering or promoting undesired reactions, and 1 mL of each wine sample was pooled for the quality control (QC) sample. The 150 wine bottles were aliquoted in 10 mL dark vials by completely filling the vial. The sequence, as well the preparation order of the samples, was according to a randomized list. A total of 2 mL of sonicated Milli-Q water was added to 1 mL of the wine samples (including the QC), which was then filtered with 0.22 µm PVDF filters into a dark LC–MS vial.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Arapitsas, P.; Mattivi, F. LC-MS untargeted protocol for the analysis of wine. In Metabolic Profiling, 1st ed.; Humana Press: Totowa, NJ, USA, 2018; pp. 225–235</p>	<p>An Acquity UPLC 1.8 µm, 2.1 mm x 150 mm, HSS T3 column (Waters) was used for the chromatographic separation at 40 °C with a flow of 0.28 mL/min for 5 µL of sample injected. The mobile phases were: 0.1% of formic acid in water (mobile phase A), and in methanol (mobile phase B), respectively. The gradient was as follow: for the first minute 0% of B, then 0-10% of B (1-3 min), 10-40% of B (3-18 min), 40-100% of B (18-21 min), 100% of B is kept for 4.5 min (until 25.5 min of gradient), then 100-0 % of B (25.6-28 min).&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>	<p>A Waters Acquity UPLC (Waters, Manchester, U.K.) was used in tandem with Synapt HDMS QTOF MS (Waters, Manchester, U.K.) controlled by <strong>MassLynxTM 4.1 </strong>software (Milford, MA, U.S.A). MS data were acquired in positive (ESI+) and negative (ESI-) mode, in W mode in a mass range of 50-2000 amu in centroid mode, with a scan duration of 0.4 seconds. The transfer collision energy and trap collision were settled with the values of 6 V and 4 V, respectively.&nbsp;For the source parameters the capillary for ESI+ and ESI- was slightly different, 3 kV for ESI+ and 2.5 kV for ESI-, meanwhile for each mode scan the values are the same for sampling cone (25 V), extraction cone (3 V), source temperature (150 °C), desolvation temperature (500 °C) and flow for nebulizer (50 L/h) and desolvation gas (1000 L/h). A leucine enkephalin solution was used (0.5 mg/L in 50:50 of methanol water with 0.1% of formic acid) at 0.15 mL/min for LockMass calibration, whereas a sodium formiate solution (0.1M of NaOH/10% formic acid/in acetonitrile at a ratio 1:1:8) was used for external calibration, with the objective of controlling the mass accuracy from 40-2000 m/z (&lt; 5ppm) and mass resolution (&gt;14000 FWHM).&nbsp;</p>	<p>The raw files were processed with <strong>Progenesis QI software </strong>(<strong>version 2.4</strong>, nonlinear Dynamics). A principal component analysis (PCA) was performed during the data acquisition, with the purpose of controlling the clustering of QC injections, and for the subsequent data analysis according to wine classifications. The parameters for the data processing are the following:&nbsp;software default mode for the alignment, maximum level for pick picking. The first and the last 6 min (washing) of each run were exclude in all data processing.</p>	<p>The annotation was performed manually with a mass accuracy of 5 ppm, taking into account the isotopic pattern and in accordance with the 4 levels convidence <strong>[1].</strong> For a few compounds, a higher tolerance was accepted, due to an instrument limitation (intensity or m/z values registered for the compound is too high or too low)<strong>[2]</strong>. An external database, as well as an internal wine metabolome database which includes more than 500 metabolites from different class chemical groups, were used for this purpose<strong>[3-7]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Sumner, L.W.; Amberg, A.; Barrett, D.; Beale, M.H.; Beger, R.; Daykin, C.A.; Fan, T.

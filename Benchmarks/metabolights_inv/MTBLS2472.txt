Study Identifier	MTBLS2472
Study Title	Short-term changes in the serum metabolome after laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass
Study Description	<p><strong>INTRODUCTION:</strong> Bariatric surgery is known to be the most effective treatment for weight loss in obese patients and for the rapid remission of obesity-related comorbidities. These short-term improvements result from not only limited digestion or absorption but also dynamic changes in metabolism throughout the whole body. However, short-term metabolism studies associated with bariatric surgery in Asian individuals have not been reported.</p><p><strong>OBJECTIVES:</strong> The aim of this study was to investigate the short-term metabolome changes in the serum promoted by laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (SG) and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) and to determine the underlying mechanisms that affect obesity-related comorbidities.</p><p><strong>METHODS:</strong> Serum samples were collected from Korean patients who underwent RYGB or SG before and 4&nbsp;weeks after the surgery. Metabolomic and lipidomic profiling was performed using UPLC-Orbitrap-MS, and data were analyzed using statistical analysis.</p><p><strong>RESULTS:</strong> Metabolites mainly related to amino acids, lipids (fatty acids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, glycerolipids) and bile acids changed after surgery, and these changes were associated with the lowering of risk factors for obesity-related diseases such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), type 2 diabetes (T2D) and atherosclerosis. Interestingly, the number of significantly altered metabolites related to the lipid metabolism were greater in SG than in RYGB. Furthermore, the metabolites related to amino acid metabolism were significantly changed only after SG, whereas bile acid changed significantly only following RYGB.</p><p><strong>CONCLUSION:</strong> These differences could result from anatomical differences between the two surgeries and could be related to the gut microbiota. This study provides crucial information to expand the knowledge of the common but different molecular mechanisms involved in obesity and obesity-related comorbidities affected by each bariatric procedure.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Patients </strong></p><p>The Korean Obesity Surgical Treatment Study (KOBESS) is a nationwide, prospective, multicenter, observational cohort study of Korean patients who underwent primary <strong>RYGB or SG</strong>. Patients aged 20–65&nbsp;years with a BMI≥35&nbsp;kg/m2 alone or a BMI≥30&nbsp;kg/m2 plus comorbidities (hypertension, glucose intolerance, dyslipidemia and obstructive sleep apnea) were invited to participate in KOBESS. The original KOBESS protocol was published earlier<strong>[1]</strong>. The trial was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) (No. B-1608/357–005) and registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03100292). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. This study was a preplanned substudy of the KOBESS trial using <strong>pre- and postsurgery blood </strong>samples from 40 enrolled patients.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Surgical procedures </strong></p><p>The KOBESS trial was not a randomized study. Therefore, all eligible patients chose their own surgical method after being fully informed about the strengths and weaknesses of each procedure. However, if the patient had Barrett’s esophagus, <strong>SG </strong>was not permitted. Furthermore, infammatory bowel disease and Helicobacter pylori infection were contraindications for <strong>RYGB</strong>. For <strong>SG</strong>, a sleeve was fashioned starting 4–6&nbsp;cm proximal to the pylorus by using serial applications of linear staples over a 36–40 Fr orogastric bougie. The last fring was 1–2&nbsp;cm away from the angle of His. For <strong>RYGB</strong>, a gastric pouch with reduced curvature was created by using linear staplers. The length of the Roux limb was 100&nbsp;cm and that of the biliopancreatic limb was 50–100&nbsp;cm in patients, according to glucose intolerance. Gastrojejunostomy was created by using a linear stapler; the anastomosis length was 20–30&nbsp;mm. Jejunojejunostomy was created by using a 45- or 60-mm linear stapler. All mesenteric defects were closed with a running suture made of nonabsorbable materials. </p><p><br></p><p><strong>Sample collection </strong></p><p>Blood samples from all enrolled patients were collected according to the study timeline and the investigation plan<strong>[1]</strong>. <strong>Sera </strong>from the centrifuged blood samples were stored at -80&nbsp;°C. Samples were first stored at each institution and then transferred to the Ulsan University laboratory for integrated management. After the planned sample collection was complete, blood samples were distributed to researchers for the implementation of each sub-study.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Park YS, Park DJ, Lee JH, Lee HJ, Ha TK, Kim YJ, Ryu SW, Han SM, Yoo MW, Park S, Han SU, Heo Y. Korean OBEsity Surgical Treatment Study (KOBESS): protocol of a prospective multicentre cohort study on obese patients undergoing laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass. BMJ Open. 2017 Oct 16;7(10):e018044. doi: 10.1136/bmjopen-2017-018044. PMID: 29042391</p><p><br></p>	<p>For metabolomics, 150 µL of methanol was added to 50 µL of plasma, and the mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. Then, 100 µL of supernatant was diluted with 50 µL of distilled water containing 4 µg/mL reserpine as an internal standard (IS). For lipidomics, 50 µL of 0.15 M NaCl and 25 µL of methanol containing 4 µg/mL IS were added to 50 µL of plasma. Phosphatidylcholine (PC) (16:0/18:1)-d31 and arachidonic acid-d8 were used as IS for the positive and negative ionization modes, respectively. Then, 250 µL of ice-cold chloroform/methanol (1:2, v/v) was added, followed by 1 min of vortexing and 1 h of incubation at room temperature for lipid extraction. The mixtures were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatants were dried under nitrogen gas. Then, the residues were reconstituted with 60 µL of ice-cold chloroform/methanol (1:1, v/v).&nbsp;</p>	<p>An LTQ Orbitrap Velos ProTM mass spectrometer coupled to an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system was used for analysis. </p><p><br></p><p>For <strong>metabolomics</strong>, 0.1% formic acid in distilled water and methanol was used for mobile phases A and B, respectively, and chromatographic separations were performed with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The gradient conditions were as follows: 100% mobile phase A was maintained from 0 to 1 min and decreased linearly to 80% to 4 min; then, mobile phase A was decreased from 80 to 30% for 6 min, followed by a linear decrease to 0% to 14 min; the column was re-equilibrated with 100% to 16 min. </p><p><br></p><p>For <strong>lipidomics</strong>, an ACE Excel 2 Super C18 column (2.1×100 mm, 1.7 µm, Advanced Chromatography Technologies Ltd., Aberdeen, Scotland, UK) with 10 mM ammonium acetate in either 40% acetonitrile (v/v, mobile phase A) and isopropanol/acetonitrile (90:10, v/v, mobile phase B) was used for chromatographic separation. The elution gradient was as follows: 60% mobile phase A was maintained from 0 to 1 min, decreased linearly to 35% from 1 to 3 min, decreased again to 15% for 2 min, and then, a linear decrease from 15 to 0% occurred from 5 to 9 min. The condition was maintained for 3 min, and the column was re-equilibrated with 60% mobile phase A to 12.5 min. The flow rates for elution were 0.4 mL/min, and the autosampler and column oven temperatures were kept at 4 °C and 50 °C.&nbsp;</p>	<p>The mass spectrometry parameters detected the compounds with a full scan range (m/z 50–2000) using the centroid mode at a resolution of 60,000. The detailed parameters were as follows: heater temperature, 40 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 45 arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 10 arb; spray voltage, 4 kV; capillary temperature, 320 °C; and S-lens RF level, 61%.</p>	<p><strong>Xcalibur 2.2 </strong>and <strong>Compound Discoverer 2.1 </strong>(Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) were used for data acquisition and peak alignments. The intensities of compounds were normalized by the areas of the ISs before statistical analysis.<strong> SIMCA 14.1</strong> (Umetrics, Inc., Ume, Sweden) and MetaboAnalyst (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) were used to perform the multivariate analysis. To identify the metabolome differences among samples taken before surgery (presurgery group), 4 weeks after SG (SG group) and 4 weeks after RYGB (RYGB group), orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and partial least square discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) with Pareto scaling were used. For all models, the Q2 and R2Y values were determined to prevent overfitting, and 1000 permutation tests were used to assess the predictability and nonrandomness of the model. Moreover, the variable importance in the projection values suggested greater discriminatory power of the metabolite and was used as a coefficient for peak selection. Significance was assessed by a paired Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare pre- and postsurgery groups and an unpaired Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare the degree of change between the 2 surgery groups using <strong>R </strong>Studio (<a href='https://www.r-project.org/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.r-project.org/</a>). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.&nbsp;</p>	<p>Metabolites that showed significant differences were identified by using databases, such as <strong>METLIN </strong>(<a href='http://metlin/scripps/edu/)' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>http://metlin/scripps/edu/)</a>, The <strong>Human Metabolome Database </strong>(<a href='http://www.hmdb.ac' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>http://www.hmdb.ac</a>), and <strong>LipidBlast</strong>, based on the m/z of the adducts ([M+H]+, [M+Na]+, etc.) and MS2 fragmentation matching with a 10 ppm accuracy tolerance window. After searching and comparing the mass fragment patterns of the references, the identified metabolites were crosschecked with commercially available authentic standards. Pathway analysis was performed on the identified metabolites using<strong> Ingenuity Pathway Analysis</strong> (Redwood City, CA).</p>

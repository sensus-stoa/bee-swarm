STUDY
Study Identifier	MTBLS2579
Study Title	Metabolomic signatures discriminate horses with clinical signs of atypical myopathy from healthy co-grazing horses
Study Description	Atypical myopathy (AM) is a severe rhabdomyolysis syndrome that occurs in grazing horses. Despite the presence of toxins in their blood, all horses from the same pasture are not prone to display clinical signs of AM. The objective of this study was to compare the blood metabolomic profiles of horses with AM clinical signs with those of healthy co-grazing (Co-G) horses. To do so, plasma samples from 5 AM horses and 11 Co-G horses were investigated using untargeted metabolomics. Metabolomic data were evaluated using unsupervised, supervised, and pathway analyses. Unsupervised principal component analysis performed with all detected features separated AM and healthy Co-G horses. Supervised analyses had identified 1276 features showing differential expression between both groups. Among them, 46 metabolites, belonging predominantly to the fatty acid, fatty ester, and amino acid chemical classes, were identified by standard comparison. Fatty acids, unsaturated fatty acids, organic dicarboxylic acids, and fatty esters were detected with higher intensities in AM horses in link with the toxins' pathological mechanism. The main relevant pathways were lipid metabolism; valine, leucine, and isoleucine metabolism; and glycine metabolism. This study revealed characteristic metabolite changes in the plasma of clinically affected horses, which might ultimately help scientists and field veterinarians to detect and manage AM. The raw data of metabolomics are available in the MetaboLights database with the access number MTBLS2579.

Study Protocol Description	<p>The population was selected according to several stringent inclusion criteria: (i) the <strong>AM </strong>horses had to have <strong>Co-G</strong> horses, (ii) samples of<strong> Co-G </strong>horses had to be collected the same day or at the latest, the day after the <strong>AM </strong>horses (iii), HGA had to be detected in both <strong>AM </strong>and<strong> Co-G </strong>horses to confirm that horses included in this study had ingested toxins from sycamore maple and were exposed to same environmental factors during 1 single season (i.e. Fall 2018). The <strong>AM </strong>diagnosis was made by veterinarians at the veterinary clinic of University of Liège based on anamnesis (i.e. season, access to pasture), clinical information (i.e. clinical signs, pigmenturia), blood analysis (i.e. increase of CK) and the absence of other diagnostic during the hospitalization. Samples from <strong>AM </strong>horses were collected on the field by practitioners. The clinical status of healthy<strong> Co-G </strong>horses was confirmed owing to a clinical examination (i.e. normal heart rate, respiratory rate, rectal temperature, etc.) performed by an investigator of this study (i.e. Dr Renaud) during the sampling.</p><p><br></p><p>Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein and were put directly on ice. Serum and plasma were collected in order to perform respectively the targeted and untargeted metabolomics analyses.</p>	<p>Heparin lithium plasma was thawed at room temperature. 100 µL of each sample were placed into 3 microtubes in order to perform the analysis in biological triplicate. 100 μL of plasma were added to 250 μL of internal standard. After vortexing during 30 s, samples were kept on ice for 10 min and were centrifuged for 25 min at 20,000 x g at 4 °C. 250 μL of supernatant were dried using the low boiling point mode of the EZ2 GENEVAC (Biopharma Technologies France, Diémoz, France) for 2 h. Samples were reconstituted using 250 μL of an aqueous solution containing 0.1% of formic acid for the positive mode analysis and 250 μL of an aqueous solution containing 1 mM of acetic acid for the negative mode analysis. Before the analysis, samples were vortexed for 30 s and were filtered using filters (PVDF 0.45 μM, 13 mm, Merck Millipore). In order to constitute the quality control (QC) samples, 50 µL of each extracted samples were placed in a vial. After filtration, 5 μL of the filtrate was injected.</p>	<p>Metabolites were separated using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) (1290 Infinity, Agilent Technologies, USA). The separation was made through a 2.1 x 50 mm Waters Acquity C18 BEH 1.7 μm particle precolumn and a 2.1 mm x 150 mm Waters Acquity UPLC C18 BEH 1.7 μm particle column (Waters Corp, USA). Samples were loaded at 0.4 mL/min in eluent A (ultrapure water) and subjected to elution with eluent B (ACN). 0.1% of formic acid or 1 mM of acetic acid was added to eluents in respectively positive and negative mode. The gradient was (0 min, 100% A; 0.6 min 98% A; 2 min, 80% A; 4 min, 40% A ; 4.3 min 30% A ; 6.5 min 10% A ; 7.5 min 5% A ; 9 min ; 2% A ; 11 min 98% A). The remaining 5 min were used for re-equilibration steps.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using an Agilent Technologies 6540 UHD Accurate-Mass Q-TOF with electrospray ionization (ESI). The temperature of the MS interface capillary was maintained at 350 °C and the drying gas flow at 11 L/min. The nebulizer was set at 40 psi and the nitrogen gas flow at 12 L/min. Both positive and negative modes were performed in independent injections. The spray voltage was 4 kW and 3.5 kW for respectively the positive and the negative mode. The instrument scan range was 100-1000 <em>m/z</em>.</p><p><br></p><p>All samples were analyzed in both modes leading to 2 batches. Some variations in the number of blanks and quality control (QC) samples were performed between positive and negative batches in order to adjust the instruments according to previous analyses. Thus, the numbers are always presented for respectively the positive and negative batches. Each analytical batch was made up in chronological order of 5 and 10 ACN samples enabling to wash leftover metabolites on the column; 5 and 3 ACN containing ^13 C diclofenac; 3 extraction solvent blanks; 5 and 10 QC samples (/i.e. mix of all samples after the sample preparation process) enabling the conditioning and the equilibration of the system in order to achieve fully reproducible conditions; the triplicate samples to be analyzed were injected by series of pasture in order to reduce the impact of the leftover metabolites and were separated every 5 samples by QC; 3 extraction solvent blanks; 10 and 8 ACN (including 5 and 3 with ^13 C diclofenac) samples enabling the rinse of the column.</p>	<p>The <strong>RAW </strong>data acquired in centroid mode and were converted in the <strong>mzXML </strong>format using <strong>MsConvert </strong>(<strong>version: 3.0.18208-7b41a1f82</strong>, ProteoWizard). </p>	<p><strong>Data processing:</strong></p><p>Data were imported on the web platform <strong>Galaxy</strong>, where the <strong>workflow4metabolomics </strong>(<strong>version 3.2.</strong>) was used for the data preprocessing<strong>[1]</strong>. Peak detection, grouping and retention time correction using <strong>XCMS </strong>software<strong>[2] </strong>resulted in 2 peak tables (i.e. 1 for each ionization mode). After that, metabolites eluted before 40 s and having a fold change (i.e. ratio between samples on blanks) lower than 1.5 were filtered out. These steps enabled to reduce noise and enhance the reproducibility of the analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Signal drift and batch effect correction:</strong></p><p>Signal drift and batch effect within each peak table were corrected by fitting a locally quadratic (loess) regression model to the sample values. The α parameter controlling the smoothing<strong>[3]</strong> was set to 1 to avoid over-fitting<strong>[4]-[6].</strong></p><p><br></p><p><strong>Missing values imputation:</strong></p><p>Missing values accounted for 5.32% and 6.64% of values for respectively positive and negative ionization modes in data matrix. In order to improve statistical power and avoid bias, missing values imputation was performed using k-nearest neighbors 10 (knn-10) algorithm as previously recommended<strong>[7]</strong>. Lastly, the matrix was log10 transformed in order to improve the normality of variables.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Features selection:</strong></p><p>In order to reduce analytical bias, the median intensity of biological triplicate for each feature was used for the selection. Features were then selected using both univariate (i.e. Wilcoxon p-FDR &lt; 0.05) and multivariate approaches (i.e. OPLS-DA variable importance in prediction [VIP_pred] score &gt; 1)<strong>[6][8]</strong>. For each variable, the pFDR values (i.e. p-values after FDR adjustment) were computed from the non-parametric hypothesis of the correlation with the horse status (AM horses vs. healthy Co-G horses). The pFDR value was the p-value corrected for multiple testing using the False Discovery Rate (i.e. the expected proportion of type I error). In the OPLS-DA model, the VIP_pred score estimates the importance of each variable in the projection<strong>[8]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p>Identification was performed using our <strong>in-house library</strong>, containing over 700 metabolites. Our <strong>in-house library</strong> was composed with the <strong>MSMLS library</strong> (IROA Technologies), MCPA-carnitine, AC. Briefly, identification was performed using a delta<em> m/z</em> of 0.005 Da and a delta time of 0.1 min. Once the feature was presumably identified using<em> m/z</em> and retention time (RT), tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was further performed on the features and the standard molecules. MS/MS data were investigated using the Global Natural Product Social Molecular Networking (<strong>GNPS</strong>)<strong>[9]</strong>. All identifications had an annotation level of one according to the Metabolomics Standard Initiative (<strong>MSI</strong>) classification<strong>[10]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Giacomoni F, Le Corguillé G, Monsoor M, Landi M, Pericard P, Pétéra M, Duperier C, Tremblay-Franco M, Martin JF, Jacob D, Goulitquer S, Thévenot EA, Caron C.

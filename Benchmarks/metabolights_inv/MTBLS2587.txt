Study Identifier	MTBLS2587
Study Title	Evaluation of ambient mass spectrometry tools for assessing inherent postharvest pepper quality
Study Description	<p>Horticulturists are interested in evaluating how cultivar, environment, or production system inputs can affect postharvest quality. Ambient mass spectrometry approaches enable analysis of minimally processed samples under ambient conditions and offer an attractive high-throughput alternative for assessing quality characteristics in plant products. Here, we evaluate direct analysis in real time (DART-MS) mass spectrometry and rapid evaporative ionization-mass spectrometry (REIMS) to assess quality characteristics in various pepper (<em>Capsicum annuum L.</em>) cultivars. DART-MS exhibited the ability to discriminate between pod colors and pungency based on chemical fingerprints, while REIMS could distinguish pepper market class (e.g., bell, lunchbox, and popper). Furthermore, DART-MS analysis resulted in the putative detection of important bioactive compounds in human diet such as vitamin C, p-coumaric acid, and capsaicin. The results of this study demonstrate the potential for these approaches as accessible and reliable tools for high throughput screening of pepper quality.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>DART-MS:</strong></p><p>Pepper (<em>C. annuum</em>) seeds from 40 different cultivars (see <strong>Table S1</strong> in the paper associated with this study) were sown on 24 March 2019 into plastic plug trays (50 cell, Harris Seeds, Rochester, NY) containing a mixture of 40 L of soilless media (Sunshine mix 4, Sun Gro Horticulture, Agawam, MA), 10 L of worm castings, 250 ml of blood meal, and 250 ml of bone meal. The average daily temperature in the greenhouse was 25 °C and the relative humidity was 50%. The transplants were hardened by placing them outside under an insect-netting covered high tunnel with the east and west end walls removed for 7 d prior to transplanting. Pepper plants were grown between 5 June and 3 Oct. 2019 in a field at the Agricultural Research, Development and Education Center (ARDEC) South in Fort Collins, CO (lat. 40°36’N., long. 104°59’W.) elevation 1,524 m. The pepper cultivars were planted 0.5 m apart in black plastic mulched beds, which were spaced 1.8 m apart. Macronutrient needs (57 kg/acre nitrogen) were met with monthly applications of Drammatic 'One' (4-4-0.5) fertilizer (Dramm Corporation, Manitowoc, WI) through the drip irrigation system. Black plastic drip tape emitting water at a rate of 500 L/h/100 m with emitters spaced 20 cm apart was used to irrigate the crop. Using an irrigation controller, we provided 15-30 min of irrigation once or twice daily. The crop was scouted for pests in the field on a weekly basis. Fully mature pepper pods were harvested on 9 and 23 Sept. as well as on 3 Oct. 2019. The pods were stored in 7 °C walk-in cooler until quality characteristics could be evaluated via DART-MS. Fresh pods were taken to the lab where they were stored in a 7 °C refrigerator until they could be rinsed with deionized water and dried with disposable paper towels. Pepper pungency was determined by the cultivar description in grower seed catalogs or by speaking with the pepper breeder. </p><p><br></p><p><strong>REIMS:</strong></p><p>Approximately 24 pepper samples were purchased for each of the 2 market classes, bell and popper (see <strong>Table S3</strong> in the paper associated with this study) from Whole Foods Market (Fort Collins, CO) on 12 Feb. 2018. On the same day, 5 bags of ~24 tri-color (red, yellow, orange) sweet mini lunchbox pepper phenotypes were purchased from King Soopers (Kroger supermarket, Fort Collins, CO). </p><p><br></p>	<p>No extraction was performed in this study.</p>	<p><strong>DART-MS:</strong></p><p>Mass spectra were acquired on a Waters ACQUITY QDa single quadrupole mass spectrometer in negative ionization mode over the mass range of 50 to 500 m/z.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>REIMS:</strong></p><p>Mass spectra were collected on a Waters Synapt G2 Si Q-ToF mass spectrometer from 50 to 1200 m/z in positive ionization mode.</p>	<p><strong>DART-MS:</strong></p><p>Preprocessing was conducted using a beta version of WRC Abstract Model Builder (Waters Corporation, Manchester, UK). Scans corresponding to the pepper sample spectra were selected and the signal was summed to generate 1 spectrum per sample. Data was normalized to the total ion current for each sample. Peak binning was conducted at an interval of 0.5 m/z (see <strong>Table S2</strong> of the paper associated with this study).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>REIMS:</strong></p><p>Pre-processing was performed using a beta version of Waters Abstract Model Builder. At least 3 peaks corresponding to 3 'burns' were selected and the signal was summed to generate 1 spectrum per sample. Data was normalized to the total ion current for each sample. Peak binning was conducted at an interval of 0.5 m/z over the m/z range from 50-550 m/z (see <strong>Table S4</strong> of the paper associated with this study).</p>	<p>Putative annotations were assigned by cross-referencing the m/z mass bins (0.5 m/z bin size) observed after pre-processing (described above) against all compounds that have been previously detected in red bell peppers as defined in the food database (<strong>FoodB, version 1</strong>) and comparison to standards when possible<strong>[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Statistical analysis DART-MS:</strong></p><p>The effects of pod color, market class, cultivar, and pungency on the chemical profile were evaluated using analysis of variance <strong>(ANOVA)</strong> with the aov function in the <strong>R statistical environment (version 3.6.2)[2][3]</strong>. Using the p.adjust function in R, we used a <strong>Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate</strong> adjustment to identify mass bins of statistical significance at the α = 0.05 level. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis <strong>(O2PLS-DA)</strong> was performed using <strong>SIMCA (version 15)</strong> on unit variance scaled and <strong>LOG10</strong> transformed data. The volcano plots were constructed using <strong>GraphPad (version 8.1.0)</strong>. The x-axis indicates the differential abundance between red and green pepper pods; it was calculated using <strong>log2</strong> red abundance divided by <strong>log2</strong> green abundance. The y-axis indicates statistical significance using -log10 FDR adjusted p values (α = 0.05) for 333 metabolites. Sphere colors, red and green, indicate metabolite class. The vertical dashed lines are the threshold for red/green [log2(red/green) &lt; -1.0 or &gt; 1.0]. The volcano plot for the sweet and pungent pepper metabolite comparison was constructed using a similar approach where sweet was divided by pungent for 368 metabolites. The heat map, which is based on z-scores for each pepper pod color and putatively annotated mass bins, was generated using the pheatmap function in R. Z-scores were calculated by comparing the average relative abundance value for a mass bin to the population mean and population standard deviation for that mass bin.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Statistical analysis REIMS:</strong></p><p>The effects of market class on the chemical profile were evaluated using analysis of variance <strong>(ANOVA)</strong> with the aov function in the <strong>R statistical environment[2]</strong>. Using the <strong>p.adjust function</strong> in R, we used a <strong>Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate</strong> adjustment to identify mass bins of statistical significance at the α = 0.05 level. <strong>O2PLS-DA</strong> was performed using <strong>SIMCA (version 15)</strong> on unit variance scaled and <strong>log10</strong> transformed data. The heat map illustrating differences in chemical profiles for each market class was generated using z-scores and the pheatmap function in R. Mass bins were putatively annotated using a similar method as to what was described above for DART-MS.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> The FoodB (version 1): Available online: <a href='https://foodb.ca/foods/FOOD00880' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://foodb.ca/foods/FOOD00880</a> (accessed on 6 April 2020).</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> The R Project for Statistical Computing (version 3.6.2). Available online: <a href='https://www.r-project.org/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.r-project.org/</a> (accessed on 6 April 2020).</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Human Metabolome Database: Available online: <a href='https://hmdb.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://hmdb.ca/</a> (accessed 7 April 2020).</p>	<p><strong>DART-MS:</strong></p><p>The experiment was run as a randomized complete block design with 8 replicates spanning 8 d. 1 representative pepper pod served as the replicate. After every 8th sample, a quality control (QC) sample was analyzed. The QCs (6/d) consisted of a sub-sample from the same pod belonging to <strong>'Ace'</strong>. A disposable utility razor blade was used to cut a 7 cm long by 2 cm wide slice of pepper longitudinally down the center of the pod just prior to analysis. From this piece, 2 mm thick cross-sectional cuts were made to expose the <strong>exocarp</strong>, <strong>mesocarp</strong>, and <strong>endocarp</strong> tissue. The sliced pepper samples were laid sideways on the tablet carrier adapter for the sample introduction rail system (IonSense, Inc., Saugus, MA).</p><p><br></p><p>The DART-MS analysis was conducted using the DART-Standardized Voltage and Pressure (DART-SVP) model ion source (IonSense, Inc., Saugus, MA). It was coupled to a single quadrupole mass spectrometer (ACQUITY QDa; Waters Corporation, Manchester, UK) via a VAPUR interface (IonSense, Inc., Saugus, MA). The DART-SVP was equipped with a motorized linear rail where the tablet carrier was mounted. The helium flow rate for the ion source was set to ~3 L/min heated to 350 °C. The cone voltage was set to 20 V. The speed of the motorized linear rail system holding the tablet carrier adapter was set to 1.0 mm/s. The standby temperature was held at 245 °C. The samples were arranged on the 10 Tablet module (metal rail) such that 3 pepper sub-samples from the same pod were placed in every other tablet location. This allowed the signal from each sample to return to the baseline before the next acquisition could be started.</p><p><br></p><p>Authentic analytical standards for <strong>p-coumaric acid,</strong> <strong>capsaicin</strong>, and <strong>L-ascorbic acid</strong> were analyzed using the same instrument conditions with the exception of the sample introduction (≥98% purity) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). A 10 mg/ml solution was made for <strong>L-ascorbic acid</strong> using HPLC grade water. Solutions (10 mg/ml) were made for <strong>p-coumaric acid</strong>, <strong>trans-p-coumaric acid</strong>, and <strong>capsaicin</strong> using 100% ethanol. 12 replicates of each sample were run through the DART using the DIP-IT method. This involved dipping individual 10 µl glass capillary rods into each solution and placing the rods into the DIP IT module holder, which allowed the analytical standards to pass directly in front of the ionizing source.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>REIMS:</strong></p><p>Analysis was performed as a randomized complete block design with 16 replicates. The individual pepper phenotypes were cut into thirds. The <strong>exocarp</strong> of the pod was placed flat on the conduction pad so that the <strong>mesocarp</strong> was exposed. The 'iKnife' was held perpendicular to the <strong>mesocarp</strong> tissue so the vacuum component could draw in the smoke generated by the ionizing source.</p><p><br></p><p>The chemical fingerprint was detected using the protocol described<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, the samples were analyzed using a Synapt G2 Si Q-ToF, fitted with a REIMS ionization source attached to a monopolar electrosurgical hand piece called an 'iKnife' (Waters Corporation, Manchester, UK). It was powered with an Erbotom ICC 300 electrosurgical generator (Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH, Turbingen, Germany) using the 'liquid coagulation' mode at a power of 40 W. A solution of 2 ng/ml leucine-enkephalin at a continual flow rate of 200 µl/min was directed to the REIMS source during sampling. The heater bias was set to 80 V and the cone voltage was set to 20 V. At least 3 'burns' were collected from each sample within a 3 cm X 3 cm square from the centre of the pod. Each burn lasted approximately 3 s.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Gredell DA, Schroeder AR, Belk KE, Broeckling CD, Heuberger AL, Kim SY, King DA, Shackelford SD, Sharp JL, Wheeler TL, Woerner DR, Prenni JE. Comparison of Machine Learning Algorithms for Predictive Modeling of Beef Attributes Using Rapid Evaporative Ionization Mass Spectrometry (REIMS) Data. Sci Rep. 2019 Apr 5;9(1):5721. doi:10.1038/s41598-019-40927-6. PMID:30952873.</p>

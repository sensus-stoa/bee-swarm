Study Identifier	MTBLS2597
Study Title	Towards engineering ectomycorrhization into switchgrass bioenergy crops via a lectin receptor-like kinase
Study Description	Soil-borne microbes can establish compatible relationships with host plants, providing a large variety of nutritive and protective compounds in exchange for photosynthesized sugars. However, the molecular mechanisms mediating the establishment of these beneficial relationships remain unclear. Our previous genetic mapping and whole-genome resequencing studies identified a gene deletion event of a Populus trichocarpa lectin receptor-like kinase gene PtLecRLK1 in Populus deltoides that was associated with poor root colonization by the ectomycorrhizal fungus Laccaria bicolor. By introducing PtLecRLK1 into a perennial grass known to be a non-host of L. bicolor, switchgrass (Panicum virgatum L.), we found that L. bicolor colonizes ZmUbipro-PtLecRLK1 transgenic switchgrass roots, which illustrates that the introduction of PtLecRLK1 has the potential to convert a non-host to a host of L. bicolor. Furthermore, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses on inoculated transgenic switchgrass roots revealed genes/proteins overrepresented in the compatible interaction and underrepresented in the pathogenic defense pathway, consistent with the view that pathogenic defense response is downregulated during compatible interaction. Metabolomic profiling revealed that root colonization in the transgenic switchgrass was associated with an increase in N-containing metabolites and a decrease in organic acids, sugars, and aromatic hydroxycinnamate conjugates, which are often seen in the early steps of establishing compatible interactions. These studies illustrate that PtLecRLK1 is able to render a plant susceptible to colonization by the ectomycorrhizal fungus L. bicolor and shed light on engineering mycorrhizal symbiosis into a non-host to enhance plant productivity and fitness on marginal lands.

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Plant transformation and fungal material growth conditions</strong></p><p><strong>ZmUbipro-PtLecRLK1</strong> transgenic lines of <strong>switchgrass </strong>were generated by the Noble Research Institute Transformation Core Facility using the published protocol<strong>[1]</strong>. <strong>Switchgrass </strong>(<em>Panicum virgatum L</em>., genotype ‘<strong>NFCX01</strong>’) was used to generate the ZmUbipro-PtLecRLK1 transgenic lines with the overexpression vector pANIC10A by cloning PtLecRLK1 under the control of the maize UBIQUITIN 1 (ZmUbi1) promoter. The 4 X14’’ tall one-tree pots from Stuewe &amp; Sons, Inc. were used for growing <strong>switchgrass </strong>in the greenhouse with 16 h photoperiod at 24 °C. The <strong>ectomycorrhizal fungus</strong> <em>Laccaria bicolor</em> isolate <strong>S238N </strong>was maintained at 25 °C on potato dextrose agar (PDA 24 g L−1) plates.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p><strong>Switchgrass mycorrhization experiments with L. bicolor in soil</strong></p><p>Free-living mycelia of <em>L. bicolor</em> S238N were grown for 7 d on potato dextrose liquid medium. In parallel, plant material was prepared for inoculation. <strong>Switchgrass tillers</strong> were pre-rooted in water for ~2 w. <strong>Rooted tillers</strong> were then transferred to soil dampened with <em>L. bicolor</em> liquid inoculum as inoculate condition, while the <strong>tillers </strong>transferred to soil dampened with liquid medium only as mock condition. For multi-omic studies, there were 3 replicates for each of the <strong>transgenic </strong>lines and <strong>WT </strong>plants. The plants were grown in mock condition and/or in inoculate condition for 2 mths in the greenhouse under a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod at 24 °C. All replicates of inoculated and mock <strong>root </strong>samples from each of the <strong>transgenic </strong>lines and <strong>WT </strong>were collected. <strong>Plants roots </strong>were rinsed, 10 to 20 root tips (~1 cm) from middle part of <strong>root </strong>were randomly cut and fixed with 4% formaldehyde for microscopic assay. The rest of whole <strong>roots </strong>(including root tips) were collected and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, followed by the homogenization by the TissueLyzer (Invitrogen). The ground <strong>root </strong>samples were used for metabolomic analyses.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Xi Y, Fu C, Ge Y, Nandakumar R, Hisano H, Bouton J, Wang Z-Y (2009) Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of switchgrass and inheritance of the transgenes. Bioenergy Research 2 (4):275-283</p>	<p>Metabolites were extracted from ~200 mg of frozen powdered tissue twice overnight with 2.5 ml 80% ethanol. Sorbitol (75 µL of 1 mg/mL aqueous solution) was added to the first extract as an internal standard. The extracts were combined, and a 1 mL aliquot was dried under nitrogen for analysis. Metabolites were silylated to produce trimethylsilyl derivatives by addition of 500 µL silylation-grade acetonitrile to the dried extract followed by addition of 500 µL of N-methyl-N-trimethylsilyltrifluoroacetamide (MSTFA) with 1% trimethylchlorosilane (TMCS). Samples were heated for 1 hour at 70 °C and injected 2 d later.</p>	<p>A 1 µL aliquot was injected from an Agilent 7683B autosampler into an Agilent Technologies 7890A gas chromatograph (GC) fitted with a Restek Rtx-5MS with Integra-Guard (5% diphenyl/95% dimethyl polysiloxane) 30 m x 250 µm x 0.25 µm film thickness capillary column. The initial oven temperature was set at 50 °C and held for 2 min followed by a temperature ramp of 20 °C/min to 325 °C and held for 11.5 min.</p>	<p>Samples were injected into an Agilent Technologies 7890A gas chromatograph (GC) coupled to a 5975C inert XL mass spectrometer (MS). The MS was operated in electron impact (70 eV) ionization mode with a scan range of 50-650 Da. The source temperature was set to 230 °C, the quad temperature was 150 °C and the transfer line was 300 °C.</p>	<p>No data transformation information was supplied for this study. </p>	<p>Metabolite peaks were extracted using a key selected ion, characteristic m/z fragment rather than the total ion chromatogram to minimize interference of co-eluting metabolites. The extracted peaks of known metabolites were scaled back up to the total ion current using predetermined scaling factors. Peaks were quantified by area integration and normalized to the amount of internal standard recovery, amount of sample extracted, derivatized and injected. The differential abundance metabolites and their enrichment analyses were performed in<strong> MetaboAnalyst 4.0[1]</strong>. Significantly enriched pathways were determined via a Holm-Bonferroni method, adjusted p-values and FDR are displayed. The differential abundance metabolites were also visualized with <strong>MapMan 3.5.1R2[2]</strong>.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>A large user-created database of mass spectral electron impact ionization (EI) patterns of TMS-derivatized compounds and the <strong>Wiley Registry 10th edition</strong> with <strong>NIST 14 </strong>mass spectral database were used to identify metabolites. Unidentified metabolites were denoted by their retention time as well mass-to-charge ratios.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Chong J, Soufan O, Li C, Caraus I, Li S, Bourque G, Wishart DS, Xia J. MetaboAnalyst 4.0: towards more transparent and integrative metabolomics analysis. Nucleic Acids Res. 2018 Jul 2;46(W1):W486-W494. doi:10.1093/nar/gky310. PMID:29762782</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Schwacke R, Ponce-Soto GY, Krause K, Bolger AM, Arsova B, Hallab A, Gruden K, Stitt M, Bolger ME, Usadel B. MapMan4: A Refined Protein Classification and Annotation Framework Applicable to Multi-Omics Data Analysis. Mol Plant. 2019 Jun 3;12(6):879-892. doi:10.1016/j.molp.2019.01.003. Epub 2019 Jan 9.

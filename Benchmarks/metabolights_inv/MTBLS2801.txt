Study Identifier	MTBLS2801
Study Title	Industrial by-products as a novel circular source of biocompatible extracellular vesicles
Study Description	<p>Extracellular vesicles (EVs) constitute an intricate system of molecular exchange that has recently gained tremendous interest. However, sustainable sources of safe biological EVs remain scarce and elusive. In this study we explore and define the use of food industry byproducts (BP) as a circular source of safe biocompatible EVs. Averaged diameter and molecular compositions indicate large yield of exosomes and high abundancy of membrane lipids with signaling capacity in these vesicles. Complex proteomes mimicking those circulating in human blood plasma were also identified. Furthermore, BP-EVs did not show relevant cytotoxicity and displayed excellent oral and intravenous bioavailability together with specific organ targeting capacity. Collectively, we believe that the novel findings reported here will open substantial venues for the use of BP as an optimal source of biocompatible nanovesicles in manifold applications of the bioeconomy and biomedical fields.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Food industry by-products:</strong></p><p>A total of 4 different fermented food industry by-products (FFIBP) were used in this study as potential source of circular EVs: i) brewer spend yeast derived from the <strong>beer</strong> fermentation production (named as <strong>B</strong> in the sample codification), provided by Mahou San Miguel brewery (Alovera, Spain); ii) whey from unbranded plain yogurt (<strong>milk</strong>) purchased in a local supermarket (named as <strong>M</strong> in the sample codification); iii) symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast SCOBY derived from the production of <strong>tea</strong> fermented drink (kombucha) (named as <strong>T</strong> in the sample codification) online purchased from Kirandia - Inggenio Corporate Group, S.L. (Madrid, Spain); and, iv) fermented wine pomace from red grapes derived from the <strong>wine</strong> fermentation production, provided by Castell del Remei winery (Penelles, Spain) (named as <strong>W</strong> in the sample codification).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Preparation of food industry by-products prior EVs isolation:</strong></p><p>Prior to extracellular vesicles (EVs) isolation, FFIBP were pre-processed as follows: brewer spend yeast (~15 mL) was centrifuged at 10,000 x g, 30 min at 5 ˚C to obtain the analyzed here byproduct 1&nbsp;(BP1); Whey from plain yogurt (BP2) was obtained by centrifugation of commercial yogurt (~25 mL) at 10,000 x g, 30 min at 5 ˚C of plain; BP3 and BP4 were obtained by respective manual homogenization of 3 g each of SCOBY and wine pomace in 15 mL of PBS by using a glass Dounce homogenizer. All pre-processed BPs were stored -80 ˚C until further use.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Molecular Weight Cut-Off:</strong></p><p>Isolation of fermented food industry by-products-derived EVs (BP-EVs) by molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) was performed by using Vivaspin PES 300 kDa MWCO spin-filters. After initial centrifugation of 4 mL of BP1-BP4 samples following the manufacturer’s instructions, the sample remaining in the upper side of the MWCO membrane was washed twice with 10 mL of PBS following manufacturer’s instructions. The BP-EVs concentrated sample remaining in the upper side of the filter was collected and stored at -80 ˚C for further downstream analysis.</p>	<p>For BP-EVs lipid extraction, 5 µL (~2.5 x 10^5 particles) of concentrated BP-EVs were processed by mixing sample with 5 μL of milliQ water and 20 μL of ice-cold methanol. Samples were vigorously shaken by vortexing for 2 min and then 250 μL of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) were added. Samples were immersed in a water bath (ATU Ultrasonidos, Valencia, Spain) with an ultrasound frequency and power of 40 KHz and 100 W, respectively, at 10 °C for 30 min. Then, 25 μL of milliQ water were added to the mixture, and organic phase was separated by centrifugation (1400 x g) at 10 °C for 10 min<strong>[1]</strong>. BP-EVs-derived lipid extracts, contained in the upper phase, were collected and subjected to mass spectrometry (MS). Internal isotopically labelled lipid standards for each class were used for signal normalization<strong>[2]</strong>. Stock solutions were prepared by dissolving lipid standards in MTBE at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, and working solutions were diluted to 2.5 μg/mL in MTBE.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Pizarro C, Arenzana-Rámila I, Pérez-del-Notario N, Pérez-Matute P, González-Sáiz JM. Plasma lipidomic profiling method based on ultrasound extraction and liquid chromatography mass spectrometry. Anal Chem. 2013 Dec 17;85(24):12085-92. doi:10.1021/ac403181c. Epub 2013 Nov 22. PMID:24266777.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> 2Pradas I, Huynh K, Cabré R, Ayala V, Meikle PJ, Jové M, Pamplona R. Lipidomics Reveals a Tissue-Specific Fingerprint. Front Physiol. 2018 Aug 28;9:1165. doi:10.3389/fphys.2018.01165. PMID:30210358.</p>	<p>BP-EVs-derived lipid extracts were analysed following a previously published method<strong>[1]</strong>. For LC-Q-TOF-based lipid molecular species analysis, BP-EVs-derived lipid extracts were subjected to LC-MS using a UPLC 1290 series coupled to ESI-Q-TOF MS/MS 6545 (Agilent Technologies, Barcelona, Spain). Sample compartment of the UHPLC was refrigerated at 4 °C and for each sample 10 μL of FFIBP EVs-derived lipid extract were applied onto 1.8 μm particle 100 x 2.1 mm id Waters Acquity HSS T3 column (Waters, Milford, MA) heated at 55 °C. The flow rate was 400 μL/min with solvent A composed of 10 mM ammonium acetate in acetonitrile:water (40:60, v/v) and solvent B composed of 10 mM ammonium acetate in acetonitrile:isopropanol (10:90, v/v). The gradient started at 40% of mobile phase B and reached 100% B in 10 min and held for 2 min. Finally, the system was switched back to 60% of mobile phase B and was equilibrated for 3 min.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Castro-Perez JM, Kamphorst J, DeGroot J, Lafeber F, Goshawk J, Yu K, Shockcor JP, Vreeken RJ, Hankemeier T. Comprehensive LC-MS E lipidomic analysis using a shotgun approach and its application to biomarker detection and identification in osteoarthritis patients. J Proteome Res. 2010 May 7;9(5):2377-89. doi:10.1021/pr901094j. Erratum in: J Proteome Res. 2011 Jul 1;10(7):3303-8. PMID:20355720.</p>	<p>Duplicate runs of the samples were performed to collect positive and negative electrospray ionized lipid species in TOF mode, operated in full-scan from 100 to 3000 m/z in an extended dynamic range (2 GHz), using N2 as nebulizer gas (5 L/min, 350 °C). The capillary voltage was set at 3500 V with a scan rate of 1 scan/s. Continuous infusion using a double spray with masses 121.050873 &amp; 922.009798 (positive ion mode) and 119.036320 &amp; 966.000725 (negative ion mode) was used for in-run calibration of the mass spectrometer instrument<strong>[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Pradas I, Rovira-Llopis S, Naudí A, Bañuls C, Rocha M, Hernandez-Mijares A, Pamplona R, Victor VM, Jové M. Metformin induces lipid changes on sphingolipid species and oxidized lipids in polycystic ovary syndrome women. Sci Rep. 2019 Nov 5;9(1):16033. doi:10.1038/s41598-019-52263-w. PMID:31690730.</p>	<p><strong>MassHunter Qualitative Analysis</strong> software (Agilent Technologies, Barcelona, Spain) was used to obtain the molecular features of the samples, representing different co-migrating ionic species of a given molecular entity using the Molecular Feature Extractor (MFE) algorithm (Agilent Technologies, Barcelona, Spain). <strong>MassHunter Mass Profiler Professional</strong> software (Agilent Technologies, Barcelona, Spain) and <strong>Metaboanalyst</strong> software were used to perform a non-targeted metabolomics and lipidomic analysis over the extracted features. Only those features with a minimum of 2 ions were selected. After that, the molecular characteristics in the samples were aligned using a retention time window of 0.1 % ± 0.25 min and 30.0 ppm ± 2.0 mDa.</p>	<p>Only features found in at least 70% of the QC samples were taken into account to correct for individual bias and signal was corrected using LOESS approach. Multivariate statistics Partial Least-squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) were performed using <strong>Metaboanalyst</strong> software. Features representing significant differences by univariate statistics ANOVA (p &lt; 0.05), defined by exact mass and retention time, were reported. An initial tentative classification into lipid families of molecular features present consistently in 3 replicates was performed using <strong>CEU Mass Mediator</strong> v.3.0 (accuracy &lt; 10 ppm). Identities of interest were also searched against <strong>HMDB</strong> (accuracy &lt; 30 ppm)&nbsp;and <strong>LIPID MAPS</strong> (accuracy &lt; 20 ppm) databases. The identities obtained were compared to retention time of the authentic standards added. Finally, identities were confirmed by MS/MS by checking the MS/MS spectrums using <strong>LipidBlast</strong> software and <strong>LipidMatch</strong>, a <strong>R</strong>-based tool for lipid identification .</p>

Study Title	Benthic exometabolites and their ecological significance on threatened Caribbean coral reefs (Metabolite collection incubation assays)
Study Description	<p>Benthic organisms sustain coral reefs through their growth and metabolism, but less is known about how their released metabolites influence reef seawater microorganisms. To investigate metabolite composition of benthic exudates and their ecological significance for reef microbial communities, we harvested exudates from six species of Caribbean benthic organisms including stony corals, octocorals, and an invasive encrusting algae, and subjected these exudates to untargeted and targeted metabolomics approaches using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. Incubations with reef seawater microorganisms were conducted to monitor changes in microbial community composition using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and abundance in relation to exudate source and three specific metabolites. Exudates tended to be enriched in amino acids, nucleosides, and vitamins, indicating that benthic organisms contribute labile organic matter to reefs. The phytohormone indole-3-acetic acid was detected in octocoral exudates, suggesting that this metabolite facilitates microbial interactions within and outside of benthic organisms. Exudate compositions were species-specific and significantly enriched in the indole class of metabolites. Microbial abundances and specific microbial taxa responded differently in relation to exudates from stony corals and octocorals, demonstrating the link between benthic organismal composition, metabolite exudates, and microbial growth. Conversely, microbial communities did not respond to additions of the individual metabolites, suggesting that reef microorganisms likely provide diverse metabolite pools that support microbial growth. This work provides novel information about the metabolites released from common Caribbean benthic organisms and indicates that the recent shifts in benthic composition from stony to octocorals alter exudate composition and likely impact microbial community composition and function on coral reefs.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>UPLC-MS Metabolite collection incubation assays</strong> are reported in the current study <strong>MTBLS2855</strong></p><p><strong>UPLC-MS Metabolite uptake incubation assay</strong> is reported in <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/editor/study/MTBLS3286' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MTBLS3286</strong></a></p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Benthic organism exudate collections:</strong></p><p>Exudate collections from benthic organism incubations were conducted on board the R/V Walton Smith vessel in November 2018 in Lameshur Bay, St. John, U.S. Virgin Islands within the Virgin Islands National Park. Each of the 6 experiments was carried out for 1 species at a time. A brief description of the experimental design is included below with additional details in the Supplementary Methods in the paper associated with this study.</p><p><br></p><p>Before each organism experiment, 58 L of surface (non-reef) seawater was collected ~1 mile offshore (18 17.127° N, 064 44.312° W, 31.6 m depth). Cells and particles were removed using peristaltic pressure through a 0.2 μm filter (47 mm, Omnipore, EMD Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA) using metabolomics-grade tubing and the filtrate was collected for the incubations. Additionally, 2-3, 2 L filtrate subsets per experiment were acidified with concentrated hydrochloric acid (final concentration 1% volume/volume) and subjected to solid-phase-extraction (SPE) using a negative vacuum pressure of -3.7 to -5 100xkPA in Hg, to serve as controls. Before SPE, 6 ml, 1 gm Bond Elut PPL cartridges (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) were pre-conditioned with 6 ml of 100% methanol.</p><p><br></p><p>For the experiments, 6 species of benthic organisms were collected from reefs around Lameshur Bay. Experiments were completed on 3 stony corals (<em>Porites astreoides</em>, <em>Siderastrea siderea</em>, and <em>Psuedodiploria strigosa</em>), 2 octocorals (<em>Plexaura homomalla</em> and <em>Gorgonia ventalina</em>), and 1 encrusting alga (<em>Ramicrusta textilis</em>) (see <strong>Table S1</strong> in the paper associated with this study). <em>P. astreoides</em>, <em>S. siderea</em>, and <em>R. textilis</em> were held in a seawater table for 24 hr before the incubations and colonies from the other 3 species were held for 2-3 hr. Coral and algal fragments were generally small (2.5-5.0 cm in length).</p><p><br></p><p>For each incubation, 9, acid-washed, 10 L polycarbonate bins containing FSW (4 L) with lids were secured into an illuminated aquarium table (Prime HD, Aqua illumination, Bethlehem, PA, USA) (Photosynthetically Active Radiation = ~350-600 μmol quanta/m^2/s). Air bubblers with sterilized Fluorinated Ethylene Propylene (FEP) tubing (890 Tubing, Nalgene, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were used to inject air into each bin. Surface seawater was circulated through the aquarium table to maintain reef seawater temperature (29.5 °C). 6 colonies/fragments of 1 species were randomly placed into 6 bins. The other 3 bins were reserved for control incubations containing filtered seawater (FSW) only. A sensor (8K HOBO/PAR loggers; Onset, Wareham, MA) monitored temperature and light conditions. At the end of each 8 hr experiment, colonies/fragments were wrapped in combusted aluminum foil and flash frozen in a charged dry shipper. The water in all the incubations was re-filtered (as outlined above) and 2 L of each filtrate was acidified and subjected to SPE as described above. SPE cartridges were wrapped in combusted aluminum foil, placed in WhirlPak bags, and frozen at -20 °C.</p>	<p>At the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI), metabolites were eluted from the thawed cartridges into combusted, borosilicate test tubes using 100% methanol (Optima grade) within 3 mth of collection. The eluents were transferred into combusted amber 8 ml vials and nearly dried using a vacuum centrifuge. Samples were reconstituted in 200 μl of 95:5 (v/v) Milli-Q (MQ, Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) water: acetonitrile with a deuterated standard mix added as an internal control (see <strong>Table S2</strong> in the paper associated with this assay), vortexed, and prepared for targeted and untargeted metabolomics analyses in both positive and negative ion modes as described previously<strong>[1]</strong>. Samples prepared for untargeted analyses were further diluted (1:200) with the reconstitution solvent. Separate pooled samples (technical replicates) were made for each analysis to assess instrument drift over the course of the run and for downstream sample processing.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Weber L, Armenteros M, Kido Soule M, Longnecker K, Kujawinski EB, Apprill A. Extracellular Reef Metabolites Across the Protected Jardines de la Reina, Cuba Reef System. Front Mar Sci 2020; 7: 582161. doi:10.3389/fmars.2020.582161.</p>	<p><strong>Untargeted metabolomics:</strong></p><p>Samples prepared for untargeted metabolomics were analyzed with a ultra-high performance liquid chromatography system (Vanquish UHPLC, Thermo Scientific) coupled to an ultra-high resolution mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribid, Thermo Scientific). A Waters Acquity HSS T3 column (2.1 x 100 mm, 1.8 μm) equipped with a Vanguard pre-column was used for chromatographic separation at 40 °C for targeted and untargeted analyses. Sample order was randomized and the pooled sample was analyzed after every 6 samples. The column was eluted at 0.5 ml/min with a combination of solvents: A) 0.1% formic acid in water and B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The chromatographic gradient was as follows: 1% B (1 min), 15% B (1-3 min), 50% B (3-6 min), 95% B (6-9 min), 95% B (10 min). The column was washed and re-equilibrated with 1% B (2 min) between injections. Individual autosampler injections (5 μl each) were taken for negative ion mode analyses. The electrospray voltage was set to 2600 V for negative mode. Settings for source gases were 55 (sheath) and 20 (auxiliary) and these settings are presented in arbitrary units. The heated capillary temperature was 375 °C and the vaporizer temperature was 400 °C. Samples were run in positive ion mode first followed by negative ion mode.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Targeted metabolomics:</strong></p><p>Samples prepared for targeted metabolomics were analyzed using an UPLC system (Accela Open Autosampler and Accela 1250 Pump, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled to a heated electrospray ionization source (H-ESI) and a triple stage quadrupole mass spectrometer (TSQ Vantage, Thermo Scientific), operated in selected reaction monitoring (SRM) mode. </p><p><br></p><p>The same chromatography column, column conditions, gradient, and flow rates were used for both targeted and untargeted analyses. Autosampler injections of 5 μl each were made separately for positive and negative modes. Samples were analyzed in a random order and pooled samples were run between every 6 samples to assess instrument drift. SRM parameters were optimized for each target compound using an authentic standard as previously described<strong>[1]</strong> and 2 SRM transitions (precursor – product ion pairs) were monitored for quantification and confirmation.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Kido Soule MC, Longnecker K, Johnson WM, Kujawinski EB . (2015). Environmental metabolomics: analytical strategies. Mar Chem 177: 374–387. doi:10.1016/j.marchem.2015.06.029.</p>	<p><strong>Untargeted metabolomics:</strong></p><p>MS data were collected in the Orbitrap analyzer with a mass resolution of 120,000 FWHM at m/z 200. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was 4e^5, the maximum injection time was 50 s, and the scan range was 100-1000 m/z. Data-dependent MS/MS data were acquired in the Orbitrap analyzer using higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy of 35%. The AGC target value for fragmentation spectra was 5e^4 and the intensity threshold was 2e^4. Cycle time was set at 0.6 s. Precursor selection was performed within the quadrupole with a 1 m/z isolation window. All data were collected in profile mode. Pooled samples were run after every 6 samples to assess instrument performance and drift over the sample run and samples were run in a random order.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Targeted metabolomics:</strong></p><p>Samples were measured using a Thermo Scientific TSQ Vantage Triple Stage Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer. Autosampler injections of 5 μl each were made separately for both positive and negative modes. Samples were analyzed in a random order and pooled samples were run between every 6 samples to assess instrument drift. SRM parameters were optimized for each target compound using an authentic standard as previously described<strong>[1]</strong> and 2 SRM transitions were monitored for quantification and confirmation.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Kido Soule MC, Longnecker K, Johnson WM, Kujawinski EB . (2015). Environmental metabolomics: analytical strategies. Mar Chem 177: 374–387. doi:10.1016/j.marchem.2015.06.029.</p>	<p><strong>Untargeted metabolomics:</strong></p><p>For the untargeted metabolomics analyses, raw files containing MS1 and MS/MS data were converted into <strong>mzML</strong> files using <strong>msconvert</strong> and processed using XCMS<strong>[1]</strong>. Ion modes were analyzed separately. Before processing with XCMS, the R package <strong>Autotuner[2]</strong> was used to find XCMS processing parameters appropriate for the data. In XCMS, the <strong>CentWave</strong> algorithm picked peaks using a gaussian fit. The specific parameters for peak picking for both ion modes were: noise = 10000, peak-width = 3-15, ppm = 15, prefilter = c(2,168.600), integrate = 2, mzdiff = -0.005, snthresh = 10. <strong>Obiwarp</strong> was used to adjust retention times and this step was followed by correspondence analysis. For statistical analyses, MS1 features (defined as unique pairings of mass-to-charge (m/z) values with retention times) in both ion modes were culled following XCMS if they: <strong>1)</strong> had &gt;1 average fold change in the MQ blanks compared to the other samples, <strong>2)</strong> occurred in less than 20% of samples (excluding pooled controls), and/or <strong>3)</strong> were invariant (relative standard deviation of &lt;15%) across the samples (adapted from<strong>[3]</strong>). This feature-removal strategy retained 27% (2317) of MS1 features ionized in positive mode and 84% (4725) of features ionized in negative mode.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Targeted metabolomics:</strong></p><p>For targeted metabolomics analysis, tandem MS/MS data files were converted into <strong>mzML</strong> files using <strong>msconvert</strong> and processed with El-MAVEN<strong>[4]</strong>. Calibration curves for each compound (8 points each) were constructed based on the integrated peak areas using El-MAVEN. The concentrations of metabolites in the original samples were determined by dividing each concentration by the volume of the filtrate that passed through each PPL column. Finally, metabolite concentrations above the limits of detection and quantification were corrected for extraction efficiency using in-house values determined using standard protocols<strong>[5]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Smith CA, Want EJ, O'Maille G, Abagyan R, Siuzdak G. XCMS: processing mass spectrometry data for metabolite profiling using nonlinear peak alignment, matching, and identification. Anal Chem. 2006 Feb 1;78(3):779-87. doi:10.1021/ac051437y. PMID:16448051.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> McLean C, Kujawinski EB. AutoTuner: High Fidelity and Robust Parameter Selection for Metabolomics Data Processing. Anal Chem. 2020 Apr 21;92(8):5724-5732. doi:10.1021/acs.analchem.9b04804. PMID:32212641.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Xia J, Broadhurst DI, Wilson M, Wishart DS. Translational biomarker discovery in clinical metabolomics: an introductory tutorial. Metabolomics. 2013 Apr;9(2):280-299. doi:10.1007/s11306-012-0482-9. PMID:23543913.</p><p><strong>[4]</strong> Agrawal S, Kumar S, Sehgal R, George S, Gupta R, Poddar S, Jha A, Pathak S. El-MAVEN: A Fast, Robust, and User-Friendly Mass Spectrometry Data Processing Engine for Metabolomics. Methods Mol Biol. 2019;1978:301-321. doi:10.1007/978-1-4939-9236-2_19. PMID:31119671.</p><p><strong>[5]</strong> Johnson WM, Kido Soule MC, Kujawinski EB. Extraction efficiency and quantification of dissolved metabolites in targeted marine metabolomics. Limnol Oceanogr Methods 2017; 15: 417–428. doi:10.1002/lom3.10181.</p>	<p>The <strong>mzML</strong> files containing MS1 and MS/MS spectra from untargeted data analyzed in positive ion mode were interrogated using <strong>classical molecular networking (version release 28.1)[1]</strong> available through the<strong> Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking</strong> (GNPS) database. Classical molecular networking was conducted using default parameters (pre-cursor ion mass tolerance = 2.0 Da, fragment ion mass tolerance = 0.5 Da, minimum cosine pair = 0.7, minimum matched fragment ions = 6) and the results can be viewed using the following link: <a href='https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=8abc6ce7fd334ff19eee1d0df12dcfe0' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=8abc6ce7fd334ff19eee1d0df12dcfe0</a>. Feature based <strong>molecular networking (version release 28)[2]</strong> using filtered MS1 data for both positive and negative ion modes did not yield many hits, so networking results are based on the full, unfiltered MS1 features that ionized in positive mode.</p><p><br></p><p>Statistical analyses of metabolomics data were conducted using <strong>Welch’s independent t-tests</strong> and <strong>ANOVA</strong>s or <strong>Wilcoxon</strong> rank sum tests if data were not normally distributed. The R packages <strong>ggplot2[3]</strong> and <strong>vegan[4]</strong> were used to visualize trends and inspect compositional differences between samples in the untargeted datasets using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, non-metric multidimensional scaling analysis, and permutational <strong>PERMANOVA</strong> (<strong>adonis</strong>) tests with and without constraining permutations across the different incubations. The <strong>MetaboAnalyst 5.0</strong> web browser<strong>[5]</strong> was used to putatively identify enriched metabolic pathways in the untargeted data using the mummichog algorithm<strong>[6]</strong>. Lastly, mass of each colony was determined and Pearson correlations were conducted to investigate whether colony size significantly correlated with targeted metabolite concentrations, but we found no correlations between colony size and metabolite concentrations across all species.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Wang M, Carver JJ, Phelan VV, Sanchez LM, Garg N, Peng Y et al.

STUDY
Study Identifier	MTBLS3038
Study Title	High complexity and diel dynamics of planktonic microbiome revealed by multi-omics in Taihu, China
Study Description	<p>Cyanobacterial blooms result from continued short-term succession of planktonic microbiomes, but these short-term variations are little known. Here we address this question with a field diel study in Lake Tai. By integrating untargeted metabolomics—verified by targeted metabolomics—and metagenomics, we reveal the diel cycle of planktonic microbiome in Lake Tai are highly dynamic and complex. First, metabolite abundance and their molecular mass display clear diel changes along with shift in the taxon abundance and biological functions, following the same environmental factors. Some taxa and biological functions (reactions) are highly correlated with the metabolite abundance, and large compounds appear to be more taxon specific. Second, phytoplanktonic and overall planktonic microbiome showed different temporal variation of abundance, opposite levels of abundance and different molecular sizes, and different inter- and intra-specific diversity dynamics. Last, planktonic microbiomes are highly dynamic and complex in inter- and intraspecific diversity in merely one diel cycle, which point to different temperature preference between species, Microcystis aeruginosa and Anabaena sp. This difference was experimentally confirmed in laboratory. Using a multi-omics approach, our study underscores the importance of diel interaction triad between population abundance, biological functions, and environmental factors in leading to microbiome structural change and blooms.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The cyanobacterial samples were collected from Meiliang Bay of Lake Taihu, China. Samples were taken on August 20 2020, when there was a <em>Microcystis aeruginosa</em> bloom in the bay and other parts of the lake. The samples were taken at 2:00 am, 7:00 am, 12:00 pm, 17:00 pm and 21:00 pm, respectively. Aliquots of 150 mL volume of surface water (0-20 cm) containing cyanobacteria was filtered through Whatman GF/F (0.7 μm pore size; glass fiber) filters to collect the entire microbiomes (M samples). Meanwhile, phytoplankton were collected into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes using a phytoplankton net of 20 μm pore size (G samples). In total 3 replicates of metagenomes and 4 replicates for metabolome were taken and both types of samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at -80 °C prior to metabolomic profiling by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China).</p>	<p>A total of 800 μL of cold methanol:acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) was added to remove protein and extract metabolites. The mixture was collected into a new centrifuge tube, and centrifuged at 14,000 x g for 5 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was dried in a vacuum centrifuge. For LC-MS analysis, the samples were re-dissolved in 100 μL acetonitrile:water (1:1, v/v) solvent.</p>	<p>Chromatographic separation of the metabolites was performed on a ExionLC AD system (AB Sciex, USA) equipped with the same ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column and mobile phases. But the solvent gradient changed according to the following conditions: from 0.0 to 3.0 min, from 95% A: 5% B to 80% A: 20% B; from 3.0 to 9.0 min, from 80% A: 20% B to 5% A: 95% B; from 9.0 to 13.0 min, from 5% A: 95% B to 5% A: 95% B; from 13.0 to 13.1 min, from 5% A: 95% B to 95% A: 5% B; from 13.1 to 16.0 min, from 95% A: 5% B to 95% A: 5% B for equilibrating the systems. The sample injection volume was 20 μL and the flow rate was set to 0.4 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. During the period of analysis, all samples were stored at 4 °C.</p>	<p>The UPLC system was coupled to a quadrupole-time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TripleTOF 5600, AB Sciex, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating with a positive mode and negative mode. The optimal conditions were set as followed: source temperature, 500 °C; curtain gas (CUR), 30 psi; Ion Source GS1 and GS2, 50 psi; ion-spray voltage floating (ISVF), -4000 V (neg) / 5000 V (pos); declustering potential, 80 V; a collision energy (CE), 20-60 V rolling for MS/MS. Data acquisition was performed with the <strong>Data Dependent Acquisition</strong> (<strong>DDA</strong>) mode. The detection was carried out over a mass range of 50-1000 m/z.</p>	<p>For peak detection and alignment, the raw data were imported into the<strong> Progenesis QI</strong> 2.3 (Nonlinear Dynamics, Waters, USA). The preprocessing results generated a data matrix consisting of the retention time (RT), mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) and peak intensity. Metabolic features detected at least 80% in any set of samples were retained. After filtering, minimum metabolite values were imputed for specific samples in which the metabolite levels fell below the lower limit of quantitation and each metabolic features were normalized by sum. The internal standard was used for data QC (reproducibility), metabolic features which the relative standard deviation (RSD) of QC &gt; 30% were discarded. Following normalization procedures and imputation, statistical analysis was performed on log transformed data to identify significant differences in metabolite levels between comparable groups.</p>	<p>Mass spectra of these metabolic features were identified by using the accurate mass, MS/MS fragments spectra and isotope ratio difference with searching in reliable biochemical databases as <strong>Human Metabolome Database</strong> (HMDB; <a href='https://hmdb.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://hmdb.ca/</a>) and <strong>Metlin</strong> (<a href='https://metlin.scripps.edu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://metlin.scripps.edu/</a>). The mass tolerance between the measured m/z values and the exact mass of the components of interest was ±10 ppm. For metabolites having MS/MS confirmation, only the ones with MS/MS fragments score above 30 were considered as confidently identified. Otherwise, metabolites had only tentative assignments.</p>

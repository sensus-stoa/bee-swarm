STUDY
Study Identifier	MTBLS3201
Study Title	Flint glass bottles cause white wine aroma identity degradation
Study Description	<p>Due to marketing recommendations, white wines are often bottled in flint glass to improve aesthetics and showcase wine color. Although this practice is known to cause a wine fault, the influence of light on the fruity and flowery aromatic profile of wine is unknown. The aim of this study was to investigate the changes to the white wine volatilome under typical supermarket shelf conditions, using 1,052 bottles of 24 white wines. After only 7 d of shelf life in flint glass bottles, a dramatic loss in terpenes (10 to 30%) and norisoprenoids (30 to 70%) was recorded, whereas colored glass bottles did not evidence such behavior even after 50 d, and darkness preserved the wine's fruity and flowery aromatic integrity. We also proposed an alternative mechanism for the insurgence of the lightstrike off-odor, which takes the varietal aroma loss into account. In light of this understanding of the flint glass negative impact on white wine aroma identity and sensorial character, this packaging should be strongly discouraged. The same findings should be valid for a wide range of several daily consumed foodstuff where transparent packaging is used.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>All samples were commercial <strong>wines </strong>(750 mL bottles) from the vintage 2015, while the experiment was realized in 2016. A room with a shelf was used to mimic the typical supermarket conditions. The room layout included <strong>windows </strong>with curtains (closed), <strong>shelfs </strong>of 4 levels and 5 columns (5 m x 2.5 m x 35 cm), air conditioner fixed at 20 °C and 4 tube lamps (TL-D 58W/33-640 1SL) turned on from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. The kinetic experiment was based on 4 mono-cultivar white <strong>wines </strong>from the 2015 harvest, 2 <strong>Pinot gris </strong>and 2 <strong>Chardonnay</strong>. A map with the position of each bottle on the shelf was prepared in advance, following a randomised order (<a href='www.random.org' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>www.random.org</a>). For each wine/bottle colour, 8 bottles were sampled at 7 time points (2, 7, 14, 22, 29, 40 and 50 days). The samples from the first 4 time points were divided as follows: 5 samples per wine/bottles were placed on the shelf at day 0 and sampled after 2, 7, 14 and 22 days, while 3 samples per wine/bottle were placed on the shelf 21, 14, 7 and 2 days before the end of the experiment and sampled at day 50. Time 0 was the 8th time point, so these bottles were stored directly at 4 °C and in the dark. For each wine, 8 bottles were left in the room simulating supermarket conditions but in a cardboard box, in order to have samples stored under the same environmental conditions but in complete darkness.</p><p>The results of the light-strike fault sensorial analysis, the CieLab colorimetric measurements, light (UV, Vis, and IR) and temperature exposure are reported in the previous publication (<a href='https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fpsl.2020.100492' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fpsl.2020.100492</a>).</p>	<p>A divinylbenzene/carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) coating 50/30 μm, 2 cm length Solid-Phase-Micro-Extraction (SPME) fibre was used for wine volatile extraction. Vials were prepared as follows. 3 ml of wine and 50 μl of 1 mg/l 2-octanol were added to 20 ml screw cap vials containing 1.5 g NaCl. A Gerstel MPS autosampler (GERSTEL GmbH &amp; Co. KG) equipped with the standard sample agitator and a SPME fibre conditioning station was used to extract the volatiles from the sample vial headspace. The samples were incubated for 5 min at 35 °C under 500 rpm rotation at 10 s intervals. Extraction took place at 20 min, prior to desorption in the inlet for 240 s at 250 °C. Quality control (QC) samples, one for each experiment, consisting of equal proportion of each sample were placed at the beginning of run (n=5) and thereafter every 10th sample.&nbsp;</p>	<p>GCxGC system was the Agilent 7890 A (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Injections were performed with splitless mode. Equipped columns were VF-Wax column (100% polyethylene glycol; 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent J&amp;W Scientific Inc., Folsom, CA) as the 1st dimension and Rxi-17Sil MS 1.50 m × 0.15 mm × 0.15 μm, Restek Bellefonte, USA) as the 2nd dimension. A non-moving quad jet dual-stage thermal modulator was used to coupling the 2 columns. The injector/transfer line was maintained at 250 °C. Oven temperature programme conditions were as follows: initial temperature of 40 °C for 4 min, programmed at 6 °C/min at 250 °C, where it remained for 5 min. The secondary oven was kept 5 °C above the primary oven throughout the chromatographic run. The modulator was offset by +15 °C in relation to the secondary oven; the modulation time was 7 s and 1.4 s of hot pulse duration. Helium (99.9995% purity) was used as carrier gas at a constant flow of 1.2 mL/min.</p>	<p>The MS protocol consisted of electron ionization at 70 eV with ion source temperature at 230 °C, a detector voltage of 1543 V with a voltage offset of 200 V, mass range of m/z 35-350, and acquisition rate of 200 spectra per second. There was an acquisition delay of 120 s. MS signal was obtained with Pegasus IV time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Leco Corporation, St. Joseph, MI).</p>	<p><strong>ChromaTOF software version 4.32</strong> was used to perform baseline correction, chromatogram deconvolution and peak alignment. The baseline offset was set to 0.8 and signal to noise (S/N) ratio was set at 100. A peak width of 42 s in the 1D was set and 0.1 s in 2D was established. Traditional, not adaptive integration was used. The required match (similarity) to combine peaks was set to 650.</p><p>For the statistical analysis and the data visualisation, initially the metabolites' areas were normalised by using the IS, and then the areas were&nbsp;expressed as % of the reference group mean concentration for each metabolite.</p>	<p>A library (<strong>NIST 2.0</strong>, <strong>Wiley 8 </strong>and<strong> FFNSC 2</strong>) search was conducted for molecular weights between 40 and 350 limited to report 5 library identifications. The mass threshold was set at 50 and the minimum similarity required to assign a compound name was set at 700. Under these conditions, we were able to detect putative compounds. A series of alkanes (C10-C30) was injected and the GC×GC-TOFMS was operated identically to the above-described protocols. Retention indices were used to calculate the experimental LRI, which was compared to the literature (<strong>NIST 2.0</strong> and <strong>Wiley 8 </strong>and <strong>FFNSC 2</strong>, <strong>VCF</strong>, <strong>Flavour net</strong>, <strong>ChemSpider</strong>). Mass spectra of the compounds that seem to be markers were compared to the mass spectra recorded in <strong>NIST 2.0</strong> and <strong>Wiley 8</strong> and <strong>FFNSC 2</strong> mass spectral library (Chromaleont, Messina, Italy), with a library similarity match of 700. Based on the identification level following assignments were given authentic standards, injected under identical GC×GC-TOFMS conditions, (1st level annotation); mass spectra and linear retention index) (2nd level of annotation); annotated based on their chemical group (3rd level of annotation);&nbsp;considered unknowns (4th level of annotation)<strong>[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Sumner LW, Amberg A, Barrett D, Beale MH, Beger R, Daykin CA, Fan TW, Fiehn O, Goodacre R, Griffin JL, Hankemeier T, Hardy N, Harnly J, Higashi R, Kopka J, Lane AN, Lindon JC, Marriott P, Nicholls AW, Reily MD, Thaden JJ, Viant MR.

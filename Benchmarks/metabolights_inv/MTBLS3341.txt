Study Identifier	MTBLS3341
Study Title	Reduced uremic metabolites are prominent feature of sarcopenia, distinct from antioxidative markers for frailty
Study Description	<p>Due to global aging, frailty and sarcopenia are increasing. Sarcopenia is defined as loss of volume and strength of skeletal muscle in elderlies, while frailty involves multiple domains of aging-related dysfunction, impaired cognition, hypomobility and decreased social activity. However, little is known about the metabolic basis of sarcopenia, either shared with or discrete from frailty. Here we analyzed comprehensive metabolomic data of human blood in relation to sarcopenia, previously collected from 19 elderly participants in our frailty study. Among 131 metabolites, we identified 22 sarcopenia markers, distinct from 15 frailty markers, mainly including antioxidants, although sarcopenia overlaps clinically with physical frailty. Notably, 21 metabolites that decline in sarcopenia or low SMI are uremic compounds that increase in kidney dysfunction. These comprise TCA cycle, urea cycle, nitrogen and methylated metabolites. Sarcopenia markers imply a close link between muscle and kidney function, while frailty markers define a state vulnerable to oxidative stress.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Blood samples were prepared for metabolomic analysis as previously reported<strong>[1][2]</strong>. Blood for clinical tests and metabolomic analysis was collected at the laboratory of Kyoto University Hospital in the morning. All participants were asked not to have breakfast to ensure overnight fasting for at least 12 h until the time of blood sampling. Participants were encouraged to spend their time normally and to drink beverages without calories.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Chaleckis R, Ebe M, Pluskal T, Murakami I, Kondoh H, Yanagida M. Unexpected similarities between the Schizosaccharomyces and human blood metabolomes, and novel human metabolites. Mol Biosyst. 2014 Oct;10(10):2538-51. doi:10.1039/c4mb00346b. PMID:25010571.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Chaleckis R, Murakami I, Takada J, Kondoh H, Yanagida M. Individual variability in human blood metabolites identifies age-related differences. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A. 2016 Apr 19;113(16):4252-9. doi:10.1073/pnas.1603023113. Epub 2016 Mar 28. PMID:27036001.</p>	<p>Since some metabolites are labile, blood samples were quickly quenched at -40 °C in methanol to ensure quick sample processing<strong>[1]</strong>. A total of 10 nmol of 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) and piperazine-N,N'-bis (2-ethanesulfonic acid) (PIPES) were added to each sample, to serve as internal standards.&nbsp;After brief vortexing, samples were transferred to Amicon Ultra 10-kDa cutoff filters (Millipore) to remove proteins and cellular debris.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Chaleckis R, Murakami I, Takada J, Kondoh H, Yanagida M. Individual variability in human blood metabolites identifies age-related differences. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A. 2016 Apr 19;113(16):4252-9. doi:10.1073/pnas.1603023113. Epub 2016 Mar 28. PMID:27036001.</p>	<p>LC-MS data were acquired using an Ultimate 3000 DGP-3600RS liquid chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) combined with an LTQ Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). LC separation utilized a ZIC-pHILIC column (Merck SeQuant, Umeå, Sweden, 150 mm x 2.1 mm, 5 μm particle size). The mobile phase consisted of ammonium carbonate buffer (10 mM, pH 9.3) and acetonitrile. Gradient elution from 80 to 20% acetonitrile over 30 min at a flow rate of 100 μL/min was performed.</p>	<p>An LTQ Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with electrospray ionization (ESI) source was utilized for MS detection. An injection of 1 μL was performed twice for each sample, once with the ESI in positive mode and once in negative mode. Ion spray was set to 4.0 and 2.8 kV (positive and negative ESI, respectively), while the temperature of the capillary was kept at 300 or 350 °C. Nitrogen gas was utilized as a carrier of ionized metabolites. The mass spectrometer ran in full scanning mode with a 100-1000 m/z range and with MS/MS fragmentation scanning in an automatic <strong>data-dependent acquisition</strong> (<strong>DDA</strong>) manner.</p>	<p>Peak areas of metabolites of interest, within the raw LC-MS data, were processed using the <strong>MZmine 2</strong> (version 2.29) software (<a href='http://mzmine.github.io/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>http://mzmine.github.io/</a>) and normalized using the peak areas of HEPES and PIPES<strong>[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Pluskal T, Castillo S, Villar-Briones A, Oresic M. MZmine 2: modular framework for processing, visualizing, and analyzing mass spectrometry-based molecular profile data. BMC Bioinformatics. 2010 Jul 23;11:395. doi:10.1186/1471-2105-11-395. PMID:20650010.</p>	<p><strong>MZmine 2</strong> (version 2.29) software was utilized to measure peak areas for metabolites <strong>[1]</strong>. Isotopic peaks were removed. Lists of peaks for individual samples were arranged, according to their retention times and corresponding m/z. A total of 131 metabolites were identified for each sample by comparing retention times and m/z values of peaks with those of standards (<strong>Data S1</strong> in the paper associated with this study)<strong>[2][3]</strong>. If no standard compound was accessible, metabolites were identified by analyzing MS/MS spectra (MS/MS). In cases where no standard compound or MS/MS spectrum was available, compounds were identified by generating chemical formulae using m/z values in <strong>MZmine</strong> module and given MS status.</p><p><br></p><p>All data acquired were converted into a spreadsheet, followed by evaluation with R statistical software (<a href='https://www.r-project.org/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.r-project.org/</a>). Student’s T test was performed to evaluate statistical significance of differences between groups (significance was set at p &lt; 0.05) and its 95% confidence interval. The ordinary least squares method was used to assess linear regression. Pearson's correlation was performed to evaluate correlations between metabolites and clinical data (assuming p &lt; 0.05). Principal component analysis was performed to visualize the metabolomic model.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Pluskal T, Castillo S, Villar-Briones A, Oresic M. MZmine 2: modular framework for processing, visualizing, and analyzing mass spectrometry-based molecular profile data. BMC Bioinformatics. 2010 Jul 23;11:395. doi:10.1186/1471-2105-11-395. PMID:20650010.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Chaleckis R, Ebe M, Pluskal T, Murakami I, Kondoh H, Yanagida M. Unexpected similarities between the Schizosaccharomyces and human blood metabolomes, and novel human metabolites. Mol Biosyst. 2014 Oct;10(10):2538-51. doi:10.1039/c4mb00346b. PMID:25010571.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Chaleckis R, Murakami I, Takada J, Kondoh H, Yanagida M. Individual variability in human blood metabolites identifies age-related differences. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A. 2016 Apr 19;113(16):4252-9. doi:10.1073/pnas.1603023113. Epub 2016 Mar 28. PMID:27036001.</p>

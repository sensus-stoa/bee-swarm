Study Title	Different carboxyl-rich alicyclic molecules proxy compounds select distinct bacterioplankton for oxidation of dissolved organic matter in the mesopelagic Sargasso Sea
Study Description	<p>Marine dissolved organic matter (DOM) varies in its recalcitrance to rapid microbial degradation. DOM of varying recalcitrance can be exported from the ocean surface to depth by subduction or convective mixing and oxidized over months to decades in deeper seawater. Carboxyl-rich alicyclic molecules (CRAM) are characterized as a major component of recalcitrant DOM throughout the oceanic water column. The oxidation of CRAM-like compounds may depend on specific bacterioplankton lineages with oxidative enzymes capable of catabolizing complex molecular structures like long-chain aliphatics, cyclic alkanes, and carboxylic acids. To investigate the interaction between bacteria and CRAM-like compounds, we conducted microbial remineralization experiments using several compounds rich in carboxyl groups and/or alicyclic rings, including deoxycholate, humic acid, lignin, and benzoic acid, as proxies for CRAM. Mesopelagic seawater (200 m) from the northwest Sargasso Sea was used as media and inoculum and incubated over 28 days. All amendments demonstrated significant DOC removal (2 – 11 µmol C L-1) compared to controls. Bacterioplankton abundance increased significantly in the deoxycholate and benzoic acid treatments relative to controls, with fast-growing <em>Spongiibacteracea</em>, <em>Euryarcheaota</em>, and slow-growing SAR11 enriched in the deoxycholate treatment and fast-growing <em>Alteromonas</em>, <em>Euryarcheaota</em>, and <em>Thaumarcheaota</em> enriched in the benzoic acid treatment. In contrast, bacterioplankton grew slower in the lignin and humic acid treatments, with oligotrophic SAR202 becoming significantly enriched in the lignin treatment. Our results indicate that the character of the CRAM proxy compounds resulted in distinct bacterioplankton removal rates and affected specific lineages of bacterioplankton capable of responding.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Mesopelagic seawater (200 m) from Hydrostation S (32°10′N, 64°30′W) in the northwestern Sargasso Sea was collected onboard the R/V Atlantic Explorer via Niskin bottles on a conductivity, temperature, and depth (CTD) profiling rosette. Water was collected on 07 September 2016 when the water column was thermally stratified (mixed layer of 22 m). Incubations were established with 70% 0.2 μm-filtered seawater and 30% unfiltered seawater. 4 carboxyl-rich alicyclic molecules (CRAM) compounds were added to the seawater at target concentrations of 10 μmol carbon/L: deoxycholate, humic acid, lignin, and benzoic acid. A fifth treatment with no-added organic carbon served as the control. The first set of samples were collected immediately after the bottles were set up, and the remaining polycarbonate bottles were incubated in the dark until samples were collected on day 7. Samples were processed by filtering the seawater through 0.2 μm Omnipore Filters (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA) mounted in Teflon filter holders. The filtrate was then acidified to pH 3 with concentrated hydrochloric acid.</p>	<p>The dissolved organic matter was extracted from the acidified seawater with 1 g/6 ml Bond Elut PPL cartridges (Varian, California, USA)<strong>[1]</strong> with modifications as previously described<strong>[2]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Dittmar, T., B. Koch, N. Hertkorn and G. Kattner (2008). A simple and efficient method for the solid-phase extraction of dissolved organic matter (SPE-DOM) from seawater. Limnology and Oceanography Methods 6: 230-235. doi:10.4319/lom.2008.6.230.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Longnecker, K. (2015). Dissolved organic matter in newly formed sea ice and surface seawater. Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta 171: 39-49. doi:10.1016/j.gca.2015.08.014.</p>	<p>High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Micro AS autosampler and Surveyor MS Pump Plus, Thermo Scientific) was used to separate metabolites on a Synergi Fusion reverse phase C18 column (4 µm, 2.0 x 150 mm, Phenomenex) maintained at 35 °C. The column was eluted with <strong>(A)</strong> 0.1% formic acid in water and <strong>(B)</strong> 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile at a flow rate of 0.25 ml/min. The column was held at 5% B for 2 min, ramped to 65% B over 18 min, quickly ramped to 100% B in 5 min, held at 100% B for 7 min and then equilibrated at 5% B for 8 min prior to the next injection (total run time = 40 min). 20 µl sample was injected individually for positive and negative ion mode analyses.</p>	<p>After liquid chromatography, electrospray ionization (ESI) coupled to a hybrid linear ion trap- Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance (FT-ICR) mass spectrometer (7T LTQ FT Ultra, Thermo Scientific) was used. The mass spectrometer was externally calibrated weekly using the manufacturer’s solutions. The capillary temperature and ESI voltage was optimised at 365 °C and 3.8 kV in negative mode. Sheath gas, auxiliary gas, and sweep gas flow rates were maintained at 35, 5, and 2, respectively (arbitrary units) for the negative ion mode data collection. High resolution MS and data dependent <strong>MS/MS</strong> scans were collected as follows: <strong>(1)</strong> a full MS scan in the FT-ICR analyzer from 150-1000 m/z, with mass resolving power set to 100,000 (defined at m/z 400); and <strong>(2)</strong> collision-induced dissociation fragmentation scans (<strong>MS/MS</strong>) in the linear ion trap for the 4 most abundant ions in each full scan. <strong>MS/MS</strong> spectra were collected under dynamic exclusion with an exclusion time of 20 s. The pooled sample was injected multiple times prior to running the samples to condition the column with the sample matrix. Pooled samples were also ran throughout the sample run to be used for quality control.</p>	<p>Untargeted data files were converted from proprietary <strong>Thermo RAW</strong> into <strong>mzML</strong> format using <strong>msConvert[1]</strong>. These files were processed using <strong>XCMS[2][3]</strong>. Processing returned a spreadsheet of features. Features were defined as peaks with unique mass to charge (m/z) and retention time values, with relative abundances determined by their area.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Chambers MC, Maclean B, Burke R, Amodei D, Ruderman DL, Neumann S et al (2012). A cross-platform toolkit for mass spectrometry and proteomics. Nat Biotechnol 30: 918-920.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Smith CA, Want EJ, O'Maille G, Abagyan R, Siuzdak G (2006). XCMS: Processing Mass Spectrometry Data for Metabolite Profiling Using Nonlinear Peak Alignment, Matching, and Identification. Anal Chem 78: 779-787.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Tautenhahn R, Böttcher C, Neumann S (2008). Highly sensitive feature detection for high resolution LC/MS. BMC Bioinformatics 9: 504.</p>	<p>No metabolite identification was carried out for this study.</p>

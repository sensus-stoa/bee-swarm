Study Identifier	MTBLS3558
Study Title	Dynamic utilization of low-molecular-weight organic substrates across a microbial growth rate gradient
Study Description	<p><strong>AIM:</strong> Low-molecular-weight organic substances (LMWOSs) are at the nexus between micro-organisms, plant roots, detritus and the soil mineral matrix. The nominal oxidation state of carbon (NOSC) has been suggested as a potential parameter for modelling microbial uptake rates of LMWOSs and the efficiency of carbon incorporation into new biomass.</p><p><strong>METHODS AND RESULTS: </strong>In this study, we assessed the role of compound class and oxidation state on uptake kinetics and substrate-specific carbon use efficiency (SUE) during the growth of three model soil micro-organisms, a fungal isolate (Penicillium spinulosum) and two bacterial isolates (Paraburkholderia solitsugae, and Ralstonia pickettii). Isolates were chosen that spanned a growth rate gradient (0.046-0.316 h-1) in media containing 34 common LMWOSs at realistically low initial concentrations (25 μM each). Clustered, co-utilization of LMWOSs occurred for all three organisms. Potential trends (p &lt; 0.05) for early utilization of more oxidized substrates were present for the two bacterial isolates (P. solitsugae and R. pickettii), but high variability (R2 &lt; 0.15) and a small effect of NOSC indicate these relationships are not useful for prediction. The SUEs of selected substrates ranged from 0.16 to 0.99 and there was no observed relationship between NOSC and SUE.</p><p><strong>CONCLUSION:</strong> Our results do not provide compelling population-level support for NOSC as a predictive tool for either uptake kinetics or the efficiency of use of LMWOS in soil solution.</p><p><strong>SIGNIFICANCE AND IMPACT OF THE STUDY:</strong> Metabolic strategies of organisms are likely more important than chemical identity in determining LMWOS cycling in soils. Previous community-level observations may be biased towards fast-responding bacterial community members.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Microbial isolation and characterization</strong></p><p>All strains were isolated from both field moist Oa and B horizons found under a hemlock-dominated stand in Arnot Forest, New York, USA. A total of 3 microbial strains were chosen from a library originally isolated using soil-extractable, solubilized organic matter (SESOM). All SESOM was derived from the Oa horizon. The extract was created using a modified water extraction<strong>[1]</strong>, involving the suspension of 40 g of field-moist Oa horizon with 200 mL of 18.2 MΩ-cm water. Samples were shaken end-to-end for 1 h at room temperature and then left to settle. After 24 h, the solution was sequentially filtered through&nbsp;1.6 μm GF/A, 0.45 μm polyethersulfone (PES), and then filter-sterilized using 0.2 μm PES filters. Enrichment steps were conducted using fresh soil shaken with DI water (1:10 ratio) for 1 h and then left standing for 24 h at room temperature. Enrichments were serially diluted using Winogradsky salts<strong>[2]</strong> and a 100&nbsp;mL sample was then spread onto agar plates (15 g/L) created with SESOM as the sole C-source (denoted 'N') and with the addition of streptomycin and Rose Bengal (denoted 'RB'). All plates were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 3-14 days. Colonies were chosen and re-streaked 3 times before growth was checked in SESOM liquid culture (2x dilution).&nbsp;Further details about the chemical characterization of SESOM and the growth of&nbsp;<em>Paraburkholderia solitsugae </em>in SESOM have previously been reported<strong>[3]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p>Isolates B3N, B1N and Oa1RB were chosen based on growth rates in defined media and characterized using a combination of genomic approaches. Both bacterial species were identified using genomic DNA as discussed previously<strong>[3]</strong>. Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from pelleted cells and submitted to the Cornell University Sequencing Facility for sequencing using 3 multiplexed runs of llumina MiSeq Nano (2 x 250 bp). Fungal identification was conducted using ITS1F (CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA) and ITS4 primers (TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC)<strong>[4]</strong>.&nbsp;<em>P. solitsugae</em>&nbsp;was extensively characterized in prior works<strong>[3][5]</strong>. The other two isolates chosen are referred to by the species name with which they have the highest similarity. The genome assembly for strain B1N (<em>P. solitsugae</em>) and strain B3N (referred to hereafter as&nbsp;<em>Ralstonia pickettii</em>) can be accessed via the NCBI portal using the BioProject accession numbers PRJNA590275. The ITS sequence for strain Oa1RB (referred to hereafter as&nbsp;<em>Penicillium spinulosum</em>) can be accessed via the NCBI GenBank portal using accession numbers MZ375756.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Media preparation and culturing conditions</strong></p><p>All experiments were conducted in batch cultures using acid-washed and autoclaved 125 mL Erlenmeyer flasks using 3 biological replicates per treatment. Incubating flasks were maintained at room temperature on a shaker at 150 rpm. Defined media was prepared based on potential carbon substrates utilized by&nbsp;<em>P. solitsugae</em>&nbsp;during growth in SESOM<strong>[3]</strong>. To assess the relative role of NOSC on utilization profiles and use efficiencies of each substrate, all carbon substrates were added to the media at equimolar concentration (25&nbsp;mM each). Substrates included 3 sugars (glucose (glc), galactose (gal), xylose (xyl)), 20 amino acids (alanine (ala), arginine (arg), asparagine (asn), citrulline (cit), cysteine (cys), glutamate (glu), glutamine (gln), glycine (gly), histidine (his), lysine (lys), methionine (met), ornithine (orn), isoleucine (ile), leucine (leu), lysine (lys), phenylalanine (phe), proline (pro), serine (ser), threonine (thr), tryptophan (trp), valine (val)) and 10 organic acids (acetate (ace),&nbsp;a-ketoglutarate (akg), citrate (citrate), formate (form), gluconate (glucon), lactate (lac), malate (mal), oxalate (oxa), pyruvate (pyr), succinate (succ)). The defined medium was filter-sterilized and subsequently supplemented with 1.68 mM NH4Cl, 0.12 mM KH2PO4, 1x Wolfe’s vitamins and 1x Wolfe’s minerals solutions<strong>[6]</strong>, and pH-adjusted to pH 4.5. The nutrient media was prepared in two different manners: (1) with all unlabeled substrates and (2) with single substrate isotopically labeled unlabeled substrates to allow the tracking of labeling into respired CO2&nbsp;and into biomass.</p><p><br></p><p>Species were individually cultured in unlabeled defined media until exponential growth phase and then a sample of this culture was used to inoculate treatment flasks for experimental trials. For trials with bacterial species (<em>R. pickettii,&nbsp;P. solitsugae</em>), this involved overnight culturing and monitoring of growth using optical density at 595 nm (OD595) followed by inoculation of 50 mL treatment flasks with less than a 250&nbsp;mL subsample (theoretical starting OD595&nbsp;~ 0.0005). For trials with&nbsp;<em>P. spinulosum</em>, biomass measurements were made using destructive sampling of biological replicates of starter flasks (n=3 per timepoint), filtration through 0.2&nbsp;mm PES filters and mass determination after drying at 55 °C for 1 h. At roughly the inflection point of the growth curve, a single flask was sonicated for 5 min and 100&nbsp;mL volumes were used to initiate all treatments. All culturing work was conducted in a laminar flow hood using aseptic technique, sterile filter-pipette tips and with negative controls (n=3) to ensure sterility was maintained.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>For determining substrate utilization profiles, growth trials were conducted solely using the defined media with unlabeled substrates. A growth trial was initiated for each isolate, as described above, with sufficient replicates to allow at least 4 destructive sampling timepoints across the organism’s growth curve. For bacterial species (<em>R. pickettii, P. solitsugae</em>), destructive sampling consisted of centrifugation of ~40 mL of the culture at 10,000 x&nbsp;g&nbsp;for 10 min.&nbsp;Supernatant was decanted and filtered through 0.2&nbsp;mm PES filters. For the fugal species (<em>P. spinulosum</em>), the entire culture of ~50 mL was filtered through 0.2&nbsp;mm PES filters. All samples were immediately allocated into 2 mL centrifuge tubes and stored frozen (-20 °C) until exometabolomics analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Liebeke M, Brözel VS, Hecker M, Lalk M. CRC press. 2010.</p>	<p>All samples are water extractions and/or filtered external media as described above. No further sample extraction was conducted. All samples were injected as is, diluted in the appropriate mobile phase, or concentrated in ultra pure water depending on the analysis method. See individual method descriptions for specific cases. Blanks were created using the appropriate solvent (LC-MS grade H2O or 50% v/v acetonitrile). </p>	<p><strong>HILIC (amino acids)</strong></p><p>A HILIC approach was used to target amino acids<strong>[1]</strong> using a Thermo Scientific Dionex Ultimate 3000 liquid chromatography system coupled to a Q Exactive orbitrap mass spectrometer Waters XBridge column (4.6 mm x 100 mm, 3.5 µm) and electrospray ionization with polarity switching. An injection volume of 50 µL was used with a sample matrix of 50% v/v acetonitrile. A constant flow rate of 1 mL/min was used and consisted of a 13 min binary gradient of fluent A (90:10 v/v acetonitrile:water, 0.15% v/v formic acid, 10 mM ammonium formate) and fluent B (0.15% v/v formic acid in water and 10 mM ammonium formate). The gradient for A was: 0.0 min, 93%; 4.6 min, 93%; 5.5 min, 90%; 6.0 min, 75%; 9.25 min, 60%; 10.25 min, 60%; 10.35 min, 93%; 13.0 min, 93%.&nbsp;Quality control (QC) checks were run every 5-10 samples with a 30% SD limit. And a dilution series was also included consisting of a reference compound mixture; HILIC_STD (all compounds identified as MSI:1 in the HILIC metabolite table).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Reverse phase (low MW compounds)</strong></p><p>A reversed phase approach was conducted to target low molecular weight compounds<strong>[2][3]</strong> using a Thermo Scientific Dionex Ultimate 3000 liquid chromatography system coupled to a Q Exactive orbitrap mass spectrometer using an Acquity UPLC C18 column (2.1 mm x 100 mm, 1.7 µm)&nbsp;and negative electrospray ionization. A volume of 10μL was used for sample injection and column temperature was maintained at 25 °C. Solvent A contained 97:3 (v/v) LC-MS grade H2O:methanol with acetic acid (15mM) and tributylamine (10mM). Solvent B contained 100% methanol. The flow rate was 180 μL/min during the entire sample run (25 min). The solvent gradient with respect to solvent A was the following: 0.0 min, 100%; 2.5 min, 100%; 5.0 min, 80%; 7.5 min, 80%; 10.0 min, 45%; 12.0 min, 45%; 14.0 min, 5%; 17.0 min, 5%; 18.0 min, 0%; 25.0 min, 0%.&nbsp;Quality control (QC) checks were run every 5-10 samples with a 30% SD limit. A dilution series was also included consisting of a reference compound mixture; reverse_phase_STD (all compounds identified as MSI:1 in the reverse phase metabolite table).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Klein AR, Barzen-Hanson KA, Aristilde L. Frontiers in Environmental Science. 2017 5. doi:10.3389/fenvs.2017.00034.</p>	<p><strong>HILIC (amino acids)</strong></p><p>The HILIC approach used a Q Exactive orbitrap mass spectrometer, heated electrospray ionization and the parameters were: sheath gas flow rate, 60 arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 15 arb; sweep gas flow rate, 0 arb; spray voltage, 3 kV; capillary temperature, 380 °C; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 400 °C. Full scan mass spectra (MS1 level) were collected in negative ionization mode at a resolution of 70,000 using 4 mass ranges across the chromatrography run: 1.4-3.0 min, 72-400 m/z (data not collected during void volume of column); 3.0-8.0 min, 85-500 m/z; 8.0-10.0 min, 80-400 m/z; 10.00-19.23 min, 100-840 m/z.</p>	<p>All LC-HRMS data (HILIC and RP) were analyzed using an internally constructed template within Thermo Scientific <strong>Xcalibur 3.0 Quan</strong> browser. The template was built using standards of all compounds identified run between 0-25 µM for quantification. Standards contained all metabolites from the associated method references at the nominal concentration value but only those listed as MSI:1 were used for quantification. Standard curves were run at the beginning and end of each analysis. Linear drift correction was done on a metabolite basis. Quality control (QC) checks were run every 10 samples with a 30% SD limit. If outside of limit, sample were re-run. All integrals were transformed to concentration (µm), except for pyruvate and alpha-ketoglutarate, which were left as area units owing to poor calibration curves.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>NMR spectra were analyzed within <strong>MestReNova</strong> (v.12.0.0-20080), spectra were Fourier transformed and zero-filled to 64 k data points. Spectra were then linearly phase, shifted and apodized using a 0 Hz exponential function. All spectra were manually phase corrected (PH0:-26, PH1:6), and baseline corrected using the built in polynomial fit function. All spectra were reference shifted so TMSP = 0.00 ppm. Lastly, residual water peak was removed using the signal suppression tool with selectivity at 24 centered on the 4.7 ppm signal.</p>	<p>Metabolites from LC-HRMS methods (HILIC and RP) were identified by m/z and retention time using standards and quantified using a linear calibration, and designated as identified (MSI:1).</p><p><br></p><p>For 1H NMR, compound identification was initiated by matching peaks of interest with suitable references as well as using online spectral databanks (<strong>Human Metabolome Database</strong>) to confirm multiplicity and chemical shift. Once identified, integrating regions were defined and used for integration on all samples, and designated as identified (MSI:1).</p>	<p>Samples were analyzed by&nbsp;1H NMR using modified methods previously reported for extracted soil solutions<strong>[1]-[3]</strong>. Briefly, 35 mL of sample was immediately frozen and lyophilized. Samples were then reconstituted with 300 µL of 18.2 MΩ-cm water. After vortexing, samples were buffered to pH 7.0 with an addition of 200 µL sodium hydrogen phosphate (0.1 mM, pH 7.0) made with 25% D2O (v/v) to provide a lock signal and containing 1 mM of sodium 3-trimethylsilyl-[2,2,3,3,-D4]-1-propionic acid (TMSP). Solutions were transferred to 5 mm NMR glass tubes (length 7 in, Wilmad 'Economy'). Spectral referencing was conducted in reference to the TMSP (final concentration = 0.4 mM).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Hochgräfe F, Wolf C, Fuchs S, Liebeke M, Lalk M, Engelmann S, Hecker M.

Study Identifier	MTBLS3565
Study Title	Unique metabolism of different glucosinolates in larvae and adults of a leaf beetle specialised on Brassicaceae
Study Description	Brassicaceae plants contain glucosinolates, which are hydrolysed by myrosinases to toxic products such as isothiocyanates and nitriles, acting as defences. Herbivores have evolved various detoxification strategies, which are reviewed here. Larvae of Phaedon cochleariae (Coleoptera: Chrysomelidae) metabolise hydrolysis products of benzenic glucosinolates by conjugation with aspartic acid. In this study, we investigated whether P. cochleariae uses the same metabolic pathway for structurally different glucosinolates, whether the metabolism differs between adults and larvae and which hydrolysis products are formed as intermediates. Feeding experiments were performed with leaves of watercress (Nasturtium officinale, Brassicaceae) and pea (Pisum sativum, non-Brassicaceae), to which glucosinolates with structurally different side chains (benzenic, indole or aliphatic) or their hydrolysis products were applied. Samples were analysed by UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS or TD-GC-MS. The same aspartic acid conjugates as previously identified in larvae were also detected as major metabolites of benzenic glucosinolates in adults. Indol-3-ylmethyl glucosinolate was mainly metabolised to N-(1H-indol-3-ylcarbonyl) glutamic acid in adults and larvae, while the metabolism of 2-propenyl glucosinolate remains unclear. The metabolism may thus proceed primarily via isothiocyanates rather than via nitriles, while the hydrolysis occurs independently of plant myrosinases. A detoxification by conjugation with these amino acids is not yet known from other Brassicaceae-feeders.

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Insect and plant maintenance:</strong></p><p>Individuals of <em>P. cochleariae</em> were taken from our laboratory rearing (details previously published<strong>[1]</strong>) and kept in ventilated plastic boxes (20 x 20 x 6.5 cm; 50-100 individuals per box) in a climate cabinet at 20 °C, 65% r.h. and 16:8 h light:dark. Insects were fed with shoots of watercress. Plants of watercress (seeds from Volmary GmbH, Münster, Germany) and pea (seeds from Kiepenkerl, Bruno Nebelung GmbH, Konken, Germany) used for the rearing and/or for feeding experiments (see below) were 6-8 wk old, not flowering and grown in a greenhouse (60% r.h., 16:8 h light:dark). The glucosinolate profile of these watercress plants was dominated by 2-phenylethyl glucosinolate (~89%), followed by 7-(methylsulfinyl)heptyl glucosinolate (~6%) and indol-3-ylmethyl glucosinolate as well as 8-(methylsulfinyl)octyl glucosinolate occurring in low amounts (~1% or less). The use of the plants in the present study complies with international, national and institutional guidelines.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Feeding bioassays with glucosinolates and hydrolysis products:</strong></p><p>The metabolism of structurally different glucosinolates was investigated by providing individuals of <em>P. cochleariae</em> with leaf discs (2 cm diameter) of either watercress or pea. Leaf discs were treated with solutions (50 μl, 40 mM) of either benzyl glucosinolate, 4-hydroxybenzyl glucosinolate, indol-3-ylmethyl glucosinolate or 2-propenyl glucosinolate (all &gt;95–99%, Phytoplan Diehm &amp; Neuberger GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany), dissolved each in a mixture of methanol (MeOH), millipore water (H2O) and dichloromethane (DCM) (64:30:6, v:v:v; organic solvents from Fisher Scientific, Loughborough, UK), or treated with the solvent only (as in<strong>[1]</strong>). Discs were kept for 40 min on moistened filter paper under a fume hood for solvent evaporation and then offered in Petri dishes (55 mm diameter) to <em>P. cochleariae</em>.</p><p><br></p><p>All mentioned glucosinolates were offered to adults (24 h after emergence), while experiments with larvae (5 h after moult to second instar) were performed with the 2 glucosinolates indol-3-ylmethyl glucosinolate and 2-propenyl glucosinolate only, because the larval metabolism of benzenic glucosinolates has already been investigated<strong>[1]</strong>. Individuals had been starved since their last moults. The treated leaf discs were offered to groups of 3 individuals for 24 h (watercress) or 48–72 h (pea) (6 replicates per plant species and test solution) to seek an uptake of at least 10%. Afterwards, from half of the groups individuals were dissected on ice into guts (<strong>“gut”</strong>) and remaining body tissues (<strong>“body”</strong>). The remaining individuals were transferred into 2 ml Eppendorf tubes without food for 3 h to collect their faeces (<strong>“faeces”</strong>), and individuals with emptied guts were collected separately (<strong>“whole”</strong>). Thus, per treatment combination, 3 replicates pooled from 3 individuals were collected. In addition, leaf discs treated with the same glucosinolates or solvent but without insects were kept in Petri dishes for the same experimental period (n = 3 per treatment group and plant species). All insect samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at −80 °C and lyophilised. The freeze-dried samples were than extracted and measured by <strong>UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Friedrichs J, Schweiger R, Geisler S, Mix A, Wittstock U, Müller C. Novel glucosinolate metabolism in larvae of the leaf beetle Phaedon cochleariae. Insect Biochem Mol Biol. 2020 Sep;124:103431. doi:10.1016/j.ibmb.2020.103431. PMID:32653632.</p><p><br></p>	<p>For the identification of glucosinolates and their breakdown metabolites in insect and plant material, samples were extracted in 90% MeOH and analysed using UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. Samples were extracted on ice in ice-cold MeOH (300 μl, 90%, v:v) containing hydrocortisone (65.1 μg in 300 μl, &gt;98%, Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Steinheim, Germany) as internal standard. The extracts were vortexed for 1 min and centrifuged for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were filtered with 0.2 μm polytetrafluoroethylene membrane syringe filters (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) and stored at -80 °C.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Friedrichs J, Schweiger R, Geisler S, Mix A, Wittstock U, Müller C. Novel glucosinolate metabolism in larvae of the leaf beetle Phaedon cochleariae. Insect Biochem Mol Biol. 2020 Sep;124:103431. doi:10.1016/j.ibmb.2020.103431. PMID:32653632.</p>	<p>Samples were analysed with an <strong>UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS</strong> system (UHPLC: Dionex UltiMate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, San José, CA, USA; Q-TOF: compact, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) connected with a Kinetex XB-C18 column (1.7 μm, 150 x 2.1 mm, with guard column, Phenomenex), using a gradient of water (eluent A) to acetonitrile (eluent B; LC-MS grade, 99.95%, Th. Geyer GmbH &amp; Co. KG, Renningen, Germany), both acidified with formic acid (FA, 0.1%, v:v; eluent additive for LC-MS, ~98%, Sigma-Aldrich). The gradient went from 2% to 30% B within 20 min and then increased to 75% B within 9 min with a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min at 45 °C, followed by column cleaning and equilibration.</p>	<p>Data were collected using a Bruker Daltonics compact Q-TOF mass spectrometer. The electrospray ionisation (ESI) source was operated in negative mode. MS and MS/MS line spectra (50-1300 m/z) were recorded at 8 Hz. The MS parameter settings were: end plate offset 500 V, capillary voltage 3000 V, nebuliser (N2) pressure 3 bar, dry gas (N2) flow and temperature 12 L/min at 275 °C, quadrupole ion energy 4 eV, low mass 90 m/z, collision energy in MS mode 7 eV, transfer time 75 μs, pre-pulse storage 6 μs. Nitrogen was chosen as collision gas in Auto-MS/MS mode to obtain fragments of the most intense ions, using a ramping of collision energies and isolation widths with increasing m/z. For recalibration, a sodium formate calibration solution was introduced into the ESI source before each sample. Next to the insect and plant samples, 12 blanks were extracted and measured using the identical method.</p>	<p>In <strong>Compass DataAnalysis 4.4</strong> (Bruker Daltonics), for each sample the m/z axis was recalibrated using the sodium formate calibration peak. Then, the <strong>Find Molecular Feature</strong> algorithm was used for picking of molecular features (each defined by a retention time and m/z), applying spectral background subtraction. Settings used for the algorithm were signal-to-noise threshold: 3, correlation coefficient threshold: 0.75, minimum compounds length: 16 spectra, smoothing width: 5. Molecular features probably belonging to the same metabolite were sorted together in so-called buckets, allowing the ion types [M-H]-, [M-H2O-H]-, [M+Cl]-, [M+HCOOH-H]-, [M+CH3COOH-H]-, [2M-H]-, [2M+HCOOH-H]-, [2M+CH3COOH-H]- and [3M-H]-. For alignments of buckets across the set of samples, shifts of 0.1 min (retention time) and 5 mDa (m/z) were allowed in the processing software <strong>Compass ProfileAnalysis 2.3</strong> (Bruker Daltonics). Within each bucket, the intensity (peak height) of the most intense molecular feature was used for quantification and divided by the height of the [M+HCOOH-H]- ion of hydrocortisone in the same sample. In the next step, fold changes were calculated for these features for each glucosinolate treatment as mean intensities in insect samples from the glucosinolate treatment divided by the mean intensities in insect samples from the solvent control treatment.</p>	<p>To screen for potential breakdown metabolites of the glucosinolates, the intensities of molecular features (normalised using the internal standard hydrocortisone) in the different treatment groups (plant species, treatment of the leaf disc, type of insect sample) were compared. In the insect datasets only features were retained: <strong>(1)</strong> whose mean intensity (peak height) in at least 1 treatment group was at least 50x greater than the mean intensity in the blank samples, <strong>(2)</strong> which were present in at least 2 of the 3 replicates in at least 1 treatment group, <strong>(3)</strong> whose fold changes were &gt;2 or features only occurred in samples from individuals fed with the glucosinolate (not in the control individuals), <strong>(4)</strong> whose mean intensity was &gt;1000 counts in at least 1 glucosinolate treatment group.</p><p><br></p><p>To identify the metabolites <strong>1-13</strong> (<strong>Tables 2</strong> and <strong>S2</strong>), the samples with the highest feature intensities were measured again by <strong>UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS</strong> with <strong>multiple reaction monitoring (MRM)</strong> in negative and/or (with capillary voltage 4500 V) positive ESI mode and/or at a lower spectral rate (2 Hz). Specific collision energies and isolation widths for the parent ion m/z were used to obtain <strong>MS/MS</strong> spectra. The <strong>SmartFormula</strong> (manually and 3D) function in <strong>DataAnalysis</strong> was applied to create molecular formulas of parent and daughter ions using their accurate m/z values and isotopic patterns. <strong>MetFrag[1]</strong> was used for <em>in-silico</em> fragmentation (compound lists from: <strong>PubChem</strong>) to obtain potential structural formulas, while spectra were additionally compared with entries in the <strong>MassBank of North America</strong> (<a href='https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/</a>). Furthermore, retention times, <strong>UV/VIS</strong>, <strong>MS</strong> and <strong>MS/MS</strong> spectra of metabolites were compared to those of reference standards in an in-house database.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Ruttkies C, Schymanski EL, Wolf S, Hollender J, Neumann S. MetFrag relaunched: incorporating strategies beyond in silico fragmentation. J Cheminform. 2016 Jan 29;8:3. doi: 10.1186/s13321-016-0115-9. PMID: 26834843; PMCID: PMC4732001.</p><p><br></p>

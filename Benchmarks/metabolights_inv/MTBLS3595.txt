Study Identifier	MTBLS3595
Study Title	Community metabolomics provides insights into mechanisms of pollution-induced community tolerance of periphyton
Study Description	<p>Chemical pollution is a major concern for freshwater ecosystems, but the impact and mechanisms of chemical stressors on communities are barely understood. Pollution stress beyond natural homeostatic capacities can trigger succession of tolerant species within a community, enhancing the overall community tolerance. This process was operationalized in the Pollution-Induced Community Tolerance (PICT) concept and applied in many case studies, however, the molecular mechanisms of community tolerance and implications for ecological functions remain largely unexplored. Our study aimed to demonstrate that 1) community metabolomics can unravel potential mechanisms of PICT in periphyton and 2) induced tolerance helps to maintain primary production under re-occuring pollution. To this end, we grew periphyton for 5 weeks with and without the model herbicide diuron in microcosms, quantified PICT, and determined the related metabolic fingerprint of periphyton by GC–MS-based untargeted metabolomics. Further, we explored the autotrophic community based on pigment composition and functional parameters including photosynthesis and gross primary production. Chronic diuron exposure resulted in a shift in pigment composition, higher community tolerance and an individual metabolic fingerprint in the contaminated communities. </p><p>Opposing responses of selected metabolites during a short-term exposure indicated differences in diuron pre-adaptation in the different communities. Metabolites (threonic acid and two sugar acid lactones) were found to be related to tolerance development, suggesting that ascorbate metabolism was induced in contaminated communities. Despite these compensating mechanism, contaminated communities were compromised in production-to-respiration ratio and biomass. A ranking of sensitivity thresholds of different biological endpoints revealed that metabolites were less sensitive than photosynthetic parameters, which reflects the mode-of-action of the herbicide. In conclusion, we could demonstrate that community metabolomics is able to unravel complex biochemical changes and allows mechanistic insights into community tolerance. </p><p>Moreover, we were able to show that induced community tolerance was insufficient to safeguard functions like primary production.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Selection Phase </strong></p><p>Glass slides (15.25 cm^2) were placed as substrate in 20 L glass tanks and unfiltered river water from the Parthe river, a local stream (sampling point: 51°22'18.5'N 12°25'07.7'E) served as inoculum for biofilm growth. The glass slide holder had varying spatial position (up, mid and low), which were approximately 10, 13 and 16 cm below water surface. Biofilms were grown for 5 wk, and half of the glass tanks were contaminated with 4 µg/L <strong>diuron</strong>, starting from the second week of cultivation. Water was stirred permanently and exchanged weekly to guarantee sufficient supply of nutrients and a near-natural inoculum of species. The exposure concentration of <strong>diuron</strong> was renewed after each water exchange and monitored before and after <strong>diuron</strong> addition on a QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS System. Oxygen saturation, pH and temperature were monitored at each water exchange. Water temperature was maintained at 22±1 °C and the light cycle was set to 14/10 h light/dark cycle. The photon yield of the LED plant lamps (C-series, Valoya, Helsinki, Finland) above the water surface was approximately 150 µmol photons m^2/s.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Detection Phase </strong></p><p>After the selection phase the slides were randomly sampled from the different tanks at varying spatial position (up, mid and low) to avoid a spatial bias in the microbial community. The slides were exposed for a second time in 500 mL petri dishes and each concentration was duplicated, while controls were triplicated. The <strong>diuron</strong> exposure solution was equilibrated for 30 min prior to biofilm addition and then an aliquot for exposure monitoring was taken. Light and temperature parameters of the second exposure were kept consistent with the settings of the selection phase using a phytotron (MultitronPro, IFORS, Einsbach, Germany). Petri dishes were slightly agitated at 30 rpm and illuminated by LEDs. After 1 h of exposure the slides were sampled by transferring them 50 mL falcon tubes. The tubes were immediately quenched in liquid nitrogen and stored until extraction at -80 °C.</p>	<p>First, 20 mg of a freeze dried sample was transferred into a FastPrep® tube. Then 150 mg of glass beads (5 mm) and 1.2 mL of a solvent mixture containing methanol, chloroform and water (10:5:4, v/v/v) and ribitol as internal standard (10 mg/L) was added to each sample. Cells were disrupted using a FastPrep®-24 (MP Biomedicals, USA) for 35 s in 3 cycles, while the samples were cooled down for 2 min after each run. Cell debris were centrifuged (6 min, 6 °C, 20000 x g, rotor 12148) and 1 mL of the supernatant, 0.8 mL milli-Q water and 1.6 mL chloroform were transferred into a 10 mL glass tube. The hydrophilic and lipophilic metabolites were separated by vortexing and a subsequent centrifugation step (10 min, 5 °C, 4000 x g, rotor). A total of 1 mL of the upper hydrophilic methanol/water (polar) phase was transferred into a 3 mL reaction glass vial and dried at 40 °C and gentle nitrogen circulation. The dried sample was incubated with 200 µL MOXTM reagent (2% methoxyamine x HCl in pyridine) for 2 h at 80 °C. The methoxaminated samples were dried the same way as before, 100 µL of MSTFA reagent was added and they were incubated at 90 °C for 20 min. Process blanks were included in the metabolite extraction and derivatization step to account and subtract the background contamination. After derivatization a pool sample (QC sample) from all individual samples was created by pooling 10 µL of each sample. Then the samples were subjected to a gas chromatograph coupled to a time-of-flight mass spectrometer (GC-TOFMS).&nbsp;</p>	<p>The autosampler parameters (CTC PAL Dual Head Autosampler) were as follows: Sample Volume, 1 µL; Solvent A/B Post Wash, 5 (n-hexane, isopropanol). The GC parameters (Agilent 7890 Gas Chromatograph) were as follows: Column, DB5 column 30 m + 10 m DG pre-column, 0.25 mm, 0.25 µm, Agilent; Front Inlet Mode, Splitless;&nbsp;Front Inlet &amp; Transfer Line Temp, 250 °C;&nbsp;Front Inlet Septum Purge Flow, 3 mL/min,&nbsp;Front Inlet Purge Time, 1 min;&nbsp;Front Inlet Purge Flow, 40 mL/min;&nbsp;Front Inlet Total Flow, 42.2 mL/min;&nbsp;Target Flow, 2.2 mL/min (Constant Flow);&nbsp;Oven Temperature Ramp, 60 (Duration: 1 min) to 325 (Duration: 10 min) at 10 °C/min; GC Method Total Time, 37.5 min.</p><p><br></p><p>The derivatized samples were analyzed in a randomized order and MSTFA blanks were ran every 4th sample. RI samples containing MSTFA and the C7-C30 saturated alkanes (1000 μg/mL, Supelco) were placed before and after each run.</p>	<p>The MS parameters (Pegasus 4D, LECO) were as follows: Mass Analyzer, TOF;&nbsp;Acquisition Delay, 5.5 min;&nbsp;Scan Range, 50-600 m/z;&nbsp;Acquisition Rate, 10 spectra/s;&nbsp;Electron Energy, -70 V;&nbsp;Ion Source, 250 °C.</p>	<p>The raw data was aligned using the free software ‘<strong>MS-DIAL</strong>’ version 4.24. The alignment table was corrected, filtered, normalized, aggregated, transformed and visualized. First the alignment was quality checked for peaks that were divided into different alignment ID’s. Those peaks were merged if the EI spectrum similarity was above 80%, the retention time gap was below 8 s and the conflict between the aligned metabolites was below 5%. In total 57 of the 1354 aligned peaks were merged with their corresponding counterpart to correct for peak splitting in the alignment routine. The dataset was filtered for artifacts, which were introduced by the <strong>MS-DIAL</strong> alignment (automatically interpolated peak areas) and sporadically occurring peaks. To this end data were filtered for consistency by requiring a peak to occur at a minimum of 30% in the replicates of 3 subsequent treatments. The filtering revealed 601 sporadic peaks which were removed. In a last filtering step the blank contamination was removed by setting a 5-fold threshold, which the mean peak area must exceed compared to the blank mean. The blank filtering step further reduced the dataset to a final selection of 190 metabolites, which were normalized to the internal standard ribitol. Then, the replicates of each exposure treatment were aggregated to a mean value, since they covered the different slide positions (up, mid and low) during cultivation. Finally, the aggregated data was mean centered and auto-scaled for the principal component analysis.</p>	<p>The metabolites were annotated by reference mass spectra of the ‘<strong>Golm Metabolome Database</strong>’ version GMD_20111121_VAR5_ALK_MSP<strong>[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Kopka J, Schauer N, Krueger S, Birkemeyer C, Usadel B, Bergmüller E, Dörmann P, Weckwerth W, Gibon Y, Stitt M, Willmitzer L, Fernie AR, Steinhauser D. GMD@CSB.DB: the Golm Metabolome Database. Bioinformatics. 2005 Apr 15;21(8):1635-8. doi:10.1093/bioinformatics/bti236. Epub 2004 Dec 21. PMID:15613389.</p>

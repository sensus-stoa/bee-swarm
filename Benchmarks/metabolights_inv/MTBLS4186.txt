Study Identifier	MTBLS4186
Study Title	Effects of Radiofrequency Field from 5G Communications on fecal microbiome and metabolome profiles in mice
Study Description	<p>With the rapid development of 5G networks, the influence of the radiofrequency field (RF) generated from 5G communication equipment on human health are gathering increasing attention. This study aimed at assessing the effects of long-term exposure to 4.9 GHz (one of the working frequency of 5G communication) RF field on fecal microbiome and metabolome profiles in 9-week-old male C57BL/6 mice. The animals were divided into sham group (n = 8) and radiofrequency group (RF group; n = 8), then whole body exposed or sham exposed to RF field for one hour per day during 3 weeks (21 days) at whole body Specific Absorption Rate (SAR) of 6.3 W/kg. After RF exposure, the mice fecal samples were collected to detect gut microorganisms and metabolites by 16S rRNA gene sequencing method and LC-MS method, respectively. The results showed that intestinal microbial compositions were altered in RF group, as evidenced by reduced microbial diversity and changed microbial community distribution. Metabolomics profiling identified 258 significantly differentially abundant metabolites in RF group, 57 of which can be classified to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Besides, functional correlation analysis showed that changes in gut microbiota genera were significantly correlated with changes in fecal metabolites. In summary, the results suggested that altered gut microbiota and metabolic profile are associated with 4.9 GHz radiofrequency exposure.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The 4.9 GHz RF-exposure system mainly consisted of a signal generator, an amplifier and a radiating antenna, which was described in a previous paper published by our laboratory<strong>[1]</strong>. Mice were randomly divided into Sham group and RF group. For the RF group, the mice with conscious were first placed in the plexiglass boxes. The size of each box was 32 x 42 x 80 mm with small ventilation holes on every side, and the mice were unable to move freely in the box. Then, the boxes with animals (an animal in a box) were laid out right next to each other during exposure. The animals were whole body exposed to 4.9 GHz RF field for 21 consecutive days (1 h/day). During exposure, the mice were awake, but not allowed to access food and water. The average power density (PD) was 50 ± 2.5 W/m^2, which measured in advance by an electromagnetic field meter (PMM8053A, PMM Costruzioni Electtroniche Centro Misure Radio Electriche S.r.l., Milan, Italy). For the Sham group, the animals were processed in the same condition as the RF group, except the exposure control system kept closed. Fecal samples (4-6 grains) were collected after the last exposure and placed directly on ice and subsequently stored at -80 °C for metabolomic analyses. Metabolome profiling was conducted on UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Rui G, Liu LY, Guo L, Xue YZ, Lai PP, Gao P, Xing JL, Li J, Ding GR. Effects of 5.8 GHz microwave on hippocampal synaptic plasticity of rats. Int J Environ Health Res. 2022 Oct;32(10):2247-2259. doi:10.1080/09603123.2021.1952165. Epub 2021 Jul 22. PMID:34293966.</p>	<p>LC-MS was used to compare the fecal metabolite signatures of Sham and RF mice. Metabolites were extracted from fecal samples using the following method. Briefly, 50 mg fecal sample was suspended in 400 μL cold methanol solution (4:1, methanol:water) with 0.02 mg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanine. The suspension was homogenized by lapping for 6 min (-10 °C, 50 Hz) followed by low temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). After 15 min of centrifugation (4 °C, 13,000 x g), the resulting supernatant was transferred into clean tubes for analysis.</p>	<p>A total of 2 μL of sample was separated by HSS T3 column (100 mm x 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm) and Thermo Scientific Vanquish Horizon system, and then entered into mass spectrometry detection. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water:acetonitrile (95:5, v/v) (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile:isopropanol:water (47.5:47.5:5, v/v) (solvent B). The solvent gradient changed according to the following conditions: from 0.0 to 3.5 min, from 0 to 24.5% B (0.4 mL/min); from 3.5 to 5.0 min, from 24.5 to 65% B (0.4 mL/min); from 5.0 to 5.5 min, from 65 to 100% B (0.4 mL/min); from 5.5 to 7.4 min, from 100 to 100% B (from 0.4 to 0.6 mL/min); from 7.4 to 7.6 min, from 100 to 51.5% B (0.6 mL/min); from 7.6 to 7.8 min, from 51.5 to 0% B (from 0.6 to 0.5 mL/min); from 7.8 to 9.0 min, from 0 to 0% B (from 0.5 to 0.4 mL/min); from 9.0 to 10.0 min, from 0 to 0% B (0.4 mL/min) for equilibrating the systems. The sample injection volume was 2 µL and the flow rate was set to 0.4 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. During the period of analysis, all these samples were stored at 4 °C.</p>	<p>The mass spectrometric data was collected using a Thermo UHPLC -Q Exactive HF-X Mass Spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in either positive or negative ion mode. The optimal conditions were set as followed: heater temperature, 425 °C; Capillary temperature, 325 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 50 arb; Aux gas flow rate, 13 arb; ion-spray voltage floating (ISVF), -3500 V (negative) / +3500 V (positive); Normalized collision energy, 20-40-60 V rolling for MS/MS. Full MS resolution was 60,000 and MS/MS resolution was 7500. Data acquisition was performed with the <strong>Data Dependent Acquisition</strong> (<strong>DDA</strong>) mode. The detection was carried out over a mass range of 70-1050 m/z.</p>	<p>After the mass spectrometry detection is completed, the raw data of LC/MS is preprocessed by <strong>Progenesis QI</strong> (Waters Corporation，Milford, USA) software and then uploaded to the <strong>Majorbio cloud platform </strong>(<a href='https://cloud.majorbio.com' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://cloud.majorbio.com</a>) for data analysis. Metabolic features detected at least 50% in any set of samples were retained. After filtering, average metabolite values were imputed for specific samples in which the metabolite levels fell below the lower limit of quantitation and each metabolic features were normalized by average. In order to reduce the errors caused by sample preparation and instrument instability, the response intensity of the sample mass spectrum peaks was normalized by the average normalization method, and the normalized data matrix was obtained. At the same time, variables with relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt; 30% of QC samples were removed, and log10 logarithmization was performed to obtain the final data matrix for subsequent analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>For metabolomic analysis, Student’s t-test was used to compare metabolite profiles and differences between two groups visualized by Volcano plot. In order to explain the physical and chemical properties and biological functions of metabolites, the online <strong>KEGG</strong> (<strong>Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes</strong>; <a href='https://www.genome.jp/kegg' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.genome.jp/kegg</a>) databases was used to perform level-one and level-two identification and annotation. The importance of each metabolite was ranked according to their projection (VIP) scores calculated from the OPLS-DA model.</p><p><br></p><p>In total, 1155 metabolites were identified, including positive ion mode and negative ion mode for 504 and 651, respectively, 174 and 201 of which were annotated to <strong>KEGG</strong>. We also investigated the difference in fecal metabolites of mice that result from long-term exposure to 4.9 GHz radiofrequency field. The volcano plot revealed that 141 metabolites were downregulated while 117 genes were upregulated RF group.</p>	<p>The metabolites were searched and identified, and the main database was the <strong>KEGG</strong> (<a href='https://www.genome.jp/kegg/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.genome.jp/kegg/</a>), <strong>HMDB</strong> (<a href='https://hmdb.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://hmdb.ca/</a>), <strong>Metlin</strong> (<a href='https://metlin.scripps.edu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://metlin.scripps.edu/</a>) and <strong>Majorbio Database</strong>.</p>

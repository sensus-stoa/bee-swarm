Study Identifier	MTBLS4415
Study Title	The pyruvate kinase, a metabolic sensor powering glycolysis, drives the metabolic control of DNA replication
Study Description	<p><strong>BACKGROUND:</strong> In all living organisms,&nbsp;DNA replication is exquisitely regulated in a wide range of growth conditions to achieve timely and accurate genome duplication prior to cell division. Failures in this regulation cause DNA damage with potentially disastrous consequences for cell viability and human health,&nbsp;including cancer. To cope with these threats, cells tightly&nbsp;control replication initiation&nbsp;using well-known mechanisms. They also&nbsp;couple DNA synthesis to nutrient richness and growth rate&nbsp;through a poorly understood process thought to involve central carbon metabolism.&nbsp;One such process&nbsp;may involve the cross-species conserved pyruvate kinase (PykA) which catalyzes the last reaction of glycolysis. Here we have investigated the role of PykA in regulating DNA replication&nbsp;in the model system&nbsp;<em>Bacillus subtilis.</em></p><p><strong>RESULTS: </strong>On analysing mutants of the catalytic (Cat) and C-terminal (PEPut) domains of&nbsp;<em>B. subtilis</em>&nbsp;PykA we found&nbsp;replication phenotypes in conditions where PykA is dispensable for growth. These phenotypes are independent from the effect of mutations on PykA catalytic activity and are not associated with significant changes in the metabolome. PEPut operates as a nutrient-dependent inhibitor of initiation while Cat acts as a stimulator of replication fork speed. Disruption of either PEPut or Cat replication function dramatically impacted the cell cycle and replication timing even in cells fully proficient in known replication control functions. <em>In vitro</em>, PykA modulates activities of enzymes essential for replication initiation and elongation via functional interactions. Additional experiments showed that PEPut regulates PykA activity and that Cat and PEPut determinants important for PykA catalytic activity regulation are also important for PykA-driven replication functions.</p><p><strong>CONCLUSIONS:</strong> We infer from our findings that PykA typifies a new family of cross-species replication control regulators that drive the metabolic control of replication through a mechanism involving regulatory determinants of PykA catalytic activity. As disruption of PykA replication functions causes dramatic replication defects, we suggest that dysfunctions in this new family of universal replication regulators may pave the path to genetic instability and carcinogenesis.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Bacteria strains and cultivation conditions:</strong></p><p>Strains and plasmids are listed in <strong>Table S4</strong> in the paper associated with this study. <em>E. coli</em> Stellar competent cells (Clontech, Saint-Germain-en-Laye, France) were used as host for plasmid constructions. Most of the <em>B. subtilis</em> strains were constructed by transforming competent cells with genomic DNA (<strong>Table S4</strong>). Genotype of constructed plasmids and strains were checked by phenotypic analyses, endonuclease restriction, PCR analysis and/or Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics, Germany).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Growth conditions:</strong></p><p>Routinely, <em>B. subtilis</em> and <em>E. coli</em> cells were grown at 37 °C in LB with or without antibiotics at the following concentrations: spectinomycin (Sp, 60 μg/mL); tetracycline (Tet, 7.5 μg/mL); chloramphenicol (Cm, 5 μg/mL); phleomycin (Phl, 7.5 μg/mL); kanamycin (Kan, 5 μg/mL); ampicillin (Amp, 50 μg/mL). Malate (0.4 % w/v) was added to LB for routine cultivation of pykA mutants. Other studies were carried out with cells grown at 37 °C in a minimal medium (K2HPO4: 80 mM; KH2PO4: 44 mM; (NH4)2SO4: 15 mM; C6H5Na3O7 2H20: 3, 4 mM; CaCl2: 50 mM; MgSO4: 2 mM; Fe(III) citrate: 11 μg/mL; MnCl2: 10 μM; FeSO4: 1 μM; FeCl3: 4 μg/mL; tryptophan: 0.01% w/v) supplemented with various carbon sources as follows (in w/v): MC medium: malate (0.4%) + enzymatic casein hydrolysate (casa, 0.02%); GC medium: glucose (0.4%) + enzymatic casein hydrolysate (casa, 0.02%); M medium: malate (0.4%); Gmedium: glucose (0.4%); C medium: casa (0.02%).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Sampling preparation:</strong></p><p>For <strong>liquid cultures</strong>, cells were grown at 37 °C until an OD600 = 0.4 is reached, as described in <strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, a saturated overnight culture was diluted in a fresh medium at an OD600 = 0.05. In total 5 mL were transferred in a well of a 24 deep-well plate (Whatman; reference 7701-5110). The cells were further grown to an OD600 between 0.6 and 0.8 in a shaking incubator (Climo Shaker ISF1-X Kühner). The plate was then centrifuged at 2700 x g at 4 °C for 10 min and the supernatant removed.</p><p>For <strong>solid medium</strong>, cells were grown according to <strong>[2]</strong>. Briefly, a saturated overnight minimal media liquid culture was diluted in a fresh liquid media at an OD600 = 0.05, and further grown to an OD600 = 0.2. In total 5 mL of this culture were filtered onto a 47 mm PTFE filter (0.45 µm). The filter was then positioned with cells on top on an agarose plate containing the same minimal media. Cells were grown to log phase (OD600 +/- 0.8).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Thomas M, Stuani L, Darii E, Lechaplais C, Pateau E, Tabet JC, Salanoubat M, Saaidi PL, Perret A. De novo structure determination of 3-((3-aminopropyl)amino)-4-hydroxybenzoic acid, a novel and abundant metabolite in Acinetobacter baylyi ADP1. Metabolomics. 2019 Mar 14;15(3):45. doi:10.1007/s11306-019-1508-3. PMID:30874951.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Stuani L, Lechaplais C, Salminen AV, Ségurens B, Durot M, Castelli V, Pinet A, Labadie K, Cruveiller S, Weissenbach J, de Berardinis V, Salanoubat M, Perret A. Novel metabolic features in Acinetobacter baylyi ADP1 revealed by a multiomics approach. Metabolomics. 2014;10(6):1223-1238. doi:10.1007/s11306-014-0662-x. Epub 2014 Apr 29. PMID:25374488.</p>	<p><strong>Liquid cultures (Extraction 1):</strong></p><p>Metabolite extraction was adapted from the Metabolomics Service Protocols from the University of Glasgow<strong>[1]</strong>, as previously described<strong>[2]</strong>. Briefly, the cell pellet was resuspended in 200 μL of water:methanol:acetonitrile (1:3:1) and placed in a cold bath (-80 °C) composed of dry ice and ethanol. After cell freezing, the mixture was left at 25 °C to complete unfreezing of the cells. This procedure was repeated twice to completely break the cells. The metabolites were next rocked on a shaker for 1 h at 4 °C. The metabolites were centrifuged at 5000 x g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was dried and stored at -80 °C. Before LC-MS analysis, the metabolites were suspended with 20 μL water and 42 μL 80% acetonitrile and 20% 10 mM ammonium carbonate (pH 9.9), centrifuged at 5000 x g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was finally filtered on 0.22 μm (PTFE, Acroprep Advance, Pall).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Solid cultures (Extraction 2):</strong></p><p>Metabolomes were prepared according to previously published details<strong>[2]</strong>. Briefly, metabolism was quenched by placing the filter containing the cells face-down in a glass dish containing 5 mL of a cold (-30 °C) mixture of 80% acetonitrile and 20% methanol. After 15 min at -30 °C, the filter coated with cells was submitted to a mild sonication in an ultrasonic bath (Bronson 2510 Ultrasonic Cleaner) for 5 min at 4 °C to remove the cells from the filter. The quenching liquid containing the cells was transferred into a cryogenic vial and underwent 6 freeze/thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen per 65 °C water to fully break the cells and extract the metabolites. The samples were lyophilized and first dissolved in 300 µL water. The debris were removed by centrifugation (2000 x g, 4 °C, 10 min) and the supernatant was filtered on 0.22 µm (Millipore Millex-GV 13 mm). The filter was further washed with 700 µL 80:20 acetonitrile containing 10 mM ammonium carbonate (pH adjusted to 9.9 with NH4OH). The resulting 1 mL samples were stored at 4 °C and analyzed within 24 h.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Metabolomics Service Protocol Sample Preparation for Cells [Internet]. Glasgow Polyomics. Available from: http://www.polyomics.gla.ac.uk/assets/downloads/MSMetabolomicsPrepCells-Aug20 13.pdf</p><p><strong>[2] </strong>Thomas M, Stuani L, Darii E, Lechaplais C, Pateau E, Tabet JC, Salanoubat M, Saaidi PL, Perret A. De novo structure determination of 3-((3-aminopropyl)amino)-4-hydroxybenzoic acid, a novel and abundant metabolite in Acinetobacter baylyi ADP1. Metabolomics. 2019 Mar 14;15(3):45. doi:10.1007/s11306-019-1508-3. PMID:30874951.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Stuani L, Lechaplais C, Salminen AV, Ségurens B, Durot M, Castelli V, Pinet A, Labadie K, Cruveiller S, Weissenbach J, de Berardinis V, Salanoubat M, Perret A. Novel metabolic features in Acinetobacter baylyi ADP1 revealed by a multiomics approach. Metabolomics. 2014;10(6):1223-1238. doi:10.1007/s11306-014-0662-x. Epub 2014 Apr 29. PMID:25374488.</p>	<p><strong>HILIC (Chromatography 1):</strong></p><p>Separation for metabolome analysis was carried out using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 Rapid Separation LC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a ZIC-pHILIC column (150 x&nbsp;4.6&nbsp;mm; 5 μm; Merck) at 40 °C using a mobile phase gradient with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of 10 mM ammonium carbonate with pH adjusted to 9.9 with NH4OH and mobile phase B of acetonitrile. The gradient started at 80% B for 2 min, followed by a linear gradient at 40% B for 20 min and remained 8 min at 40% B. The system returned to the initial solvent composition in 5 min and re-equilibrated under these conditions for 15 min. The autosampler was kept at 4 °C and a total of 10 µL were injected.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>HDX (Chromatography 2):</strong></p><p>For hydrogen/deuterium exchange (HDX) experiments, deuterated (D2O) mobile phase was used. Elution was carried out using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 Rapid Separation LC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a ZIC-pHILIC column (100 x 2.1 mm; 5 μm; Merck) to minimize the use of heavy water. Column temperature was set to 15 °C using a mobile phase gradient with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of 10 mM ammonium carbonate in deuterated water and mobile phase B of acetonitrile. The gradient started at 80% B for 1 min, followed by a linear gradient to 40% B at 8 min and remained 4 min at 40% B. The system returned to the initial solvent composition in 3 min and re-equilibrated under these conditions for 15 min. A total of 2.5 μL were injected.</p>	<p>High-resolution measurements were obtained with a VelosPro Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) fitted with a heated electrospray ionization source operating in positive and negative ionization modes.&nbsp;In the positive ion mode, the ion spray (IS) was set to +3.5 kV and the capillary temperature at 275 °C. Sheath gas and auxiliary gas flow rates were set at 60 and 44 arbitrary units (a.u.), respectively. The S-lens RF level was set to 55. In the negative ion mode, the IS was set to -4.0 kV and the S-lens RF level to 60. Mass spectra were acquired over a m/z range from 50 to 1000 m/z with the mass resolution set to 60,000 FWHM at 400 m/z. Collision-induced dissociation spectra (CID) in resonant excitation conditions were acquired using data-dependent scanning function for identification purpose. Non-targeted CID experiments using data-dependent scan were conducted at 27% Normalized Collision Energy (NCE) in positive and negative modes. CID experiments to confirm the presence of Pse (Leg) in metabolome were conducted at 22% NCE in positive and negative modes.</p>	<p>LC/MS raw data were converted to <strong>mzXML</strong> using <strong>MSConvert</strong> 3.0.20338 and processed with <strong>R</strong> package <strong>XCMS</strong>.</p>	<p>An 'in house” modified <strong>KEGGREST</strong> <strong>R[1]</strong> function for automatic compound annotation was used.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Tenenbaum D, Maintainer B (2021). KEGGREST: Client-side REST access to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). R package version 1.34.0. doi:10.18129/B9.bioc.KEGGREST.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><em>Streptococcus suis</em> ATCC 700794 used in this study was purchased from the ATCC and stored in our laboratory. Unless otherwise stated, the bacteria were cultured in Todd-Hewitt Broth (THB) at 37 °C under aerated conditions. Strain ATCC 700794 was induced to have macrolide resistance levels by broth dilution under long-term selection pressure of tylosin i<em>n vitro</em><strong>[1]</strong>, named as resistant strain. All experiments were performed using bacteria in the exponential phase. We used the minimum inhibitory concentration to assess sensitivity to antibiotics. After collecting 1 x 10^7 bacterial cells, wash twice with ice cold PBS and centrifuge at 14,000 x g for 2 min at 4 °C retaining the precipitate.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Che RX, Xing XX, Liu X, Qu QW, Chen M, Yu F, Ma JX, Chen XR, Zhou YH, God'Spower BO, Liu JW, Lu ZX, Xu YP, Li YH. Analysis of multidrug resistance in Streptococcus suis ATCC 700794 under tylosin stress. Virulence. 2019 Dec;10(1):58-67. doi:10.1080/21505594.2018.1557505. PMID:31874073.</p>	<p>1 ml of cold extraction solvent methanol and acetonitrile and H2O (2;2;1) was added to 4 mg sample, and adequately vortexed. The lysate was sonicated at 4 °C for 1 h then centrifuged at 14,000 x g for 20 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was dried in a vacuum centrifuge at 4 °C. For LC-MS analysis, the samples were re-dissolved in 0.1 ml acetonitrile and water (1:1) solvent.</p>	<p>For metabolomic analysis, samples were precipitated and analyzed using UHPLC 1290 Infinity (Agilent Technologies USA) coupled to a AB TripleTOF 6600 quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved by a hydrophilic interaction chromatography on a ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm x 100 mm, 1.7 µm particle size (Waters, Ireland) using a gradient of solvent A (25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide in water) and solvent B(acetonitrile). The gradient was 85% B for 1 min and was linearly reduced to 65% in 11 min, and then was reduced to 40% in 0.1 min and kept for 4 min, and then increased to 85% in 0.1 min, with a 5 min re-equilibration period employed. Flow rate was 0.4 ml/min, column temperature was 25 °C, auto sampler temperature was 5 °C, and injection volume was 2 µl.</p>	<p>Extracts were analyzed using a AB TripleTOF 6600 quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer, equipped with an electrospray ionization source. The mass spectrometer was operated in negative ion mode. The ESI source conditions were set as follows: Ion Source Gas1 as 60, Ion Source Gas2 as 60, curtain gas as 30, source temperature: 600 °C, IonSpray Voltage Floating ±5500 V. In MS acquisition, the instrument was set to acquire over the m/z range 60-1000 Da, and the accumulation time for TOF MS scan was set at 0.20 s/spectra. In auto MS/MS acquisition, the instrument was set to acquire over the m/z range 25-1000 Da, and the accumulation time for product ion scan was set at 0.05 s/spectra. The product ion scan is acquired using information dependent acquisition with high sensitivity mode selected. The parameters were set as follows: the collision energy was fixed at 35 V with ±15 eV; declustering potential (DP), 60 V (+) and -60 V (-); exclude isotopes within 4 Da, candidate ions to monitor per cycle 10.</p>	<p>The <strong>raw</strong> MS data were converted into <strong>mzXML</strong> files using the <strong>ProteoWizard MSConvert</strong> tool and processed using <strong>XCMS</strong> for feature detection, retention time correction, and alignment. For peak picking, the following parameters were used: centWave m/z 25 ppm, peakwidth c (10, 60), prefilter c (10, 100). For peak grouping, bw 5, mzwid0.025, minfrac0.5 were used. In the extracted ion features, only the variables having more than 50% of the nonzero measurement values in at least one group were kept.</p>	<p>The metabolites were identified by accurate mass spectrometry (&lt; 25 ppm) and <strong>MS/MS</strong> spectra matched against an in-house standards database. After normalisation to total peak intensity, the processed data was imported into the web-based <strong>MetaboAnalyst</strong> (<a href='https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/</a>) system for multivariate statistical analysis, followed by <strong>Pareto scaling</strong>, <strong>PCA</strong> and <strong>OPLS-DA</strong>. The significantly different metabolites were determined based on the combination of a statistically significant threshold of <strong>VIP</strong> value obtained from the <strong>OPLS-DA</strong> model and 2-tailed <strong>Student’s t-test (P-value)</strong> on the raw data, and the metabolites with <strong>VIP</strong> values &gt;1.0 and <strong>P</strong> values &lt;0.05 were considered significant.</p><p><br></p><p>For <strong>KEGG</strong> pathway annotation, the metabolites were blasted against the online <strong>Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database</strong> to retrieve their COs and were subsequently mapped to pathways in <strong>KEGG</strong>. The corresponding <strong>KEGG</strong> pathways were extracted. To further explore the impact of differentially expressed metabolites, enrichment analysis was performed. <strong>KEGG</strong> pathway enrichment analyses were applied based on the Fisher exact test, considering the whole metabolites of each pathway as background dataset. And only pathways with p-values under a threshold of 0.05 were considered as significant changed pathways. For hierarchical clustering, <strong>Cluster 3.0</strong> and the <strong>Java Treeview software</strong> were used. Euclidean distance algorithm for similarity measure and average linkage clustering algorithm (clustering uses the centroids of the observations) for clustering were selected when performing hierarchical clustering. A heat map is often presented as a visual aid in addition to the dendrogram.</p>

Study Identifier	MTBLS479
Study Title	A Combined LC-MS Metabolomics- and 16S rRNA Sequencing Platform to Assess Interactions between Herbal Medicinal Products and Human Gut Bacteria in Vitro: a Pilot Study on Willow Bark Extract
Study Description	Herbal preparations are complex mixtures of natural products, many of which are able to reach the distal gut. There, they influence the microbial communities, and can be metabolized into bioactive metabolites. The aim of this study was to establish an interdisciplinary platform to unravel interactions of herbal medicine and intestinal microbiota, using a combined LC-MS metabolomics and 16S rRNA microbiome sequencing approach. Willow bark extract (WBE), a herbal medicinal product with well-established anti-inflammatory activity, was incubated with human fecal suspension under anoxic conditions. Samples were drawn after 0.5, 4 and 24 h of incubation.</p>Microbiome analyses revealed that incubation with WBE had a marked effect on microbial community composition and functions. In particular, the proportion of Bacterioides sp., generally regarded as beneficial for the gut ecosystem, was clearly enhanced in WBE-treated samples. LC-MS analysis showed that WBE constituents were readily metabolized by fecal bacteria. Numerous microbial metabolites could be annotated, allowing the construction of putative microbial degradation pathways for the main groups of WBE constituents.</p>We suggest that studies of this type help to increase the knowledge on bioactive principles of herbal medicine, since gut microbial metabolites have certainly been underestimated as a source of bioactive compounds in the past.

Study Protocol Description	A commercially available 70% ethanolic willow bark extract (final concentration 2 and 10 mg/ml) was incubated with 10% human fecal suspension under anoxic conditions at 37 °C (sample annotation: 10 mg/ml = WBE_conc1; 2 mg/ml=WBE_ conc2). Human fecal suspension incubated with vehicle instead of willow bark extract (sample annotation: HFS_vehicle) and willow bark extract (10 mg/ml) incubated with PBS buffer instead of human fecal suspension (sample annotation: WBE_conc1_PBS) served as controls. Samples were drawn after 0.5 hr (sample anntotation: T0), 4 hr (sample annotation: T4) and 24 hr (sample annotation: T24) of incubation. Incubation was done in triplicates (sample annotation: _1, _2, _3). 5% ethanol in PBS buffer was injected as LCMS blank (sample annotation: blank).	Samples were immediately ice-cooled and centrifuged (10 min, 13.000 rpm, 4 °C). The supernatant was sterile filtered (0.45 µm filter pore size) and frozen at -80 °C until analysis.	Analyses were performed on an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC (Thermo Scientific). As a stationary phase, a Kinetex C18 column (1.7 µm, 2.1 x 100 mm, Phenomenex) protected by a SecurityGuard UltraGuard cartridge (C18; 2.1 mm ID; Phenomenex) was used. The mobile phase consisted of water + 0.2% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile/methanol (7/3) + 0.2% formic acid. The gradient was set as follows: 0-2 min, 3% B in A; 3-36 min, 3-36% B in A; 36-46 min, 36-100% B in A; 46-52 min, 100% B in A; 52-53 min, 100-3% B in A; 53-63 min, 3% B in A. Flow rate was 0.2 ml/min and column temperature was 40 °C. Injection volume was 1 µl for samples containing willow bark extract at concentration 1 and 3 µl for samples containing willow bark exract at concentration 2. As a blank, 5% ethanol in PBS buffer was injected.	The mass spectrometer was run in the HESI negative mode using the following parameters: probe heater temperature 400 °C; capillary temperature 275 °C; spray voltage 2.5 kV, sheath gas flow 40 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas flow 12 arbitrary units; scan: m/z 100-1500; resolution: 70.000 (full MS) and 17.500 (data dependent MS2).	For data transformation, SIEVETM 2.2 (Thermo Scientific) was applied, using the Component Extraction algorithm. The resulting frame reports consisted of all features (i.e. m/z – retention time pairs) detected in the different samples and their respective peak areas. XLS spreadsheets containing frame reports have been added as supplementary information to the submitted manuscript.	From the processed data, those compounds were selected for identification that significantly increased or decreased over time and that were not contained in the fecal matrix samples, indicating that they were either WBE constituents or metabolites thereof. For identification, monoisotopic mass and isotope abundance were used to calculate the putative elemental formula. Comparison of elemental formula and ddMS2 fragmentation data with literature data and/or reference spectra in databases (Metlin, HMDB) allowed tentative compound identification (MSI level 2-3). Some compounds were unambiguously identified by comparison with authentic reference compounds (MSI level 1).

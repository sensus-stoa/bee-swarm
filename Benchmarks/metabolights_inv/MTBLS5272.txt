Study Identifier	MTBLS5272
Study Title	Coral symbiotic bacterial community and metabolites changes under thermal stress: A case of laboratory experiment with heating treatment
Study Description	Coral bleaching and coral reef degradation become severe as the surface seawater temperature rises. Much research to date has focused on the bacterial community composition properties within the coral holobiont, but less attention has been paid to the interactions of bacteria and corals under thermal stress. We investigated the changes of coral symbiotic bacteria and metabolites under thermal stress, and analyzed the internal relationship between bacteria and metabolites as well as their relationship with coral health. We found obvious signs of coral bleaching after heating treatment, and the interaction within symbiotic bacterial community became closer. The coral symbiotic bacterial community and metabolites changed significantly under thermal stress, and bacteria such as Flavobacterium, Shewanella and Psychrobacter increased significantly. Bacteria associated with stress tolerance, biofilm formation and mobile elements decreased, and bacterial DMSP metabolism increased slightly after heating treatment. Differential metabolites in corals after heating treatment were associated with cell cycle regulation and antioxidant. This study revealed the correlation between differential metabolites and bacterial community composition changes in corals under thermal stress, and providing valuable insight on metabolomics research of corals.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Here, we used <em>Acropora digitifera</em> obtained from Daya Bay, Shenzhen, China, in Oct 2020 as the model species. A total of 6 30 L aquariums were placed in a coral breeding room with an air temperature of 25 °C. In total 2 <em>A. digitifera</em> colonies were acclimatized in each aquarium for 2 months at a water temperature of 25 °C, and all the culture waters were circulated through the entire system. The original seawater in the tank was taken from collection site and was replaced with a constant water flow at a rate of ~1/4 tank per day. All the twelve coral colonies were splited from the same coral. At the beginning of the heating experiment, all the 6 aquariums were separated into individuals. Then 3 aquariums (Heating group) were heated to 32 °C using 100 W heaters (Atman, China) and the other 3 aquariums (Control group) were maintained at 25 °C.</p><p><br></p><p>One fragment (~3 g) from each coral colony in each aquarium was taken at the beginning (6 samples for each group) and end (Day 13, 6 samples for each group) of the expriment for a total of 24 samples were obtained. Fragments were freezed with liquid nitrogen, then ground with liquid nitrogen precooled sterile mortar. Liquid nitrogen were added into the mortar to maintain cryogenic condition during the grounding process. The coral powder was then transferred into a 2 mL liquid nitrogen precooled sterile eppendorf tube and stored in -80 °C for further use. LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted by Majorbio company.</p>	<p>The 50 mg coral powder collected above were added into a 2 mL Eppendorf tube filed with 400 µL methanol:water (4:1, v/v) solution. The mixture was placed at -20 °C and treated by high throughput tissue crusher Wonbio-96c (Shanghai Wonbio biotechnology co., LTD) at 50 Hz for 6 min, then vortexed for 30 s and ultrasonication at 40 kHz for 30 min at 4 °C. Samples were placed at -20 °C for 30 min to precipitate proteins. After centrifugation at 13,000 x g at 4 °C for 15 min, the supernatant was carefully transferred to sample vials for LC-MS analysis. Standardized samples for quality control (QC) were prepared by mixing equal volumes of all samples into a pooled sample and then analysed in the same method as the collected samples. The QC samples were injected at regular intervals (every 6 samples) to monitor the stability of the analysis. </p>	<p>The instrument platform for LC-MS analysis is the ultra-high performance liquid chromatography tandem time-of-flight mass spectrometry UPLC-TripleTOF system of AB SCIEX. Chromatographic conditions: 10 μL of sample was separated by HSS T3 column (100 mm x 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm) before entering mass spectrometry detection. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water:acetonitrile (95:5, v/v) (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile:isopropanol:water (47.5:47.5, v/v) (solvent B). The solvent gradient changed according to the following condition: from 0.0 to 0.5 min, from 0% to 0% B; from 0.5 to 2.5 min, from 0% to 25% B; from 2.5 to 9.0 min, from 25% to 100% B; from 9.0 to 13.0 min, from 100% to 100% B; from 13.0 to 13.1 min, from 100% to 0% B; from 13.1 to 16.0 min with sustaining 0% B for equilibrating the systems. The sample injection volume was 10 µL and the flow rate was set to 0.4 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. During the period of analysis, all these samples were stored at 4 °C.</p>	<p>The UPLC system was coupled to a quadrupole-time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TripleTOF 5600, AB Sciex, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in positive mode and negative mode. The optimal conditions were set as followed: source temperature at 550 °C; curtain gas (CUR) at 30 psi; both Ion Source Gas1 and Gas2 at 50 psi; ion-spray voltage floating (ISVF) at -4000 V in negative and 5000 V in positive mode, respectively; declustering potential at 80 V; collision energy (CE) at 20-60 eV rolling for MS/MS. Data acquisition was performed with the Information Dependent Acquisition (IDA) mode. The detection was carried out over a mass range of 50-1000 m/z.</p>	<p>The raw data were imported into the <strong>Progenesis QI</strong> software (v2.3, Nonlinear Dynamics, Waters, USA) for peak detection and alignment, generating a data matrix that consisted of the retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) and peak intensity. Metabolites detected in ≥ 80% of samples were retained, and then minimum metabolite values were set for these samples that below the limit of quantitation. Metabolites were normalized by summation. QC data were used as the internal standard, and metabolites with the relative standard deviation (RSD) &gt; 30% were discarded. Logarithmic transformation was performed to identify significant differences in metabolite levels between groups.</p>	<p>Metabolites were identified by comparing observed accurate mass and MS/MS spectra to accurate mass data and spectra available in online databases including <strong>Metlin</strong> Database and <strong>Human Metabolite Database</strong> (HMDB).</p>

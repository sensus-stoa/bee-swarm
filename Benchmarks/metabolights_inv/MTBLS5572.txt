Study Title	Gut microflora alleviate immune response during E.coli-induced bladder cystits via AhR/MANF activation
Study Description	<p>In recent years, with the deepening of research on intestinal microbiota, we found intestinal microbiota plays an important role in host immune regulation and barrier maintenance through cross-talk between the microbiota and the host. New evidence suggests that disruption of the fragile balance within the intestinal microbiota, termed as dysbiosis, may cause tremendous inflammation in both intestine proximal and distant organ. Our previous studies have shown that Bacterial infections of the liver and lungs are accompanied by changes in intestinal flora diversity, and the depletion of intestinal bacteria can aggravate bacterial infection. Though, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from a healthy population has already been associated with the reduction of urinary tract infections which caused by C. difficile. However, the mechanisms by which the intestinal microbiota affects the immune responses in bacterial cystitis (BC) remain poorly characterized.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Bacterial strains </strong></p><p><em>Escherichia coli</em> <strong>CFT073</strong> (ATCC 700928) was cultured in Luria broth (LB) at 37 °C overnight.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Animals</strong></p><p>7 to 8 wk old Female <strong>C57BL/6 mice</strong> were purchased from GemPharmatech Co. Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Mice were allowed to recover from the stress of transport in our institutional animal facility for 3 d before the experiments were carried out. All mice were housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF)-grade individually ventilated cages (IVCs) and maintained under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle at a temperature of 20-26 °C and relative humidity of 40-70%. The mice were provided food and sterilized water <em>ad libitum</em>. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering. The animal study was approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of the Anhui Medical University (approval no. LLSC20230023), and all animals in this study were treated according to the guidelines of Anhui Medical University and with the approval of the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Animal Usage of Anhui Medical University (Hefei, China).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Antibiotic-treatment</strong></p><p>The group of animals labelled as <strong>MGF</strong> were treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics (neomycin sulfate, 1 g/L; ampicillin, 1 g/L; vancomycin, 0.5 g/L; metronidazole, 1 g/L) dissolved in drinking water for 14 d. Antibiotic treatment was stopped 3 d prior <em>E. coli</em> infection.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Mouse models of<em> E. coli</em>-induced bacterial cystitis</strong></p><p><strong>C57BL/6 mice</strong> were used to create a previously described unobstructed ascending UTI mouse model, with slight modifications. The bacterial inoculum was prepared by overnight incubation at 37 °C in LB broth, followed by reinoculation in fresh LB broth. The bacterial cells were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, the pellets were dissolved in 10 mL of sterile PBS, and cell densities were adjusted to 5 x 10^7 colony-forming unit (CFU)&nbsp;bacterial cells per 25 μL for the prototypic UPEC, <strong>CFT073</strong>. After the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, a syringe covered with a sterile urinary catheter was slowly pushed into the urethra. Once the catheter was fully inserted, the syringe plunger was slowly depressed to deposit 50 μL of inoculum into the bladder. Mice were anesthetized 30 min after urethral inoculation with <strong>CFT073</strong>. Once fully able to regain their crawling ability, they were returned to their original cages. The mice were sacrificed on <strong>Day1</strong>, <strong>Day 3</strong>, and<strong> Day 28</strong> after infection.</p><p><br></p><p>Serum samples were collected from the mice after sacrifice, and stored at -80 °C immediately.&nbsp;</p>	<p><strong>LCMS:</strong></p><p>Samples stored at -80 °C were thawed at room temperature. 100 μL of sample was added to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube with 10 μL of <strong>L-2-chlorophenylalanine (0.3 mg/mL)</strong> dissolved in methanol as internal standard, and the tube was vortexed for 10 s. Subsequently, 300 μL of ice-cold mixture of methanol and acetonitrile (2/1, vol/vol) was added, the mixtures were vortexed for 1 min and the whole samples were extracted by ultrasonic for 10 min in ice-water bath and stored at -20 ℃ for 30 min. The extract was centrifuged at at 4 °C (13,000 rpm) for 10 min and 300 μL of supernatant was transferred to a glass vial and dried in a freeze concentration centrifugal dryer. Then, 300 μL mixture of methanol and water (1/4, vol/vol) was added to each sample. Samples were vortexed for 30 s, extracted by ultrasonic for 3 min in ice-water bath and then placed at -20 °C for 2 h. Samples were centrifuged at 4 °C (13,000 rpm) for 10 min. The supernatants (150 μL) from each tube were collected using glass syringes, filtered through 0.22 μm microfilters and transferred to LC vials. The vials were stored at -80 °C until LC-MS/MS analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GCMS:</strong></p><p>Samples stored at -80 °C were thawed at room temperature. 100 μL of sample was added to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube with 10 μL of <strong>L-2-chlorophenylalanine</strong> (0.3 mg/mL) dissolved in methanol as internal standard, and the tube was vortexed for 10 s. Subsequently, 300 μL of ice-cold mixture of methanol and acetonitrile (2/1, vol/vol) was added, and the mixtures were vortexed for 1 min, and the whole samples were extracted by ultrasonic for 10 min in ice-water bath, stored at -20 °C for 30 min. The extract was centrifuged at at 4 °C (13,000 rpm) for 10 min, 150 μL of supernatant was transferred to a glass sampling vial for vacuum-dry at room temperature and 80 μL of 15 mg/mL <strong>methoxylamine hydrochloride in pyridine</strong> was subsequently added. The resultant mixture was vortexed vigorously for 2 min and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. Then, 50 μL of BSTFA (with 1% TMCS) and 20 μL of n-hexane were added into the mixture, which was vortexed vigorously for 2 min. The samples were then derivatized at 70 °C for 60 min, before leaving them at ambient temperature for 30 min before GC-MS analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>QC samples for both analytical platforms were prepared by pooling 100 μl of each sample.</p>	<p><strong>LCMS:</strong></p><p>A <strong>Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC</strong> and an <strong>ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) </strong>(Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were employed in both positive and negative modes. The binary gradient elution system consisted of (A) water (containing 0.1 % formic acid, v/v) and (B) acetonitrile (containing 0.1 % formic acid, v/v) and separation was achieved using the following gradient: 0 min, 5% B; 2 min, 5% B; 4 min, 30% B; 8 min, 50% B; 10 min, 80% B; 14 min, 100% B; 15 min, 100% B; 15.1 min, 5% B and 16 min, 5%B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min and column temperature was 45 °C. All the samples were kept at 4 °C during the analysis and the injection volume was 2 μL. The QCs were injected at regular intervals throughout the analytical run to provide a set of data from which repeatability can be assessed.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GCMS:</strong></p><p>The derivatived samples were analyzed on a&nbsp;<strong>Trace1310 gas chromatography system </strong>(Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A <strong>DB-5MS fused-silica capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent J &amp; W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA)</strong> was utilized to separate the&nbsp;derivatives. Helium (&gt; 99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL/min through the column. The injector temperature was maintained at 300 °C. Injection volume was 1 μL by splitless mode. The initial oven temperature was 60 °C, and held at 60 °C for 0.5 min, ramped to 125 °C at a rate of 8 °C/min, to 210 °C at a rate of 8 °C/min, to 270 °C at a rate of 15 °C/min, to 305 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min, and finally held at 305 °C for 5 min.</p>	<p><strong>LCMS:</strong></p><p>A <strong>Q-Exactive plus quadrupole-Orbitrap</strong> mass spectrometer equipped with heated electrospray ionization (<strong>ESI</strong>) source (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to analyze the metabolic profiling in both ESI positive and ESI negative ion modes. The <strong>mass range</strong> was <strong>m/z 100-1000</strong>. The resolution was set at 70.000 for the full MS scans and 17.500 for HCD MS/MS scans. The Collision energy was set at 10, 20 and 40 eV. The mass spectrometer operated as follows: spray voltage, 3.800 V (+) and 3.000 V (−); sheath gas flow rate, 35 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas flow rate, 8 arbitrary units; capillary temperature, 320 °C; Aux gas heater temperature, 350 °C; S-lens RF level, 50.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GCMS:</strong></p><p>The derivatized samples were analyzed on a&nbsp;<strong>TSQ9000 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer </strong>with an electron impact (<strong>EI</strong>) source (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The temperature of the mass spectrometer and EI ion source was set to 280 °C and 330 °C, respectively. Mass spectrometric data was acquired in a full-scan mode (<strong>m/z 50-500</strong>), and the solvent delay time was set to 5 min.</p>	<p><strong>LCMS:</strong></p><p>The original LC-MS data were processed by software <strong>Progenesis QI V2.3</strong> (Nonlinear, Dynamics, Newcastle, UK) for baseline, integral, filtering, retention time correction, normalization, peak identification and peak alignment.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GCMS:</strong></p><p>The obtained GC-MS/MS raw data in .<strong>d</strong> format were transferred to <strong>.abf </strong>format via software <strong>Analysis Base File Converter</strong> for quick retrieval of data. Then, data were imported into software <strong>MS-DIAL</strong>, which performs peak detection, peak identification, <strong>MS2Dec</strong> deconvolution, characterization, peak alignment, wave filtering, and missing value interpolation.</p>	<p><strong>LCMS:</strong></p><p>Compound identification were based on secondary fragments, precise mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and isotopic distribution using The Human Metabolome Database (<strong>HMDB</strong>), <strong>Lipidmaps</strong>, <strong>Metlin</strong>, and self-built databases to do qualitative analysis. Variable Importance of Projection (<strong>VIP</strong>) values obtained from the Orthogonal Partial Least-Squares-Discriminant Analysis (<strong>OPLS-DA) </strong>model were used to rank the overall contribution of each variable to group discrimination. A 2-tailed Student’s T-test was further used to verify whether the metabolites significantly changed between groups. Differential metabolites were selected when VIP values were＞1 and p-values were &lt; 0.05.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>GCMS:</strong></p><p>Metabolite characterization is based on <strong>LUG database</strong>. A data matrix was derived. The three-dimensional matrix includes: sample information, the name of the peak of each substance, retention time, retention index, mass-to-charge ratio, and signal intensity. In each sample, all peak signal intensities were segmented and normalized according to the internal standards with RSD greater than 0.3 after screening. After the data was normalized, redundancy removal and peak merging were conducted to obtain the data matrix.</p><p><br></p><p>The matrix was imported in<strong> R</strong> to carry out Principle Component Analysis (PCA) to observe the overall distribution among the samples and the stability of the whole analysis process. <strong>OPLS-DA </strong>and Partial Least-Squares-Discriminant Analysis (<strong>PLS-DA</strong>) were utilized to distinguish the metabolites that differ between groups. To prevent overfitting, 7-fold cross-validation and 200 Response Permutation Testing (<strong>RPT</strong>) were used to evaluate the quality of the model. <strong>VIP</strong> values obtained from the <strong>OPLS-DA</strong> model were used to rank the overall contribution of each variable to group discrimination. A 2-tailed Student’s T-test was further used to verify whether the metabolites of difference between groups were significant. Differential metabolites were selected with VIP values greater than 1.0 and p-values less than 0.05.</p>

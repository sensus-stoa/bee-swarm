Study Identifier	MTBLS5583
Study Title	Lung Lipidomic Alterations in Beagle Dogs Infected with Toxocara canis
Study Description	<p>Toxocariasis, mainly caused by <em>Toxocara canis</em>, and to a lesser extent, <em>Toxocara cati</em>, is a neglected parasitic zoonosis. The mechanisms that underlie the changes in lipid metabolism of <em>T. canis</em> infection in Beagle dogs' lungs remain unclear. Lipidomics is a rapidly emerging approach that enables the global profiling of lipid composition by mass spectrometry. In this study, we performed a non-targeted lipidomic analysis of the lungs of Beagle dogs infected with the roundworm <em>T. canis</em> using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). A total of 1197 lipid species were identified, of which 63, 88 and 157 lipid species were significantly altered at 24 h post-infection (hpi), 96 hpi and 36 days post-infection (dpi), respectively. This global lipidomic profiling identified infection-specific lipid signatures for lung toxocariasis, and represented a comprehensive comparison between the lipid composition of dogs' lungs in the presence and absence of <em>T. canis</em> infection. The potential roles of the identified lipid species in the pathogenesis of <em>T. canis</em> are discussed, which has important implications for better understanding the interaction mechanism between <em>T. canis</em> and the host lung.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The experimental procedures of the study were reviewed and approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Approval No. 2018-015). The Beagle dogs used in this study were handled in accordance with good animal practice as defined by the relevant Animal Ethics Procedures and Guidelines of the People's Republic of China.</p><p><br></p><p>In this study, 6 to 7 weeks-old, specific pathogen free (SPF) Beagles dogs were used according to our previous description<strong>[1]</strong>. In total 46 puppies were divided into 3 groups according to the stage of <em>T. canis</em> migration in the canine host: 24 h group (n = 14, 7 infected puppies vs 7 control puppies), 96 h group (n = 14, 8 infected puppies vs 6 control puppies) and 36 days group (n = 18, 9 infected puppies vs 9 control puppies). Puppies from the same litter were randomly allocated to infected groups (I) and control groups (C) to minimize background differences. Puppies in the infected group were infected with 1 mL of normal saline containing 300 embryonated <em>T. canis</em> eggs, and puppies in the control group were infected with an equal amount of normal saline only. The lung samples from all puppies were collected at 24 hpi, 96 hpi and 36 dpi as previously described<strong>[2]</strong> and stored at -80 °C.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Zou Y, Zheng WB, He JJ, Elsheikha HM, Zhu XQ, Lu YX. Toxocara canis Differentially Affects Hepatic MicroRNA Expression in Beagle Dogs at Different Stages of Infection. Front Vet Sci. 2020 Nov 12;7:587273. doi:10.3389/fvets.2020.587273. PMID:33282932.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Zheng WB, Zou Y, He JJ, Elsheikha HM, Liu GH, Hu MH, Wang SL, Zhu XQ. Global profiling of lncRNAs-miRNAs-mRNAs reveals differential expression of coding genes and non-coding RNAs in the lung of beagle dogs at different stages of Toxocara canis infection. Int J Parasitol. 2021 Jan;51(1):49-61. doi:10.1016/j.ijpara.2020.07.014. Epub 2020 Sep 28. PMID:32991917.</p>	<p>In total 25 mg lung sample was placed into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube containing 2 small steel beads. Then, 600 μL dichloromethane, 200 μL methanol and 10 μL of SPLASH internal standards solution (330707, Avanti Polar Lipids, USA) were added into Eppendorf tube, followed by grinding using a tissue grinder (50 Hz, 5 min). Then, the ground lung sample was further processed by ultrasonic treatment with water bath at 4 °C for 10 min and was cooled at -20 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 600 μL of supernatant was taken and lyophilized after being centrifuged at 25,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by reconstituting with 100 μL isopropanol, 50 μL acetonitrile and 50 μL water. The sample was shaken for 1 min, followed by ultrasonic treatment and centrifuged as above. Then, the supernatant was transferred to 1.5 mL vial. Quality control (QC) sample were prepared by pooling 20 μL of each sample to evaluate the stability and repeatability of LC-MS analysis.</p>	<p>In this study, the separation and detection of lipids in each lung sample and QC sample were performed by using Waters 2D UPLC (Waters, USA) and Q-Exactive high resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). CSH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 x 100 mm, Waters, USA) was used in this study. In the positive ion mode, the mobile phase consisted of solvent A (60% acetonitrile aqueous solution (ACN), 0.1% formic acid (FA) and 10 mM ammonium formate (AF)) and solvent B (10% ACN, 90% isopropanol, 0.1% FA and 10 mM AF). In the negative ion mode, the mobile phase consisted of solvent A (60% ACN and 10 mM AF) and solvent B (10% ACN, 90% isopropanol and 10 mM AF). The gradient elution conditions were set using mobile phase B as follows: 40-43% for 0.0-2.0 min, 43-50% for 2.0-2.1 min, 50-54% for 2.1-7.0 min, 54-70% for 7.0-7.1 min, 70-99% for 7.1-13.0 min, 99-40% for 13.0-13.1 min, 40% for 13.1-15.0 min with a constant flow 0.35 μL/min at 55 °C.</p>	<p>The lipids separated by liquid phase were injected into the Q-Exactive mass spectrometer to obtain MS1 and MS2 data with the range of 200-2000 m/z. In the MS1 analysis, MS scan settings were: resolution, 70,000; maximum injection time (MIT), 100 ms; automatic gain control (AGC), 3e^6. For subsequent MS2 analysis (resolution, 17,500; MIT, 50 ms; AGC, 1e^5), top 3 ions were selected according to the precursor ion intensity. Collision energy (stepped a normalized collisional energy) were set as: 15, 30 and 45 eV.</p>	<p>LC-MS/MS data analysis was performed by <strong>LipidSearch</strong> v.4.1, including intelligent peak extraction, lipid identification and peak alignment, and subsequent data analysis. Statistical analysis was performed through the metabolomics <strong>metaX</strong> <strong>R</strong> package<strong>[1]</strong>, including deletion of lipid molecules missing more than 50% in QC samples and more than 80% in experimental samples, normalization of the data by using the probability quotient normalization (PQN) method and deletion of the lipid molecules with a relative peak area CV (Coefficient of Variation) &gt; 30% in all QC samples. Data quality was evaluated by detecting the reproducibility of QC samples, including chromatograms overlap of QC samples, principal component analysis (PCA), peak number, and peak response intensity difference. Quantitative analysis of lipid subclasses was detected for relative content in the infected and control groups. The PCA and partial least squares method-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were used for the multivariate statistical analysis. Fold change (FC) and t-test analysis was used for univariate analysis. Screening conditions for differential lipid molecules were VIP ≥ 1, FC ≥1.2 or ≤ 0.8333 and p value &lt; 0.05. Additionally, the differential lipid molecules were clustered using the hierarchical clustering method.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Wen B, Mei Z, Zeng C, Liu S. metaX: a flexible and comprehensive software for processing metabolomics data. BMC Bioinformatics. 2017 Mar 21;18(1):183. doi:10.1186/s12859-017-1579-y. PMID:28327092.</p>	<p>LC-MS/MS data analysis was performed by <strong>LipidSearch</strong> v.4.1, including intelligent peak extraction, lipid identification and peak alignment, and subsequent data analysis. The identified lipids were functionally annotated through the <strong>Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes</strong> (<strong>KEGG</strong>; <a href='https://www.genome.jp/kegg/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.genome.jp/kegg/</a>) database to understand the functional properties of differential lipids and signal transduction pathways. The conversion of name between the lipid compounds acquired by LC-MS/MS and the <strong>KEGG</strong> ID in the <strong>KEGG</strong> database was conducted by <strong>MetaboAnalyst</strong> v.5.0 (<a href='https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/MetaboAnalyst/upload/ConvertView.xhtml' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/MetaboAnalyst/upload/ConvertView.xhtml</a>) prior to the <strong>KEGG</strong> enrichment analysis<strong>[1]</strong>. Because multiple lipids with a specific function may correspond to the same <strong>KEGG</strong> ID, the conversion results were manually selected to march the correct <strong>KEGG</strong> ID and to determine the effect of altered lipids on the metabolic pathways.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Pang Z, Chong J, Zhou G, de Lima Morais DA, Chang L, Barrette M, Gauthier C, Jacques PÉ, Li S, Xia J. MetaboAnalyst 5.0: narrowing the gap between raw spectra and functional insights. Nucleic Acids Res. 2021 Jul 2;49(W1):W388-W396. doi:10.1093/nar/gkab382. PMID:34019663.</p>

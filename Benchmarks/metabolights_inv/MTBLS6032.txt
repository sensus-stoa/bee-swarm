Study Identifier	MTBLS6032
Study Title	Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses reveal the differential accumulation of phenylpropanoids and terpenoids in hemp autotetraploid and its diploid progenitor
Study Description	<h4>Background</h4>Cannabis sativa, a dioecious plant that has been cultivated worldwide for thousands of years, is known for its secondary metabolites, especially cannabinoids, which possess several medicinal effects. In this study, we investigated the autopolyploidization effects on the biosynthesis and accumulation of these metabolites, transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were performed to explore the gene expression and metabolic variations in industrial hemp autotetraploids and their diploid progenitors.<h4>Results</h4>Through these analyses, we obtained 1,663 differentially expressed metabolites and 1,103 differentially expressed genes. Integrative analysis revealed that phenylpropanoid and terpenoid biosynthesis were regulated by polyploidization. No substantial differences were found in the cannabidiol or tetrahydrocannabinol content between tetraploids and diploids. Following polyploidization, some transcription factors, including nine bHLH and eight MYB transcription factors, affected the metabolic biosynthesis as regulators. Additionally, several pivotal catalytic genes, such as flavonol synthase/flavanone 3-hydroxylase, related to the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, were identified as being modulated by polyploidization.<h4>Conclusions</h4>This study enhances the overall understanding of the impact of autopolyploidization in C. sativa and the findings may encourage the application of polyploid breeding for increasing the content of important secondary metabolites in industrial hemp.

Study Protocol Description	<p>This study took place in a laboratory setting, with the aim of determining the mechanisms of polyploidization-induced changes in cannabis plants. The industrial hemp variety <strong>DMG265</strong> (2n = 2x 274 = 20) used in this study was donated by the National Germplasm Bank of the Institute of Bast Fiber Crops, Chinese Academy of Agriculture Sciences. <strong>DMG265</strong> is a dioecious CBD medicinal variety and is commonly used as a diploid donor. The autotetraploid hemp material was induced using colchicine as described previously<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, the seeds of <strong>DMG265</strong> were planted in pots and incubated under indoor white fluorescent lighting (light duration, 18 h; average light intensity, 100 µmol/(m^2 x s) ; temperature, 24 ± 2 °C). After the emergence of 2 true leaves, the apical growing points of the seedlings were treated with 0.2% (w/v, pH 6.0) colchicine solution every 6 h for 24 h. After 50 d, the young leaves at the top of the plant were subjected to flow cytometric analysis to detect the DNA content and ploidy. The ploidy of the tetraploids of different genotypes and their clones was assessed 3 times, and the results showed that their ploidy was stable (see <strong>Fig. S4B</strong> in the paper associated with the study). Healthy <strong>diploid (Cs2x)</strong> and <strong>tetraploid (Cs4x)</strong> female plants were selected as mother plants for clonal propagation. 20 cuttings from the mother plant in the vegetative stage were placed in a Jiffy seeding block for rooting and covered with a transparent plastic dome to maintain high humidity. The cuttings were fully rooted approximately 3 wk later. In the fifth wk, 8 vigorous and uniform clones of diploid and tetraploid genotypes were transferred into pots. Plants were maintained in the vegetative growth phase (light duration, 18 h; average light intensity, 200 µmol/(m^2 x s) ) for 4 wk and then switched to reproductive growth (light duration, 12 h; average light intensity, 450 µmol/(m^2 x s)). After 9 wk, the female flowers of each clone were separately sampled and frozen in liquid nitrogen for transcriptome and metabolome analyses.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Bagheri M, Mansouri H. Effect of induced polyploidy on some biochemical parameters in Cannabis sativa L. Appl Biochem Biotechnol. 2015 Mar;175(5):2366-75. doi: 10.1007/s12010-014-1435-8. Epub 2014 Dec 10. PMID: 25492688.</p>	<p>3 inflorescences (approximately 15 cm) from each plant were mixed as a biological replicate (8 replicates or each of the different ploidy cannabis genotypes) and dried overnight at 35 °C, followed by the removal of stems and debris. Thereafter, the samples were ground, sieved through a &lt; 0.25 mm filter, extracted in a glass tube using 4 mL of methanol per 200 mg of milled sample, and ultrasonicated at 30 °C for 10 min as as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. After standing for 1 h, the mixture was centrifuged at 2920 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected and diluted to 10 mL with methanol. The supernatant was filtered with a 0.45 μM filter and analyzed using LC-MS/MS.</p><p><br></p><p>The quality control (<strong>QC</strong>) samples were prepared by mixing the extracts of all samples in equal volume.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Xiong QQ, Shen TH, Zhong L, Zhu CL, Peng XS, He XP, Fu JR, Ouyang LJ, Bian JM, Hu LF, Sun XT, Xu J, Zhou HY, He HH, Chen XR. Comprehensive metabolomic, proteomic and physiological analyses of grain yield reduction in rice under abrupt drought-flood alternation stress. Physiol Plant. 2019 Dec;167(4):564-584. doi: 10.1111/ppl.12901. Epub 2019 Feb 9. PMID: 30561011.</p>	<p>An<strong> ACQUITY UHPLC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA)</strong> and an <strong>ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm x 100 mm, Waters)</strong> were employed. The binary gradient elution system consisted of (A) water (containing 0.1% formic acid, v/v) and (B) acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid, v/v) and separation was achieved using the following gradient: 0 min, 5% B; 2 min, 5% B; 4 min, 30% B; 8 min, 50% B; 10 min, 80% B; 14 min, 100% B; 15 min, 100% B; 15.1 min, 5% and 16 min, 5% B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min and the column temperature was 45 °C. All the samples were kept at 4 °C during the analysis. The injection volume was 2 μL. The <strong>QCs</strong> were injected at regular intervals throughout the analytical run to provide a set of data from which repeatability can be assessed.</p>	<p>An <strong>AB Sciex Triple TOF 5600 System (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA) </strong>was used to analyze the metabolic profiles in both ESI-positive (+) and ESI-negative (−) ion modes. The mass range was from <strong>100 to 1200 m/z</strong>. The resolution was set at 70,000 for the full MS scans and 17,500 for HCD MS/MS scans , in both (+) and (−) ion modes. The mass spectrometer operated as follows: spray voltage, 3800 V (+) and 3000 V (−); sheath gas flow rate, 40 arbitrary units (+), 35 arbitrary units (−); auxiliary gas flow rate, 10 arbitrary units (+), 8 arbitrary units (−) and capillary temperature, 320 °C in both (+) and (−) modes.</p>	<p>The original LC-MS/MS data were processed by software <strong>Progenesis QI v2.3</strong> (Nonlinear, Dynamics, Newcastle, UK) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integral, retention time correction, peak alignment and normalization.</p>	<p>Compound identification were based on precise mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), secondary fragments and isotopic distribution using The Human Metabolome Database (<a href='https://hmdb.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>HMDB</strong></a>), <a href='https://www.lipidmaps.org/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>Lipidmaps </strong></a><strong>(v2.3)</strong>, <a href='https://metlin.scripps.edu/landing_page.php?pgcontent=mainPage' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>Metlin</strong></a> and self-built databases to do qualitative analysis.</p>

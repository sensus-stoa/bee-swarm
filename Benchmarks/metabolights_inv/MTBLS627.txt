Study Identifier	MTBLS627
Study Title	Metabolic Changes of Maternal Uterine Fluid, Uterus, and Plasma during the Peri-implantation Period of Early Pregnancy in Mice
Study Description	Embryo implantation is a complex process which involves biochemical and physiological interactions between an implantation-competent blastocyst and a receptive uterus. However, the exact biochemical changes of uterine fluid, uterus, and plasma during peri-implantation remain unclear. This study aims to characterize the biochemical and metabolic changes that occur during the peri-implantation period of early pregnancy, using mice as an animal model. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry was used to analyze the metabolite profiles of the uterus, uterine fluid, and maternal plasma at pre-implantation and implantation. The multivariate analyses, ANOVA and Tukey's HSD test, were applied to detect significant changes in metabolites and metabolic pathways. The metabolic networks were reconstructed in silico based on the identified metabolites and KEGG metabolic framework. Between pre-implantation day 1 and day 4, dramatic metabolic changes were observed in the uterine fluid that could be important for blastocyst development and protection against the harsh uterine environment. Palmitoleic acid, fumaric acid, and glutaric acid changed levels at day 4 in the uterus, suggesting that they may be associated with endometrial receptivity. Both the uterus and maternal plasma showed profound changes in cellular metabolism at the early implantation period, including upregulation of branched-chain amino acids and intermediates of one-carbon metabolism, an upregulation of glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, and downregulation of aerobic respiration; all of which could be involved in the regulation of the maternal-fetal interface, alternative nutrient utilization, and energy preservation for implantation as well as later placentation and fetal development to ensure successful embryo implantation.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Mature mice (Kunming White outbred strain, 6–8 weeks old, weight 25–30 g) were housed in a temperature-controlled room (22 °C) with a 12-h light cycle. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University. Adult females were mated overnight with fertile or vasectomized males of the same strain to induce pregnancy or pseudopregnancy, respectively (vaginal plug = day 1 (D1) of pregnancy).</p><p><br></p><p>Uterus and plasma samples were collected at D1, D4, and D5 from mice with normal pregnancy as well as mice with pseudopregnancy at day 5 (P-D5). The diagrammatic illustration on the time of different sample collections and the number of mice and samples collected in each group are shown in Fig 1 of the paper associated with this study.</p><p><br></p><p>After embryo implantation, IS (Implantation site) and IIS (Inter-implantation site) were visualized by an intravenous injection of 0.4% Trypan blue (Sigma, America, 0.2 ml/mouse). 4 to 6 mice were used in each stage or treatment in this study. For the induced decidualization model, sesame oil (10 μl) was infused intraluminally into one uterine horn at day 4 of pseudopregnancy (P-D4) to induce decidualization (Dec samples), and mice were sacrificed at D8. The contralateral horn served as a control (unDec samples).</p>	<p>The uterus was prepared by adding 20 mg of uterine tissue into new tubes, followed by the addition of 20 μl of 2,3,3,3-d4-alanine (10 Mm). After adding 500 μl of cold methanol-water (50% v/v), tissues were homogenized and centrifuged (17,000 x g, 15 min) to collect the supernatant. In parallel, 120 μl of plasma was transferred into new sterile polypropylene tubes and 20 μl 2,3,3,3-d4-alanine (10 Mm; Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) was added as an internal standard. Proteins were precipitated by adding 400 μl cool methanol, vortexing for 1 min, and then incubating at -20 °C for 30 min. The plasma supernatant was collected by centrifugation (17,000 x g, 15 min). Both uterus and plasma supernatants were dried via speedVac (Labconco, Kansas, USA) and stored at -20 °C, prior to derivatization.</p>	<p>The boiling points of extracted metabolites were lowered using a methyl chloroformate (MCF) derivatization approach based on a protocol published previously<strong>[1]</strong>. In brief, 200 μl of sodium hydroxide (1 M) was added to the SpeedVac-dried samples. 167 μl of methanol and 34 μl of pyridine were added as the methyl group donor and catalyst. The reaction started by adding 20 μl MCF followed by 30 s of vortexing and subsequently adding another 20 μl of MCF followed by 30 s of vortexing. To isolate derivatized metabolites from the reactive mixture, 400 μl of chloroform and 400 μl of sodium bicarbonate (50 mM) were added and vortexed for 10 s respectively. The lower chloroform phase was isolated and excess water was removed by adding anhydrous sodium sulfate.</p><p><br></p><p>The derivatized samples were analyzed in a 7890 GC system coupled to a 5975 MSD mass selective detector (Agilent, CA, USA) emitting electrons at 70 eV. The gas column implemented to separate non-polar compounds was a ZB-1701 GC capillary column (30 m x 250 μm id x 0.15 μm with 5-m guard column, Phenomenex, CA, USA). The gas chromatography oven was operated according to a previously published protocol<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, the chloroform phase of the derivatized sample was injected into the GC inlet at 290 °C with the pulsed splitless mode. Helium gas was used as a carrier gas flow through the GC column at 1 ml/min.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Smart KF, Aggio RB, Van Houtte JR, Villas-Bôas SG. Analytical platform for metabolome analysis of microbial cells using methyl chloroformate derivatization followed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. Nat Protoc. 2010 Sep;5(10):1709-29. doi:10.1038/nprot.2010.108. PMID:20885382</p>	<p>Mass spectra were collected on an Agilent 5975 MSD emitting electrons at 70 eV. Mass spectrometry parameters were operated according to a previously published protocol<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, the auxiliary temperature, MS quadrupole, and MS source were set to 250 °C, 230 °C, and 150 °C respectively. The detected mass range was set from 30 to 550 au, scan speed was set to 1.562 scans/s, and the solvent delay was set to 5.5 min.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Smart KF, Aggio RB, Van Houtte JR, Villas-Bôas SG. Analytical platform for metabolome analysis of microbial cells using methyl chloroformate derivatization followed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. Nat Protoc. 2010 Sep;5(10):1709-29. doi:10.1038/nprot.2010.108. PMID:20885382</p>	<p>The metabolite values were normalized by the abundance of the internal standard (2,3,3,3-d4-alanine) and total ion count, to correct for technical variability. The abundances of identified compounds were adjusted to Gaussian distribution via log transformation, prior to statistical analysis.</p>	<p>Compound deconvolution and identification were performed by automated mass spectral deconvolution and identification system (AMDIS) software using our internal methyl chloroformate (MCF) derivatization mass spectra library of metabolite standards. The compounds were identified based on 2 criteria; &gt;85% match to our library spectra and within a 1-min bin of the respective chromatographic retention time. The relative abundance of the metabolites was extracted by our in-house MassOmics software using peak height of the highest reference ion mass.</p>

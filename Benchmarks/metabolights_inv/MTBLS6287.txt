Study Identifier	MTBLS6287
Study Title	Isotope Labeled Reactivity-Based Metabolomics Reveal Cysteine as a Potent In Vivo Scavenger of Lactoylglutathione
Study Description	<p>Metabolite adducts are increasingly being recognized as important intermediates in cellular metabolism and as circulating messengers. Methylglyoxal (MG) is a highly reactive metabolite involved in diabetes and aging, via formation of a wide variety of protein adducts. Though new types of protein adducts with biological function continuously appear, little is known regarding the potentially more diverse MG-metabolite adductome. To address this, we here devise a “symmetric” isotopic labeling and reactivity-based metabolomics approach. We find more than 100 direct MG- as well as lactoylglutathione (LGSH)-derived adducts and among them characterize 10 as D- and L-lactoylated amino acids. We find cysteine to generate mainly L-Lac-Cys from MG, whereas the diasteromer , D-lactoyl-cysteine, mainly originate via a rapid reaction with LGSH with rates on par with click-reactions. The reaction salvages glutathione and also scavenges/regulate prote in cysteine lactoylation, in aggregate assigning cysteine with both glyoxalase 1 and 2 like function. &nbsp;Several of the adducts are circulating human metabolites, as well as D-Lac-Cys, L-Lac-Cys and D-Lac-Phe are increased in the urine of diabetic mice and may serve as a novel type of biomarker for metabolic diseases.&nbsp;</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Cell culture:</strong></p><p><strong>GLO2KO</strong> and paternal <strong>wild-type HEK293</strong> were cultured in low-glucose (1 g/L) Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Cell Sample Preparation:</strong></p><p>In total 1 x 10^6 cells were plated in 60 mm cell culture dishes (growth area 22.2 cm^2) and incubated until 90% confluent. At 90% confluency, the cell media was removed, and cells were treated with the designated compounds; <strong>Methylglyoxal (MG)</strong> (1 mM) or <strong>isotope labeled Methylglyoxal (13C3-MG)</strong> (1 mM) and incubated for 6 h.</p>	<p>The cell media was removed, and the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) before quenching with 1000 µl ice-cold methanol (MeOH)/H2O (80:20 v/v) and stored on dry ice. Cells were harvested through scraping and extraction was repeated twice by adding MeOH/H2O to the culture dish. All fractions were pooled in glass tubes and stored on dry ice. The extraction suspension was shaken at room temperature for 5 min, followed by centrifugation (2000 x g, 4 °C, 10 min). Supernatants were collected in glass tubes and kept on dry ice. Cell pellets were extracted one addition time in 1000 µl MeOH/H2O (80:20 v/v). Supernatants were pooled and dried by vacuum centrifugation (SpeedVac, 40 °C). The dried metabolome samples were resuspended in 33 µl NH4HCO3 (100 mM) under sonication. The samples were reduced with 5 µl 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at room temperature for 20 min Subsequently, samples were acidified with 2 µl formic acid (FA; 2.5% v/v) and diluted with H2O to achieve a sample volume of 50 µl. Afterward, all samples were centrifuged (10,000 x g, 5 min), supernatants transferred to MS-vials, and stored at -20 °C until further analysis. For each experiment, a quality control (QC) sample was obtained by pooling an aliquot of each metabolomics sample. A dilution series of the QC was obtained in water, 0.1% FA (v/v) (1:2, 1:5, 1:10, 1:20) to obtain the optimal sample concentration. The metabolomics samples were diluted accordingly in water, 0.1% (v/v) FA.</p>	<p>Untargeted metabolomics analyses were performed on a UPLC-HR-TOF-MS system with an ACQUITY I-Class UPLC (Waters Cooperation, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 mm x 100 mm, 1.8 µm, Waters) and coupled to a Bruker maXis Impact QTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). The mobile phase consisted of H20, 0.1% FA (v/v) (A) and a mixture of MeOH/ACN (1:1 v/v), 0.1% FA (v/v) (B). The flow rate was set to 0.4 mL/min at a column temperature of 50 °C with the following 21 min gradient: 0% B (0-2 min), 0-40% B (2-6 min), 40-60% B (6-6.5 min), 60-88% B (6.5-11 min), 88-100% (11-11.5min), 100% B (11.5-18 min), 100-0% B (18-19.5 min), 0% B (19.5-21 min). The samples were kept at a temperature of 6 °C.</p>	<p>The QTOF-MS instrument Bruker maXis Impact QTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) operated both in ESI+, using a capillary voltage of 4.0 kV, and ESI-, using a capillary voltage of 2.5 kV. The nebulizer gas pressure was 1.2 bar, and the drying gas flow and temperature were 8.0 L/min at 220 °C. The injection volume was 10 µl in ESI- and 5 µl in ESI+. A mass range of 50-1000 m/z and a sampling rate of 4 Hz were used.</p><p><br></p><p>Calibration was performed at the end of each run using sodium formate in both ionization modes. In total 3 QC samples were injected at the beginning followed by random injection of samples with QC samples in between every 5th sample for quality assessment of instrument performance.</p>	<p>The UHPLC-QTOF data was converted to the <strong>mzML file format</strong> by <strong>BrukerCompassXport (version: 3.0.7)</strong>. Features were extracted using <strong>XCMS (version 3.12.0)[1]</strong>, including m/z values, retention times, and peak areas using <strong>R project (version 4.0.3)</strong>. Removal of calibration scans (all scans after 800 s) was performed prior to peak detection using <strong>KNIME (version 3.6.2)[2]</strong>. The <strong>centWave algorithm[3]</strong> was used for peak picking with a resolution of 12 ppm and a signal-to-noise threshold of 6. The <strong>Obiwarp algorithm[4] </strong>was used for retention time correction. Gap filling was conducted to recover missing signals in the raw data. Features had to be present in at least 50% of the samples within 1 specific sample group to be considered for further analysis. Isotopes, adducts, and ion source fragments were annotated using <strong>CAMERA[5]</strong>. Coefficients of variation (CV) of QC samples were calculated to omit features with CV &gt; 35% from further processing, and each sample was normalized to total peak intensity.</p><p><br></p><p>The areas from the <strong>XCMS</strong> results were exported to <strong>SIMCA (version 16.0.1, Satorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Goettingen, Germany)</strong> for multivariate data analysis. <strong>Principal Component Analysis (PCA)</strong> was used as quality control to show the potential cluster trend of the groups and QC samples. Subsequently, <strong>Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA)</strong> was used to assess the global change between treatment groups (e.g. MG vs. 13C3-MG). <strong>Pareto scaling</strong> was applied for both <strong>PCA</strong> and <strong>OPLS-DA</strong>. <strong>Variable Importance in Projection (VIP)</strong> scores were calculated from <strong>OPLS-DA</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1</strong>] Smith CA, Want EJ, O'Maille G, Abagyan R, Siuzdak G. XCMS: processing mass spectrometry data for metabolite profiling using nonlinear peak alignment, matching, and identification. Anal Chem. 2006 Feb 1;78(3):779-87. doi:10.1021/ac051437y. PMID:16448051.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Berthold MR et al. (2008). KNIME: The Konstanz Information Miner. In: Preisach C, Burkhardt H, Schmidt-Thieme L, Decker R. (eds) Data Analysis, Machine Learning and Applications. Studies in Classification, Data Analysis, and Knowledge Organization. Springer, Berlin, Heidelberg. doi:10.1007/978-3-540-78246-9_38.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Tautenhahn R, Böttcher C, Neumann S. Highly sensitive feature detection for high resolution LC/MS. BMC Bioinformatics. 2008 Nov 28;9:504. doi:10.1186/1471-2105-9-504. PMID:19040729.</p><p><strong>[4]</strong> Prince JT, Marcotte EM. Chromatographic alignment of ESI-LC-MS proteomics data sets by ordered bijective interpolated warping. Anal Chem. 2006 Sep 1;78(17):6140-52. doi:10.1021/ac0605344. PMID:16944896.</p><p><strong>[5]</strong> Kuhl C, Tautenhahn R, Böttcher C, Larson TR, Neumann S. CAMERA: an integrated strategy for compound spectra extraction and annotation of liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry data sets. Anal Chem. 2012 Jan 3;84(1):283-9. doi:10.1021/ac202450g. PMID:22111785.</p>	<p>Features directly derived from <strong>MG</strong> were determined by direct comparison of the light and heavy labeled treatment groups (<strong>MG vs. 13C3-MG</strong>). Features were pre-selected as <strong>‘light’-MG</strong> derived if they followed these criteria: Fold change <strong>MG vs. 13C3-MG</strong> &gt;1.2, <strong>peak area</strong> &gt; 5000 in at least one of the compare groups, p-value &lt;0.05 (unpaired t-test; equal, or unequal variance based on an <strong>F-test</strong>; corrected for multiple comparisons using the <strong>Benjamini-Hochberg method;</strong> <strong>FDR</strong> &lt; 5%), and a <strong>VIP score</strong> &gt;1 (<strong>OPLS-DA</strong>).&nbsp;</p><p>A feature was pre-selected as <strong>‘heavy’-MG</strong> derived if it followed these criteria: <strong>Fold change</strong> of <strong>MG vs.13C3-MG</strong> &gt;0.8, <strong>peak area</strong> &gt; 5000 in at least 1 of the compare groups, <strong>p-value</strong> &lt;0.05 (<strong>FDR corrected</strong>), and a <strong>VIP score</strong> &gt;1.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>MG-derived</strong> features were selected as <strong>‘MG pairs’</strong> if 2 corresponding features had a mass difference of exactly 3.01 (± 0.05), or 6.02 (± 0.05) Da within a retention time difference of &lt;2s. Subsequently, corresponding features were labeled as <strong>MG</strong>, or <strong>13C3-MG</strong> derived. For preliminary identification of <strong>MG-derived</strong> metabolites, we calculated the <strong>pre-MG modified</strong> metabolite masses by subtracting MG (C3H4O2; 72.021 Da) from the <strong>MG-modified</strong> feature masses. For feature fragmentation and identification, preselected ions were fragmented using <strong>collision induced fragmentation (CID)</strong> with a collision energy of 10, 15, or 20 eV and compared to fragments from authentic standards. The data was analyzed in <strong>Bruker Compass DataAnalysis (version: 4.1)</strong>.</p>

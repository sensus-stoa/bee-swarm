Study Identifier	MTBLS671
Study Title	Semi‐polar root exudates in natural grassland communities
Study Description	In the rhizosphere, plants are exposed to a multitude of different biotic and abiotic factors, to which they respond by exuding a wide range of secondary root metabolites. So far, it has been unknown to which degree root exudate composition is species-specific and is affected by land use, the local impact and local neighborhood under field conditions. In this study, root exudates of 10 common grassland species were analyzed, each five of forbs and grasses, in the German Biodiversity Exploratories using a combined phytometer and untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) approach. Redundancy analysis and hierarchical clustering revealed a large set of semi-polar metabolites common to all species in addition to species-specific metabolites. Chemical richness and exudate composition revealed that forbs, such as Plantago lanceolata and Galium species, exuded more species-specific metabolites than grasses. Grasses instead were primarily affected by environmental conditions. In both forbs and grasses, plant functional traits had only a minor impact on plant root exudation patterns. Overall, our results demonstrate the feasibility of obtaining and untargeted profiling of semi-polar metabolites under field condition and allow a deeper view in the exudation of plants in a natural grassland community.

Study Protocol Description	Ten species were raised for the experiment: 5 forbs (Achillea millefolium L. [Asteraceae], Galium mollugo L., Galium verum L. [Rubiaceae], Plantago lanceolata L. [Plantaginaceae], Ranunculus acris L. [Ranunculaceae]) and 5 grasses (Alopecurus pratensis L., Arrhenatherum elatius [L.] P.Beauv. ex J.Presl & C.Presl., Dactylis glomerata L., Lolium perenne L., Poa pratensis L. [all Poaceae]). The plants were planted as phytometer in 18 experimental grassland plots of each of the three German Biodiversity Exploratories [1]. For detailed descriptions of the raising conditions, planting process and experimental setup see [2].</br></br> Three hundred and eighty nine plants of 46 of the 54 experimental plots were harvested using the same approach described in Herz et al. [2]. To extract one individual, a block of soil with size of 20 cm depth and 15 cm length containing the plant was dugged out. Whereby, damage to the roots was avoided, as they did not grow into other patches. The rhizosphere soil and other plant roots were carefully removed followed by a gentle wash step of the target roots with tap water and dionised water.</br></br> Ref:</br> [1] Fischer, M., Bossdorf, O., Gockel, S., Hänsel, F., Hemp, A., Hessenmöller, D., Korte, G., Nieschulze, J., Pfeiffer, S., Prati, D., Renner, S., Schöning, I., Schumacher, U., Wells, K., Buscot, F., Kalko, E. K. V., Linsenmair, K. E., Schulze, E.-D., Weisser, W. W., Implementing large-scale and long-term functional biodiversity research: The Biodiversity Exploratories. Basic and Applied Ecology, 2010. 11(6): p. 473-485</br> [2] Herz, K., Dietz, S., Haider, S., Jandt, U., Scheel, D., Bruelheide, H., Drivers of intraspecific trait variation of grass and forb species in Germanmeadows and pastures. Journal of Vegetation Science, 2017. 28(4): p. 705 - 716	<p>The complete roots of the intact plant were placed in brown plastic vessels containing deionised water of HPLC quality and exuded for 2 hrs. To distinguish exudates from procedure artefact, water samples without exudation (blank) were treated exactly like the exudate samples. All samples were frozen and stored at -20 °C until further processing. The samples were filtered after a slowly thawing process and reduced to soluble substances by evaporating the water under reduced pressure (20 mBar) at 40 °C by rotary evaporator. The metabolites were resolved by dissolving them two times with 100 % methanol (Sigma-Aldrich), sonicating them for 10 min at 20 °C and followed by an second evaporation step (under pressure, 40 °C) in a vacuum centrifuge. The metabolites were reconstituted in 80% MeOH containing 20 µg/ml 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid and 10 µM Ribitol as internal standards and centrifuged to remove remaining particles. An aliquot of 100 µl of exudate was subjected to mass spectrometry.</p><p><br></p><p>Exudate samples and water controls (blanks) were pooled according to their affiliation to the ten species and three Exploratory sites.</p>	<p>The separation of samples was performed by ultra performance liquid chromatography (ACQUITY UPLC; Waters) equipped with an Acquity UPLC® HSS T3 column (ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 Column, 100Å, 1.8 µm, 1 mm x 100 mm,; Waters) with 2 µl full loop injection at 40 °C. The following gradient was utilized: flow rate of 150 µl/min 0–1 min, isocratic 95% A (water/formic acid, 99.9/0.1 (v/v)), 5% B (acetonitrile/formic acid, 99.9/0.1 (v/v)); 1–14 min, linear from 5 to 95% B; 14–18 min, isocratic 95% B; 18–20 min, isocratic 5% B. </p><p><br></p><p>Quality control sample; performance was supervised by measurements of a standard mix of eight substances (MM8: 10 µM α-Phenylglycin, 10 µM Kinetin, 10 µM Rutin, 10 µM o-Anisic acid, 10 µM Phlorizin, 10 µM IAA Valine, 10 µM Indolacetonitril, 10 µM Biochanin) every ten samples.</p>	The eluting features were detected from m/z 90 to 1000 using a MicrOTOF–Q II hybrid quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer equipped with an Apollo II electrospray ion source (Bruker Daltonics, www.bruker.com) in negative ion mode. The following instrument settings were used: nebulizer gas: nitrogen, 1.6 bar; dry gas: nitrogen, 6 l/min, 190 °C; capillary: -4000 V; end plate offset: -500 V; funnel 1 RF: - 200 Vpp; funnel 2 RF: - 200 Vpp; in- source CID energy: 0 eV; hexapole RF: - 100 Vpp; quadrupole ion energy: -5 eV; collision gas: nitrogen; collision energy: -7 eV; collision: - 150 Vpp; transfer time: 70 µs; pre pulse storage: 5 µs; spectra rate: 3 Hz. The fragment mass spectra were obtained by acquisition of collision induced dissociation (CID) mass spectrometry on a MicrOTOF–Q I hybrid quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer also equipped with an Apollo II electrospray ion source (Bruker Daltonics, www.bruker.com). Argon was used as collision gas. Product ions were detected using the same instrument settings as described above. CID mass spectra were acquired at first by automated data dependent MS/MS with an isolation width of ± 3-15 m/z and fragmentation inside the collision cell with an applied collision energy in the range of 15–70 eV. Additional CID mass spectra were acquired by scheduled precursor ion list (SPIL) dependent MS/MS with an isolation width of ± 3-15 m/z and fragmentation inside the collision cell with an applied collision energy in the range of 15–70 eV. SPIL were prepared according to the statistical selection of species specific compounds.	The individual raw data files where recalibrated on lithium formate cluster ions obtained by automatic infusion of 20 µl 10mM lithium hydroxide in isopropanol/water/formic acid, 49.9/ 49.9/0.2 (v/v/v) at a gradient time of 18 min and by using a diverter valve. Peak picking was performed with Compass DataAnalysis 4.4.2 software (Bruker Daltonics) and a signal-to-noise threshold of 2, correlation coefficient threshold 0.7, minimum feature length 7 spectra, smoothing width of 3. Automated detection of adducts was enabled for M-H, M-H2O-H, M+Na-H2, M+K-H2 and M+HCHOOH-H. No background subtraction were performed. Retention time alignment and feature extraction was performed by Compass ProfileAnalysis 2.3 (Bruker Daltonics) within the retention time (RT) range of 0.01 to 18.00 min and mass range of 90 to 1000 mass to charge ratio (m/z). Peak grouping was performed with an allowed deviation of 0.1 min for the retention time and 200 mDa for the mass. Features occurring only once in all samples were excluded from further analysis.	<p>An automated annotation of the feature list by m/z and RT was done with MetaboScape 2.0 (Bruker). Features occurring in 50 % of water controls were excluded from the metabolite list.</p><p><br></p><p>For the acquisition of CID mass spectra, exudate samples and water controls were pooled according to their affiliation to the ten species and three exploratory sites. The separation and MS measurement was performed as described in the mass spectrometer protocol. CID mass spectra were acquired at first by automated data dependent MS/MS and, if necessary, using a scheduled precursor ion list with an isolation width of ± 3-15 m/z and fragmentation inside the collision cell with an applied collision energy in the range of 15–70 eV. Argon was used as collision gas. Product ions were detected using the same parameter settings as described above. MS as well as tandem MS measurements of pooled samples were processed with MetaboScape 3.0.1 software (Bruker Daltonics). T-Rex 3D algorithm with following settings: Intensity threshold 1500, minimum peak length 7 spectra, minimum peak length recursive 7 spectra, minimum of features for extraction 2, no log mass calibration, primary ion M-H-, expected ions M+Cl-, M+Na-H-, M+K-H-, ions for pseudo spectra M-H-H2O, M+ HCOOH-H-, EIC correlation 0.8, mass range 90 – 1001, RT range 0.01-18.</p><p><br></p><p>In a first step, the most probable elemental composition was calculated with MetaboScape 3.1’s Smart Formula algorithm (Bruker) and a putative annotation of compounds was performed automatically by MetaboScape 3.1’s 'Annotate with analyte list' and 'Annotate to spectral library' functions. These program uses in-house library of measured standards and previously identified metabolites containing m/z values, RT, precursor ion and fragment ions of measured MS and CID spectra. In a second step, MS/MS spectra of compounds (limited to the species specific metabolites) were exported and processed with MetFamily metabolite classification online software [1]. There, fragment spectra were grouped according to their m/z fragment similarity by hierarchical clustering and jaccard distance. Putative classification of metabolites was done manually based on the grouping diagnostic fragments and neutral losses, either by searching for them in the in-house library or using expert knowledge.</p><p><br></p><p>Ref:</p><p>[1] Treutler, H., Tsugawa, H., Porzel, A., Gorzolka, K., Tissier, A., Neumann, S., Balcke, G. U., Discovering Regulated Metabolite Families in Untargeted Metabolomics Studies. Anal Chem, 2016. 88(16): p. 8082-8090

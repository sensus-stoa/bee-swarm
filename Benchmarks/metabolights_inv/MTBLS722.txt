Study Identifier	MTBLS722
Study Title	Untargeted LC-MS metabolomic studies of Asteraceae species to discover inhibitors of Leishmania major dihydroorotate dehydrogenase
Study Description	<p><strong>INTRODUCTION:</strong> Interesting data about the family Asteraceae as a new source of Leishmania major dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (LmDHODH) inhibitors are presented. This key macromolecular target for parasites causing neglected diseases catalyzes the fourth reaction of the de novo pyrimidine biosynthetic pathway, which takes part in major cell functions, including DNA and RNA biosynthesis.</p><p><strong>OBJECTIVES:</strong> We aimed to (1) determine LmDHODH inhibitor candidates, revealing the type of chemistry underlying such bioactivity, and (2) predict the inhibitory potential of extracts from new untested plant species, classifying them as active or inactive based on their LC-MS based metabolic fingerprints.</p><p><strong>METHODS:</strong> Extracts from 150 species were screened for the inhibition of LmDHODH, and untargeted UHPLC-(ESI)-HRMS metabolomic studies were carried out in combination with in silico approaches.</p><p><strong>RESULTS:</strong> The IC50 values determined for a subset of 59 species ranged from 148&nbsp;µg/mL to 9.4&nbsp;mg/mL. Dereplication of the metabolic fingerprints allowed the identification of 48 metabolites. A reliable OPLS-DA model (R2 &gt; 0.9, Q2 &gt; 0.7, RMSECV &lt; 0.3) indicated the inhibitor candidates; nine of these metabolites were identified using data from isolated chemical standards, one of which-4,5-di-O-E-caffeoylquinic acid (IC50 73&nbsp;µM)-was capable of inhibiting LmDHODH. The predictive OPLS model was also effective, with 60% correct predictions for the test set.</p><p><strong>CONCLUSION:</strong> Our approach was validated for (1) the discovery of LmDHODH inhibitors or interesting starting points for the optimization of new leishmanicides from Asteraceae species and (2) the prediction of extracts from untested species, classifying them as active or inactive.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Intact and fully developed leaves from 150 Asteraceae species belonging to 47 genera from 14 tribes were (1) collected or obtained from collaborators or (2) purchased. In Table S1 in the paper associated with this study, all information regarding the exact locations of the collection sites (geographical coordinates) and collector/voucher specimen codes are reported. Plant material was deposited mostly at the SPF herbarium of the Department of Botany, Institute of Biosciences, University of São Paulo (USP), São Paulo-SP, Brazil. There is documentation to attest the authenticity of the commercial material. The Genetic Heritage Management Council authorization number is 010047/2015-8. All the collected plant samples were immediately deposited in resealable zippered storage bags containing silica gel beads. The leaves were dried under air circulation at 35 °C for 48 h.</p>	<p>The ultrasound-assisted extraction was based on detailed protocols for large-scale untargeted plant metabolomics[1]-[3] and previous studies from our research group [4]-[8]. 60 mg of leaf tissue were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle and then extracted with 3.0 ml of an HPLC-grade EtOH-H2O (8:2, v/v) solution in an ultrasonic bath (40 kHz/135 Watts RMS) at 25 °C for 10 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 19,975 x g for 15 min. The supernatant was partitioned with 1.5 ml of HPLC-grade n-hexane, and the aqueous layer was dried under reduced pressure (Savant SPD 2010-220, Thermo, EUA). The extracts were kept at -4 °C. All the extraction yields are reported in Table S1 of the paper associated with this study. These 150 plant extracts, obtained in triplicate (analytical replicates), are part of our library of extracts from the Asteraceae species (AsterLib II).</p><p><br></p><p>Ref:</p><p>[1] De Vos RC, Moco S, Lommen A, Keurentjes JJ, Bino RJ, Hall RD. Fingerprinting metabolomics in tropical mistletoes: A case study with facultative aluminum-accumulating species. Phytochemistry Letters, 25(March), 90–94. doi:10.1016/j.phytol.2018.04.013.</p>	<p>The analytical platform was an Accela UHPLC (Thermo Scientific, USA) apparatus coupled to a heated electrospray ionization source (H-ESI) and an Exactive Plus mass spectrometer with an Orbitrap analyzer (Thermo Scientific). Each plant extract was solubilized in the mobile phase (1.0 mg/ml) and filtered through a 0.2 µm PTFE membrane filter. The samples (4 µl) were subjected to chromatography on a XB-C18 column (Kinetex, 150 x 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm, Phenomenex, USA) with a C18 Security Guard Ultra cartridge (Phenomenex). The mobile phase consisted of purified water (A) and acetonitrile (B), both with 0.1% formic acid. All organic solvents were LC–MS grade (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). The flow rate was 400 µl/min. The gradient was as follows: 0–15 min at 0–5% B; 15–20 min at 5–50% B; and 20–30 at 50–100% B. The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and the tray temperature was maintained at 25 °C.</p>	<p>High resolution mass spectrometry was carried out on a Thermo Scientific Exactive Plus mass spectrometer in polarity switching mode with the following settings: resolution power, 70,000 (Thermo Calmix calibration solution, Sigma-Aldrich); scan time, 2.5 scans/s; mass range, from 150 to 2000 amu; centroid acquisition mode; maximum inject time, 250 ms (fixed Automatic Gain Control); and higher-energy collisional dissociation gas on (N2, 35 units, all ion fragmentation mode). The ESI source settings were as follows: sheath gas (N2) flow rate, 30; auxiliary gas (N2) flow rate, 10; capillary temperature, 300 °C; spray voltage in positive ionization mode, 3.6 kV; spray voltage in negative ionization mode, 3.2 kV; and S-lens radio frequency level, 55. All samples were randomly analyzed in triplicate (technical replicates) to check for reproducibility. Solvent blanks were analyzed for comparison. The mass spectra were acquired using the Xcalibur 2.1.0 software package (Thermo Scientific).</p>	<p>The raw data files were separated into their respective positive and negative ionization modes using Xcalibur and converted to the .mzXML format using ProteoWizard-MSconvert software (Proteowizard Software Foundation, USA)[1]. Data were then exported to MZmine 2.23 software (MZmine VTT, Finland)[2][3] to be processed with the following parameters[4]-[6]: baseline correction (smoothing: 1.0 x E^4); mass detection (exact mass algorithm, noise level 1.0 x E^6); Fourier transform MS shoulder peaks filter (Lorentzian function); chromatogram builder (minimum time span: 0.3 min, minimum height: 1.1 x E^6, m/z tolerance: 0.005); chromatogram deconvolution (local minimum search algorithm, search minimum in the RT range: 0.2 min, minimum absolute height: 1.1 x E^6); deisotoping (isotopic peak grouper, RT tolerance: 0.6 min); alignment by RANSAC (RT tolerance: 30 min, RT tolerance after correction: 0.6 min, RANSAC iterations: 2000); and gap filling (peak finder, intensity tolerance: 1%, RT tolerance: 0.6). The parameters were established based on the MZmine default values[2][3], chromatograms, mass analyzer features and trial and error.</p><p><br></p><p>Ref:</p><p>[1] Kessner D, Chambers M, Burke R, Agus D, Mallick P. J Chromatogr A. 2007 Jul 27;1158(1-2):318-28. doi:10.1016/j.chroma.2007.04.021. PMID:17466315</p>	<p>The dereplication was initiated with all the annotated peaks of the 59 plant extracts analyzed by UHPLC-(ESI)-HRMS. Then, the identification was focused on the list of inhibitor candidates. Compounds were tentatively identified based on a comparison of the RT, accurate mass measurements, MS2 fragmentation patterns, chemotaxonomy and searches of the in-house and online databases. 4 confidence levels of metabolite identification were described[1] as follows. Level 1: compounds that were identified through our in-house database AsterLib I (coupled to MZmine) containing the RT and accurate mass measurement data for 120 natural compounds analyzed under the same conditions as the extracts; identifications were confirmed by MS2 fragmentation patterns in UHPLC-(ESI)-HRMS (all ion fragmentation mode); the compounds used as chemical standards were previously isolated, and identified by 1H NMR, from the Asteraceae species (AsterLib II)[2]-[14]. Level 2: compounds that were putatively annotated from (1) our in-house database AsterDB (www.asterbiochem.org/asterdb;[15]) (coupled to MZmine), which contains more than 2000 metabolites reported in the family Asteraceae and (2) from the online databases Dictionary of Natural Products (DNP, http://dnp.chemnetbase.com/), SciFinder (https://scifinder.cas.org/scifinder) and ChemSpider (www.chemspider.com); no standard compound was used, and the identification was performed based on accurate mass values, spectral similarity and/or another physicochemical property. Level 3: putatively characterized compound classes based only on accurate mass comparisons relative to the monoisotopic exact mass (&lt; 5 ppm accuracy) of the secondary metabolites from AsterDB and the three online databases previously mentioned. Level 4: compounds that were not identified (n.i.).</p><p><br></p><p>Ref:</p><p>[1] Sumner LW, Amberg A, Barrett D, Beale MH, Beger R, Daykin CA et al.

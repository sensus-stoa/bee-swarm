Study Title	The Effect of Dietary Bioactives on Gut Microbiome DivErsity (DIME) - A pilot study (Targeted metabolomics)
Study Description	<p>The gut microbiome refers to the complex community of microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, archaea and viruses) present within our gut. The composition and complexity of gut microbiota communities are crucial for maintaining human health. Humans depend on the gut microbiota to undertake essential metabolic functions and for effective immune system operations, such as protection against pathogens and maintaining gut integrity. Richer diversity in these microbiota communities has been associated with better health. A key modulator of intestinal microbial colonies is food. Dietary bioactives, also referred to as phytochemicals (non-nutrient food components) present in fruit and vegetables, can modulate metabolic processes, promoting better health; however, the interaction of bioactives with microbial communities is not yet fully understood. We have conducted a 2x2 randomised crossover study to estimate the effect of diets rich in dietary bioactives on gut microbial diversity and health markers in 20 healthy participants. Participants consumed a diet of high in bioactives (HB) rich foods (containing polyphenols, Sulphur (S) metabolites and carotenoids) vs a low in bioactives (LB) diet for 2 wk, with an in-between washout period of 4 wk. The primary aim was to examine the effect of a 2-wk diet high in bioactives rich foods on gut microbial diversity compared to a low in bioactives diet. The secondary aim was to assess a high vs a low in bioactives diet on markers of cardiovascular health, inflammation and post-prandial glucose. We hypothesized that consuming a high in bioactives diet would increase microbial diversity compared to a low in bioactives diet.</p><p><strong>METHODS:</strong> Each volunteer completed 4 study visit days, 1 at the start and 1 at the end of each intervention phase. Dietary intake, including bioactives, was recorded through the Libro app linked to Nutritics. We recorded diets during a 7-d period (baseline habitual diet) followed by a 14-d intervention period per arm. On each study day, volunteers provided a 24 h urine sample collected in the 24 h preceding their visit and a stool sample. Urine and stool samples were utilized for both untargeted and targeted metabolomics. Microbial composition was assessed through whole-genome shotgun sequencing carried out by GeneWiz, performed using Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform with paired-end read length 2 x 125 bp. For vascular health, total HDL/LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, along with inflammatory markers such as hs-CRP, were analyzed with an at-home finger-prick blood sample collection kit (Medichecks). Participants also carried out an oral glucose tolerance test; glucose measurements were provided through the Freestyle Libre continuous glucose monitor (Abbott Laboratories) over a 2 h period following ingestion of 113 mL of polycal (75 g anhydrous glucose).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Targeted metabolomics</strong> is reported in the current study <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS7290' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MTBLS7290</strong></a>.</p><p><strong>Untargeted metabolomics</strong> is reported in <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS8100' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MTBLS8100</strong></a>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Linked cross omic data sets:</strong></p><p>Whole genome sequencing data associated with this study are available in the <strong>European Nucleotide Archive (ENA)</strong>: accession number<strong> </strong><a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/browser/view/PRJEB55607' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>PRJEB55607</strong></a>.</p><p>Metagenomic data associated with this study are available from <strong>MGnify</strong>: accession number<strong> </strong><a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metagenomics/studies/MGYS00006076' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MGYS00006076</strong></a>.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The primary aim of the <strong>DIME study</strong> was to test the hypothesis that a <strong>2-wk high in bioactive compounds diet</strong> compared to a <strong>2-wk</strong> <strong>low in bioactive compounds diet</strong> increases microbiome diversity. The secondary aims were: (1) to determine gut diversity and microbiome composition, (2) to determine levels of microbial and host metabolites (3) to determine the levels of inflammatory cardiovascular markers and glycaemic response. The study was a <strong>randomised 2 x 2 cross-over study</strong>, where participants consumed a <strong>diet</strong> <strong>high in bioactive rich foods for 2 wk</strong> and, following a <strong>4 wk washout period</strong>, a <strong>diet low in bioactive rich foods for 2 wk</strong> (<strong>Figure 1</strong> in the paper associated with this study). Levels of dietary fibre were kept constant at 30 g. The <strong>'high bioactive'</strong> arm consumed polyphenols, Sulphur-S containing metabolites and carotenoids (<strong>Table 1</strong> in the paper associated with this study). The protocol was approved by the Human Research Governance Committee of the Quadram Institute Bioscience (<strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>HRGC QIB</strong>) in December 15th 2020. The North West – Greater Manchester West&nbsp;Research Ethics Committee (<strong>REC: 20/NW/0359</strong>) and the NHS Health Research Authority (<strong>HRA</strong>) gave full ethical approval. The trial was registered on a publicly accessible database (<strong>ClinicalTrials.gov, ISRCTN14510401</strong>).</p><p><br></p><p>In total,<strong> 20 healthy adults aged 18-60</strong>, with a body mass index (BMI) between 18.5 and 35 kg/m^2 were recruited and randomised to the order in which they received the 2 dietary intervention phases (diet<strong> 'high bioactive'</strong> and diet <strong>'low bioactive'</strong>). Block randomisation (1 block of 12 and 1 block of 13) was carried out to ensure that the diets were roughly balanced with respect to calendar dates, which otherwise could have confounded the effects of the diet. Written consent from participants for undertaking the study and tissue banking of samples was obtained. Before the dietary intervention, participants completed a <strong>1-wk “run-in”</strong> period during which participants recorded their habitual diet and activity using the <strong>Libro app</strong> and fitness tracker. Each volunteer completed <strong>4 study visit days</strong>, 1 at the start and 1 at the end of each intervention phase.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>On each study day (Study <strong>Visit 1, Visit 2, Mid-point sample, Visit 3 and Visit 4</strong>), volunteers provided a 24 h <strong>urine</strong> and <strong>stool samples</strong> collected in the 24 h preceding their visit, completed oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) by drinking 113 mL of Polycal and then collecting the CGM readings over a 2 h period, had anthropometric data recorded (these were self-reported using calibrated study equipment alongside data collected at the clinical research facility (CRF)) and provided fasted blood samples for full blood count and HbA1c. Urine samples were collected in a 3 L universal container containing 1 g ascorbic acid. Following collection and mixing, the sample was weighed, and <strong>urine</strong> was divided into <strong>1 mL aliquots</strong> and frozen at -80 °C until required for analysis. <strong>Stool samples</strong> were aliquoted into <strong>200 mg</strong> and <strong>diluted 1:1 in water</strong>, so 200 µL of MiliQ sterile water were added and the samples were vortexed to obtain a slurry consistency, before storing them at -80 °C until further processing.</p>	<p>Fecal samples were heated to 36.5 °C and homogenized by ultrasonic processing before sample preparation. Fecal samples global profiling was performed using a single phase extraction protocol. For quantification of short chain fatty acids (<strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>SCFAs</strong>) in each sample, <strong>50 mg slurry</strong> (faeces:Milli-Q water, 1:1 v/v) was mixed with <strong>1492.5 µL of</strong> <strong>96% EtOH</strong> and <strong>7.5 µL of 30 mM&nbsp;isotope-labelled standard (butyric-d7 acid)</strong>. The mix was vortexed for 30 s, incubated at room temperature for 30 s, vortexed again for 30 s, and mixed at 60 °C for 2 min using a Thermo mixer at 1400 rpm. The samples were centrifuged for 2 min at 14000 rpm, and supernatants were then filtered through a 0.2 µm filter. <strong>Fecal supernatant (50 µL)</strong>, <strong>10 µL of 200 mM</strong> 13C6-3-Nitrophenylhydrazine (<strong>13C6-3-NPH</strong>) and <strong>10 µL of 120 mM</strong> N-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)-N'-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (<strong>EDC</strong>) - <strong>6% pyridine</strong> solution were added into a 1 mL 96-well plate. The samples in the plate were left to react at room temperature for at least 30 min with shaking. Subsequently, the reaction mixture was diluted to 1 mL by adding <strong>930 µL of 10% EtOH </strong>solution and mixed. <strong>100 µL of the diluted sample</strong> reaction product were mixed with <strong>100 µL of isotope-labelled IS mix</strong>. Through this derivatization procedure, the samples have been diluted 40x in total. The less abundant SCFAs were under the limit of quantification after this dilution in all the samples; therefore, we analyzed the samples twice using 40x and 4x dilutions to have the less abundant SCFAs within the calibration range. The 4x dilution was obtained by diluting the sample derivatization reaction mixture to 100 µL by adding 30 µL of 10% EtOH solution.</p><p><br></p><p>A pool of all extracted fecal samples in the sample set was used as <strong>QC </strong>and run<strong> </strong>every 8-10 samples, for normalization between or within analytical batches or for assessing quality by PCA analysis of the complete dataset. In addition, an<em> in-house</em> stool pool of feces from ∼15 healthy subjects was used as a <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>QC</strong> to allow comparison between studies (<strong>Fecal_MetNexs pool</strong>). The<strong> calibration curve</strong> consisted of 7 different dilutions of a mixed standard solution where each <strong>SCFA </strong>(Table S4) has 10 mM concentration before the derivatization. 20 µL of each dilution of the mixed standard solution was derivatized using <strong>10 µL of 200 mM</strong> <strong>3NPH</strong> solution and <strong>10 µL of 120 mM EDC-6% pyridine</strong> solution. The reaction mixture was diluted to 1 mL with 10% EtOH solution. <strong>100 µL of a derivatized mixed standard solution</strong> were mixed with <strong>2 µL of pooled matrix</strong> (consisting of 100 µL of 5 random samples) and <strong>98 µL isotope-labelled IS mix</strong> at the sample plate. The final concentrations of the calibration samples were <strong>50, 25, 12.5, 6.25, 3.125, 0.781 and 0.195 µM</strong>.</p>	<p>The SCFA quantification was performed on a <strong>Waters ACQUITY UPLC system</strong>. An <strong>ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm x 100 mm; Waters)</strong> and an <strong>ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 VanGuard Pre-column (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm x 5 mm; Waters)</strong> were used, at a temperature of 50 °C for chromatographic separation. In total 5 µL of each sample were injected into a gradient of mobile phase A (0.01% formic acid in Milli-Q water) and mobile phase B (0.01% formic acid in Acetonitrile). The binary solvent elution gradient was optimized at 15% B for 2 min, 15-55% B in 9 min, and then held at 100% B for 1 min. The column was equilibrated for 3 min at 15% B between injections. The column flow rate was 0.6 mL/min and the autosampler was kept at 5 °C.</p>	<p>The SCFA quantification was performed on a <strong>Waters SYNAPT G2 HDMS Q-TOF</strong> <strong>System</strong>. The <strong>electrospray ionization</strong> was operated in <strong>negative mode</strong> with 3.0 kV capillary probe voltage. The cone voltage and the collision energy were set at 30 kV and 5 eV.&nbsp;The ion source and desolvation gas (nitrogen) temperature were 120 and 400 °C, while the sampling cone and desolvation gas flow rates were 50 and 1000 L/h. The scan time was set at 0.08 s with 0.02 s interscan delay for both modes. Data were acquired in centroid mode with a mass range from <strong>100 to 560 Da</strong>. Leucine-enkephalin (500 ng/mL) was infused as a lock-spray agent to calibrate the mass accuracy every 5 s with 1 s scan time.</p>	<p>The raw data were converted to <strong>netCDF</strong> format using <strong>DataBridge Software</strong> (Waters) and imported into <strong>MZmine v2.28. </strong></p>	<p>Data acquisition and visualization were performed in <strong>MassLynx v4.1</strong> software (Waters Corporation, Manchester, UK). The SCFA intensities were preprocessed in <strong>MZmine v2.28</strong>, and the identification of compounds was confirmed with <strong>m/z, RT</strong> and <strong>isotopically labelled internal standards</strong>. The calculation of the internal standard corrected responses, and the quantification of SCFAs were performed using an in-house script in <strong>MATLAB R2015b</strong>. If the response for a given analyte is below <strong>0.195 µM</strong>, the concentration is reported as under the limit of quantification (<strong>ULQ</strong>).&nbsp;The software integrates the peak area for the metabolites, which is then exported as an Excel document. The concentration of the SCFAs was calculated using the equation of the calibration curve and the peak area ratio (analyte peak area/internal standard peak area) of the SCFA.</p>

Study Identifier	MTBLS742
Study Title	Metabolome-based prediction of yield heterosis contributes to the breeding of elite rice
Study Description	The improvement of breeding efficiencies for heterotic and climate-resilient crops can mitigate the food shortage crisis from overpopulation and climate change. Thus far, diverse molecular markers have been utilized for guiding field phenotypic selections, while the accurate predictions of complex heterotic traits are rarely reported. Here we present a practical metabolome-based prediction strategy for yield heterosis in rice. The dissection of population structures based on untargeted metabolite profiles, rather than the screening of predictive variables, was proved to be the initial critical step in multivariate modeling. Then the assessment of each predictive variable's contribution to predictive models was more precise according to all latent factors compared to the conventional first one. Metabolites belonging to specific pathways were tightly connected to yield heterosis, and the up-regulation of galactose metabolism represent robust yield heterosis for hybrids across different growth conditions. Our study demonstrates that metabolome-based predictive models with correctly dissected population structures and screened predictive variables can realize accurate prediction of yield heterosis and manifest great potential for establishing molecular marker-based precision breeding programs.

Study Protocol Description	More than thirty seeds of three pairs of reciprocal hybrids, 18 cultivars, 107 recombinant inbred lines, and Yuetai A were submerged in water at 30 °C for 48 h and then transferred to an incubator for germination at 30 °C for 24 h. Ten seedlings at the same developmental stage were transplanted in plastic pots with a spacing of 2 x 2 cm. Two biological replicates were set for each genotype. Seedlings were grown at 30 °C in a light incubator set to 70% humidity with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. On the 15th day, approximately ten seedlings without parts close to soil (approximately 0.5 cm) and roots were harvested in liquid nitrogen after being washed with ddH2O three times. Two biological replicates of the three pairs of reciprocal hybrids and 18 parents were treated as two independent experimental samples, and two repeats of the 107 recombinant inbred lines and Yuetai A were combined as a single experimental sample for each genotype.	All tissues of each experimental sample were ground into homogenized powders with liquid nitrogen, and 80 mg of powder was transferred into 2 ml EP tubes. After 1 ml of precooled extraction liquid (methanol/acetonitrile/water, 2/2/1, v/v/v) was added with the Agilent Bravo automated liquid handling system (Agilent Technologies, California, USA), samples were vortex-mixed for 60 s. Ten microliters of all samples were pooled as a quality control sample. Two 30-min ultrasonic treatments were applied, and tubes were kept at -20 °C for 60 min. Then, the samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 14,000 g at 4 °C. The supernatants were dried in a vacuum concentrator, and 100 µl of acetonitrile (acetonitrile/water, 1/1, v/v) was added to each tube. After vortex mixing, samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 14,000 g at 4 °C, and the supernatants were used for subsequent LC-MS analysis.	Samples were separated on a Waters (Milford, Massachusetts, USA) reversed-phase T3 column (ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3, 2.1 mm x 100 mm column containing 1.8 μm particles) using the Agilent 1290 Infinity LC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA). After the injection of 2 µl of the sample, the T3 column was washed with solvent A (0.5 mM ammonium fluoride; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) at a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min at 25 °C. The liquid chromatography gradient elution began with 1% solvent B (acetonitrile; Merck, Whitehouse Station, New Jersey, USA) for 1 min, linearly increased to 100% B in 7 min, maintained for 2 min for 100% B, reduced to 1% B in 0.1 min and maintained another 1.9 min for 1% solvent B for re-equilibration. Experimental samples were analyzed in random order at 4 °C, and a quality control sample was injected every nine experimental samples to check the stability of the LC-MS system.	Mass spectrometry data of all samples were collected with a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Agilent 6550 iFunnel QTOF; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA) in negative electron spray ionization mode. The temperature and flow rate of the drying gas were 250 °C and 16 L/min, respectively. The temperature and flow rate of the sheath gas were 400 °C and 12 L/min, respectively. The pressure of the nebulizer gas was 20 pounds per square inch (psi). The capillary voltage, nozzle voltage, and fragment voltage were 3,000 V, 0 V, and 175 V, respectively. The TOF mass spectra data were collected from 60 to 1,200 Da. The acquisition rate was 4 spectra/s and the cycle time was 250 ms. Subsequently, another quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Triple TOF 6600, AB SCIEX, Foster City, California, USA) was used to acquire tandem mass spectra (MS/MS) data of the quality control sample. The parameters of ion source gas1, ion source gas2, and the curtain gas were 40, 80, and 30 psi, respectively. The source temperature was at 650 °C, and the ion spray voltage floating was -4,500 V. The mass range for the TOF MS scan was 60-1,200 Da, and the quality control sample was injected twice to increase the data acquisition rate with the mass range divided into four sequential windows: 60-200, 190-400, 390-600, and 590-1,200 Da and 200-600, 590-750, 740-900, and 890-1,200 Da. The MS accumulation time was 200 ms/spectra and the tandem mass spectra were collected in an information-dependent acquisition manner in high-sensitivity mode. The declustering potential was -60 V, and the collision energy was at 35 ± 15 eV. Ten candidate ions were monitored per cycle using an isotope isolation width of 4.0 Da, and the MS/MS accumulation time was 50 ms.	<p>The msconvert function in ProteoWizard [1] was used to convert raw MS files (.d) to the mzXML format. Then, these files were uploaded to the XCMS [2] for data processing including feature detection, retention time correction and alignment with the method described previously [3].</p><p><br></p><p>Ref:</p><p>[1] Chambers MC, MacLean B, Burke R, Amode D, Ruderman DL, Neumann S, Gatto L, Fischer B, Pratt B, Egertson J et al. 2012.

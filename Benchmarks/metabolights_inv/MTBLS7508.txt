Study Identifier	MTBLS7508
Study Title	Characterisation and localisation of plant metabolites involved in pharmacophagy in the turnip sawfly
Study Description	<p>Several herbivorous insects consume certain metabolites from plants for other purposes than nutrition, such as defence. Adults of the turnip sawfly, <em>Athalia rosae</em> take up specific terpenoids, called clerodanoids, from <em>Ajuga reptans</em>. These metabolites are slightly modified by the sawflies and influence their mating behaviour and defence against predators. We characterised these metabolites and investigated their localisation in the insect and the specificity of the uptake and metabolite modification. Therefore, we performed feeding assays with adults and larvae of <em>A. rosae</em> as well as larvae of <em>Spodoptera exigua</em>, followed by chemical analyses. Two main clerodanoid-derived metabolites were detected in the abdomen and thorax but also on the surface of the adults. Small amounts were also found in larvae of the sawfly, while they were not detectable in <em>S. exigua</em>. Our findings provide new insights into the peculiarities of pharmacophagy and specialised metabolism in <em>A. rosae</em>.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Rearing of insects and plants</strong></p><p>Adult individuals of <strong><em>Athalia rosae</em></strong> were collected over the summer in the surroundings of Bielefeld, Germany. For a continuous rearing, adults were offered <em>Sinapis alba</em> (Brassicaceae) plants for oviposition and larvae were kept on non-flowering plants of <strong><em>Brassica rapa var. pekinensis</em> (Brassicaceae)</strong> for feeding. Insects were kept in cages at a 16:8 h day:night cycle. Larvae pupated in soil provided in Petri dishes or the potting soil. Emerging adult individuals were separated by sex, provided with 2% honey water and kept at ~5 °C in the dark until they were used for experiments. Plants of <strong><em>Ajuga reptans</em></strong> were grown from seeds (RHS, London, United Kingdom) in a greenhouse and kept in a 16:8 h day:night cycle without specific climate control. Additional leaf material was collected around Bielefeld, Germany, and stored at -80 °C. Eggs of <strong><em>Spodoptera exigua</em> </strong>were kindly provided by A. Steppuhn, University of Hohenheim. Larvae were reared on <strong><em>B. rapa var. pekinensis</em></strong> until the fourth instar and then used for feeding trials.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p><strong>Exposure trials with insects</strong></p><p>To study the metabolism of clerodanoids in <strong><em>A. rosae</em></strong>, <strong>adult females </strong>(1-2 d after adult emergence) were placed individually in Petri dishes (5.5 cm diameter) lined with moist filter paper and offered fresh middle-aged leaf discs (6 mm diameter) of <strong><em>A. reptans</em></strong> plants for 1 h (<strong>C+</strong> group). During the exposure, the nibbling duration was tracked visually. After the exposure time, adults were immediately frozen at -80 °C and stored until sample preparation. <strong>Naive adults </strong>(<strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>C- </strong>control group)<strong>,</strong> without contact to <strong><em>A. reptans</em></strong> leaves, were frozen in parallel.</p><p><br></p><p>To investigate the potential metabolism of clerodanoids in<strong> larvae</strong>, feeding experiments were carried out with extracts of <strong><em>A. reptans</em></strong> leaves. For extract preparation, young leaves of flowering <strong><em>A. reptans</em></strong> plants were harvested, weighed, frozen in liquid nitrogen, lyophilized, weighed again and homogenized. The dried material was extracted twice for 10 min in ethyl acetate (LC-MS grade, VWR, Leuven, Belgium) in a concentration of 80 mg dw/ml using an ultrasonic bath. 5 technical replicates were prepared in parallel and aliquots were frozen until use. Fourth-instar larvae of<strong> <em>A. rosae</em></strong> and <strong><em>S. exigua</em> </strong>were starved for 2 h prior the trial. Since both species do not feed as larvae on <strong><em>A. reptans</em></strong>, 15 µl of a leaf extract of <strong><em>A. reptans</em> </strong>(see below) were applied on leaf discs (6 mm diameter) of the <strong>host plant <em>B. rapa var. pekinensis</em></strong> and offered on moistened filter paper to individuals in Petri dishes for 1 h (n = 5 to 6, per species). As <strong>control</strong>, larvae were provided with<strong> solvent-treated host leaf discs</strong> (n = 3, per species). The solvent was allowed to fully evaporate, before the treated leaves were offered to the larvae. After 1 h, leaf discs were mostly consumed and larvae were immediately frozen at -80 °C for later sample preparation.</p>	<p>Insect samples were lyophilized and weighed and either <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>extracted entirely (larvae of both species and adults of </strong><em style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>A. rosae</em><strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>)</strong> or separated into <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>thorax</strong> and <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>abdomen (adult </strong><em style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>A. rosae</em><strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>)</strong>. In a subgroup of frozen <strong><em>A. rosae </em>C+</strong> adults, <strong>surface washings</strong> were performed to test whether the focal substances are mostly distributed on the surface or occur in the insect body itself (see <strong>Fig. 7</strong> in the paper associated with the study). These insects were individually vortexed for either <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>30 s</strong> or <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>2 min</strong> in <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>500 µl ethyl acetate</strong> and the <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>surface washes</strong> kept for later analyses (<strong>n = 5</strong>, each). <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>Washed individuals</strong> were transferred into new tubes and freeze-dried.</p><p><br></p><p>All freeze-dried body samples were homogenized and extracted in <strong>130 µl of 100% ethyl acetate</strong> using an ultrasonic bath. Following that, body extracts and surface washes were dried under reduced pressure at 30 °C and resolved in <strong>100 µl of methanol with 2.4 mg/L mefenemic acid</strong> (Sigma-Aldrich GmbH, Taufkirchen, Germany) as internal standard. All samples were filtered (polytetrafluoroethylene membrane, 0.2 µm pore size, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) and afterwards transferred to glass vials with inserts for LC-MS analysis.</p>	<p>Extracts were measured using an<strong> Dionex UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San José, CA, USA)</strong> equipped with a<strong> Kinetex XB-C18 100 Å (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm x 150 mm; Phenomenex)</strong> with a <strong>SecurityGuard ULTRA C18 (2.1 mm x 2 mm; Phenomenex)</strong> guard column. The injection volume was 5 µl. The column was preheated at 45 °C and a solvent gradient from 0.1% formic acid (~98%, Sigma-Aldrich) in millipore water to 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (LC-MS grade, Fisher Scientific, UK; eluent B) was applied at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min. The proportion of eluent B started at 2% and increased to 30% B within 20 min, then to 75% B within 9 min.</p>	<p>Analysis were done in a<strong> Bruker compact QToF-MS/MS (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) </strong>in <strong>negative electrospray ionisation mode</strong>. The ionisation was performed with a capillary voltage of 3000 V and an end plate offset of 500 V, while the nebuliser (N2) pressure was 3 bar. The dry gas (N2) flow and temperature were 12 L/min at 275 °C. Line spectra were captured at 6 Hz, with a range of <strong>50-1300 m/z</strong>. For internal calibration, a Na(HCOO)-solution was constantly injected to the source at the beginning of each sample to recalibrate the mass axis. Mass spectrometry was performed with an ion energy of 4 eV and the collision cell was set on collision energy of 7 eV, with a transfer time of 75 µs and pre-pulse storage of 6 µs.</p>	<p>Data was pre-checked using <strong>Compass DataAnalysis 4.4 (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany)</strong> and then processed via <strong>Metaboscape 2021b (Bruker Daltonics)</strong>. Mass axis recalibration and peak picking with the <strong>T-ReX 3D algorithm</strong> including spectral background subtraction were performed in <strong>Metaboscape</strong>. The algorithm used an intensity threshold of 1000 counts and minimum peak length of 9 spectra. The allowed ion types for bucket generation were: [M-H]-, [M-H2O-H]-, [M+Cl]-, [M+HCOOH-H]-, [M+CH3COOH-H]- and [2M-H]-. Data was aligned in a bucket table with the same <strong>T-ReX 3D algorithm</strong> using the highest intensity as bucketing basis. The generated bucket table was then exported to <strong>Excel (2016, Microsoft, Washington, USA)</strong>.</p>	<p>For semi-quantification, intensities of the masses <strong>484</strong> and <strong>482 </strong>were related to the intensities of <strong>mefenemic acid </strong>(internal standard). The fragmentation patterns of these focal peaks were used to propose the chemical structure with competitive fragmentation modelling for metabolite identification<strong> (CFM-ID)[1][2]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Allen F, Pon A, Wilson M, Greiner R, Wishart D. CFM-ID: a web server for annotation, spectrum prediction and metabolite identification from tandem mass spectra. Nucleic Acids Res. 2014;42(W1):W94-W9. doi:10.1093/nar/gku436. PMID:24895432.</p><p><strong>[2] </strong>Djoumbou-Feunang Y, Pon A, Karu N, Zheng JM, Li C, Arndt D, et al. CFM-ID 3.0: Significantly Improved ESI-MS/MS Prediction and Compound Identification. Metabolites. 2019;9(4):23. doi:10.3390/metabo9040072. PMID:31013937.</p>

Study Identifier	MTBLS7576
Study Title	Gut microbiota-driven regulation of queen bee ovarian metabolism
Study Description	<p>With the global prevalence of <em>Varroa</em> mites, more and more beekeepers resort to confining the queen bee in a queen cage to control mite infestation or to breed superior and robust queen bees. However, the impact of such practices on the queen bee remains largely unknown. Therefore, we subjected the queen bees to a 21-d egg-laying restriction treatment (from the egg stage to the emergence of adult worker bees) and analyzed the queen bees' ovarian metabolites and gut microbiota after 21 d, aiming to assess the queen bee's quality and assist beekeepers in better hive management. Our findings revealed a significant reduction in the relative expression levels of Vg and Hex110 genes in the ovaries of egg-laying-restricted queen bees compared to unrestricted egg-laying queens. The diversity of gut microbiota in the queen bee exhibited a notable decrease, accompanied by corresponding changes in the core bacterial species of the microbial community. Following egg-laying restriction, the activity of the queen bee's ovaries decreased, while the metabolism of glycerophospholipids remained or stored more lipid molecules, awaiting environmental changes for the queen bee to resume egg-laying promptly. Furthermore, we observed that <em>Bombella</em> in the queen bee's gut may regulate the queen's ovarian metabolism through tryptophan metabolism. These findings provide novel insights into the interplay among queen egg-laying, gut microbiota, and ovarian metabolism.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>This experiment was conducted in the apiary of Kunming University of Science and Technology in May 2022. 20 healthy, disease-free colonies of honey bees (<em>Apis mellifera</em>) were selected, and their colony strength, sealed brood area, and food supply were equal. The queen of each colony was a mated queen of the same species and age, healthy, and in egg-laying condition. 10 colonies were randomly selected as 1 group, with each queen being placed in the queen cage (<strong>CQ</strong>) to study the impact of a <strong>21-d egg-laying restriction treatment</strong>, while the remaining 10 colonies were left as queens untreated (<strong>FQ</strong>). After 21 d of captivity, same timeframe as in a previously published study<strong>[1]</strong>,<strong> </strong>all 20 queens were removed from the colonies and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments. Each queen bee to be analyzed was taken out from the -80 °C refrigerator and placed it on ice to thaw. After thawing,&nbsp;each queen bee was soaked in 75% alcohol for 2 min and then placed in PBS for cleaning.&nbsp;Then, a pair of forceps was used to hold the queen bee's body in place.&nbsp;Scissors were used to lift the scales off the queen bee's abdomen starting from the caudal end of the stomach and along the sides of the abdomen and as described in a previous published protocol<strong>[2]</strong>, the scales of the queen bee's abdomen were then fixed with a needle and the ovaries and intestinal tissues were removed with another pair of forceps. Finally, the intestinal and ovarian tissues were snap frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Toomemaa K and Kaart T. 2022. Caging the queen with and without a comb section to achieve high efficacy of&nbsp;<em>Varroa</em> destructor&nbsp;control.&nbsp;Journal of Apicultural Research, doi:10.1080/00218839.2022.2036310</p><p><strong>[2]&nbsp;</strong>Prešern, J. and Smodiš Škerl, MI. 2019. Parameters influencing queen body mass and their importance as determined by machine learning in honey bees (<em>Apis mellifera carnica</em>).&nbsp;<em>Apidologie</em>&nbsp;50, 745–757, doi:10.1007/s13592-019-00683-y</p>	<p>50 mg of sample were added into a 2 mL centrifuge tube with a 6 mm diameter grinding bead. 400 µL of extraction solution (methanol: acetonitrile; 1:1, v:v) containing 4 internal standards (L-2-chlorophenyl alanine and other 3 confidential commercial compounds) were added. Tissue was homogenized using a cryogenic tissue grinder&nbsp;for 6 min (-10 °C, 50 Hz) and subjected to low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 KHz). The samples were left at -20 °C for 30 min, then centrifuged for 15 min (13,000 x g, 4 °C), and the supernatant was transferred to a vial for analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>A total of 12 experimental samples, 6 for the control group and 6 for the experimental group, were mixed by pipetting 20 µL of supernatant from each sample as <strong>quality control (QC) </strong>samples.</p>	<p>Untargeted metabolomics profiling of the queen bee ovary was performed using a <strong>Thermo Scientific Vanquish UHPLC System</strong>. The chromatographic column was an <strong>ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm x 100 mm; Waters)</strong>, the mobile phase A was 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), mobile phase B was 47.5% acetonitrile + 47.5% isopropanol + 5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid). For <strong>positive mode</strong>, the gradient was as follow: 0% B (0 min), 20% B (3 min), 35% B (4.5 min), 100% B (5 to 6.3 min) and back to 0% B (6.4 to 8 min). For <strong>negative mode</strong>, the gradient was: 0% B (0 min), 5% B (1.5 min), 10% B (2 min), 30% B (4.5 min), 100% B (5 to 6.3 min) and back to 0% B (6.4 to 8 min). The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min in both modes and the injection volume was 3 μL. The column temperature was 40 °C.</p><p><br></p><p>3 quality control (<strong>QC</strong>) samples were injected at the start, to balance the system and column. In the analysis process, 1 QC sample was injected after every 3 samples to monitor instrument stability.</p>	<p>The mass spectrometer used was a <strong>Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). </strong>Samples were subjected to electrospray ionization, and mass spectra were acquired in positive (+) and negative (-) ion scanning modes. The scan range was <strong>70-1050 m/z</strong>, the sheath gas flow rate was 50 arb, the auxiliary gas flow rate was 13 arb, the heating temperature was 425 °C, the capillary temperature was 325 °C and the S-lens voltage was 50. The spray voltage was 3500 V in the positive mode (+) and -3500 V in the negative mode (-).</p>	<p>The raw data were imported into the metabolomics processing software <strong>Progenesis QI</strong> (WatersCorporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak pairing to obtain a data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity information.</p>	<p>The raw metabolomic data were imported into the metabolomics software <strong>Progenesis QI</strong> (WatersCorporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment, resulting in a data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity information. The MS and MS/MS mass spectra were matched to public databases, such as<strong> </strong><a href='https://metlin.scripps.edu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>METLIN</strong></a>, the human metabolome database (<a href='https://hmdb.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>HMDB</strong></a>) and <a href='https://www.lipidmaps.org/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>LIPID MAPS</strong></a>, with the MS mass error set to less than 10 ppm, and the metabolites being identified based on the secondary mass spectra matching score.</p>

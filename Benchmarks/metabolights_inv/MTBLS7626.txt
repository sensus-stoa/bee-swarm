Study Identifier	MTBLS7626
Study Title	Previous infestation by conspecifics leads to a transient increase of the performance of <i>Sitobion avenae</i> aphids on wheat leaves
Study Description	<p>1. The ecological niche of aphids is largely defined by the phloem sap of plants, which they consume. Previous studies revealed that aphids may change the phloem sap metabolome and that a previous aphid infestation may benefit them. As aphids reproduce parthenogenetically, the performance and preference of aphids of the same monoclonal aphid lineage can be compared between previously uninfested and previously aphid-infested plants.</p><p>2. Wheat (<em>Triticum aestivum</em>) leaves were infested by clonal lineages of <em>Sitobion avenae</em> aphids for 9–10 days. Leaf phloem exudates were analysed using amino acid profiling and metabolic fingerprinting, capturing mainly specialised metabolites. It was investigated whether aphid colony sizes differ between leaves that were previously uninfested or infested by aphids of a different clonal lineage or by aphids of the same clonal lineage and whether aphids show a preference for any of these leaves.</p><p>3. The relative concentrations of metabolites in the phloem exudates were not affected by aphid infestation. Aphid colonies were partly larger on previously aphid-infested leaves than on control leaves. However, this effect was transient and finally colonies had similar sizes. Given a choice between previously aphid-infested and uninfested leaves, <em>S. avenae</em> nymphs showed no preferences.</p><p>4. The findings that positive effects of previous aphid infestation on aphid performance are transient and that the performance but not the preference of the aphids was affected by previous aphid infestation highlight the temporal dynamics and complexity of aphid–plant interactions. Niche alteration processes may be partly involved in these interactions.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>An experiment was conducted to test whether/how previous infestation of wheat leaves with <em>S. avenae</em> (Fabricius; Aphididae) aphids affects the metabolic composition of phloem exudates. Clonal lineages of aphids were used. Experiments were carried out in climate chambers (LD 16:8 h) for which the temperature was set to 20°C (maximum deviation ±1°C) and the relative humidity to 70% (± 3%).</p><p><br></p><p>Plant cultivation</p><p>Spring wheat (<em>T. aestivum</em> L., cv. Tybalt, Poaceae; von Borries- Eckendorf, Leopoldshöhe, Germany) was germinated in a 2:1 mixture of river sand: soil (Fruhstorfer Spezialsubstrat Type P; Hawita Group, Vechta, Germany), which had been steamed (90–95°C, 4 h). The sowing was done in October 2020. After 7 days, seedlings were transferred to 0.5 L pots with the same substrate. Plants were watered twice weekly with tap water, with equal amounts per pot that increased over time. Twenty-one days after sowing, each plant was fertilised with 1.5 g fertiliser (Plantosan N-P2O5-K2O 20-10-15, 6% MgO, 2% S, traces of B, Cu, Fe, Mn, Mo, Zn; Manna, Düsseldorf, Germany).</p><p><br></p><p>Aphid infestation</p><p><em>S. avenae</em> was obtained from re-natur (Stolpe, Germany), where the aphids had been reared on wheat plants at circa 20°C. The aphids in the rearing of this company were originally derived from a local population on a conventional wheat field in Schleswig-Holstein (Germany) and re-natur supported this rearing as needed with aphids from Katz Biotech (Baruth, Germany), which in turn were from a laboratory population of the University of Göttingen. Aphids were reared in two gauze tents in the climate chamber (see above). Those of the first tent were directly derived from re-natur. The aphids of the second tent were delivered by Sautter &amp; Stepper (Ammerbuch, Germany); this company also ordered the aphids from re-natur and did not cultivate them in between. We assume that there were different genotypes of aphids within both tents and, because all aphids were from the same company but delivered at different dates (see below) and reared in different tents, there may have been differences in the genotype distributions between the tents. Thus, using aphids from both tents probably increased the coverage of different genotypes. The aphids were reared in the tents for 114 days (Tent 1) and 361 days (Tent 2) before the beginning of the infestation phase. The aphids were reared on non-flowering wheat (cv. Tybalt) of different ages using the substrate and conditions described above; only apterous (wingless) females were parthenogenetically produced. To avoid a mixing of the aphids of the different tents, plants inside the tents were surrounded by water barriers, the tents were only opened in a separate room and gloves were changed between handling the aphids of the different tents. For each tent, 8 monoclonal lineages were established by putting single apterous adults into gauze cages (diameter 20 cm, height 40 cm) with 15–20 two-week-old wheat. This was done 48 days before the infestation phase. To reduce the risk of sampling the same clones several times, the aphids were taken from different plants. Water barriers around the cages were used to ensure a separation of the clonal lineages. Aphids were allowed to reproduce for 35 days and then age-standardised 13 days before the infestation phase by placing 80 adults per lineage in separate cages and collecting nymphs produced within 48 h for the experiments. Twenty-one days after sowing, each wheat plant was equipped with a cage, consisting of a PET cup (WIMEX s.r.o, Náchod, Czech Republic; 500 mL, diameter 95 mm) with the bottom replaced with a metal mesh. The second oldest leaf of each wheat plant was inserted through a cut in white foam rubber affixed to the lid. The distance of the rubber to the stem was circa 3 cm to avoid contamination of phloem exudates with honeydew. Plants were divided into two infestation treatment groups. For the plants of the aphid infestation group (I), 10 apterous aphids with an age of 11–13 days were added to the cages using a brush. All aphids within a cage were derived from the same clonal lineage. The leaves of control (C) plants were inserted into empty cages and brushed similarly. To avoid cross-infestations of the plants, the tightness of the cages was daily checked and water barriers around the plants were used. Plants were arranged in a randomised block design.</p><p><br></p><p>Phloem exudates</p><p>Phloem exudates were collected from <em>n</em> = 8 control plants (C) and <em>n</em> = 16 previously aphid-infested plants (I), including eight plants infested with aphids of Tent 1 and another eight plants with aphids from Tent 2. We used a method described earlier [<strong>1</strong>] with some modifications. Nine days after the beginning of the aphid infestation, cages were opened and aphids (including their exuviae) carefully removed with a brush, while control plants were brushed similarly. The aphids were counted later on. Leaves were cut at the base of the leaf blades, the cut edges placed in 8 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; AppliChem, Darmstadt, Germany; pH 7), re-cut and placed into 50 mL Falcon tubes with 1 mL EDTA solution, using further tubes as lids. After incubation (2 h, dark, room temperature), the cut edges were washed (Millipore water) and the leaf blades were transferred to other tubes with 1 mL Millipore water and incubated (2 h, dark). Subsamples were frozen in liquid N2 and stored at - 80°C. Four blanks were prepared.</p><p><br></p><p><strong><em>References:</em></strong></p><p>[<strong>1</strong>] Stallmann, J., Pons, C.A.A., Schweiger, R. &amp; Müller, C. (2022) Time point- and plant part-specific changes in phloem exudate metabolites of leaves and ears of wheat in response to drought and effects on aphids. PLoS One, 17, e0262671.</p>	<p>Samples were lyophilised and redissolved in 50 μL 85% (v:v) methanol (LC–MS grade; Fisher Scientific, Loughborough, UK) containing L-norvaline and sarcosine (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany; internal standards for primary/secondary amino acids) and hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany; internal standard for metabolic fingerprinting).</p>	<p>Metabolic fingerprinting was done similar to Schweiger et al. 2021 [<strong>1</strong>], using a Thermo Scientific Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system. Metabolites were separated using a Kinetex XB-C18 100 Å (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm x 150 mm; Phenomenex) column at 45 °C and a flow of 0.5 mL/min. A gradient from eluent A (Millipore water with 0.1% formic acid [FA; Sigma-Aldrich]) to B (acetonitrile with 0.1% FA) was applied: 2%–30% B within 20 min, to 75% B within 9 min.</p><p><br></p><p><strong><em>References:</em></strong></p><p>[<strong>1</strong>] Schweiger, R., Castells, E., Da Sois, L., Martínez-Vilalta, J. &amp; Müller, C. (2021) Highly species-specific foliar metabolomes of diverse woody species and relationships with the leaf economics spectrum. Cells, 10, 644.</p>	<p>The Bruker Compact QTOF was run in positive electrospray ionisation mode at 1 Hz, using a <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> (mass-to-charge) range of 50–1300. Some samples were measured again to obtain MS/MS spectra (AutoMSMS, collision gas N2). A sodium formate calibrant was introduced prior to each sample.</p>	<p>Data were analysed in DataAnalysis 4.4 (Bruker Daltonics). The <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> axis was recalibrated, and metabolic features (characterised by <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>, RT) were quantified via their peak heights: Find Molecular Features tool, spectral background subtraction, signal-to-noise ratio 2, correlation coefficient threshold 0.75 and minimum compound length 4. Features probably representing the same metabolite were sorted together in ‘buckets’, using the feature with the highest intensity for quantification. Features were aligned across samples (ProfileAnalysis 2.3, Bruker Daltonics), considering only the RT range 1.2–29 min to exclude most primary metabolites. RT shifts of 0.1 min and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> shifts of 6 mDa were allowed. Only features were retained, which occurred in minimum half of the samples of at least one group and which had an average peak height of at least 1000 in minimum one group. Peak heights were divided by those of the [M + H]+ ion of hydrocortisone. Those features were left in the data set, which showed a mean intensity at least five times higher than the averaged blanks in at least one group. For those features, extracted ion chromatograms (EICs; DataAnalysis, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> ± 0.01Da) were used to discard features with bad peak shapes. Peak heights of the remaining features were manually determined based on these EICs to ensure proper quantification of low-intensity features in all samples. If there was evidence that a feature was an in-source fragment, only the precursor was retained. Finally, the peak heights of the manually quantified features were divided by the corresponding peak heights of hydrocortisone, using the manually picked EIC of its [M + H]+ ion. Because phloem exudation rates may have differed between the treatment groups, relative metabolite concentrations (0%–100%) were used.</p>	<p>Putative molecular formulas were assigned (Smart Formula [3D], DataAnalysis), considering only suggestions with a good isotope pattern fit (mSigma &lt;20) and a <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> deviation ≤ 5 mDa. Only meaningful molecular formulas were retained, that is, those with reasonable element numbers/ratios according to the rules 1, 4, 5 (common range) and 6 in Kind and Fiehn 2007 [<strong>1</strong>], filtering also for the P/O ratio (0–0.34; suggested by Kessler et al. 2014 [<strong>2</strong>]); implemented in MetaboScape 2021b, Bruker Daltonics). RT, MS and MS/MS spectra were compared with the standards of an in-house database.</p><p><br></p><p><strong><em>References:</em></strong></p><p>[<strong>1</strong>] Kind, T. &amp; Fiehn, O. (2007) Seven Golden Rules for heuristic filtering of molecular formulas obtained by accurate mass spectrometry. BMC Bioinformatics, 8, 1–20.</p><p>[<strong>2</strong>] Kessler, N., Walter, F., Persicke, M., Albaum, S.P., Kalinowski, J., Goesmann, A. et al. (2014) ALLocator: an interactive web platform for the analysis of metabolomic LC-ESI-MS datasets, enabling semiautomated, user-revised compound annotation and mass isotopomer ratio analysis. PLoS One, 9, e113909.</p>

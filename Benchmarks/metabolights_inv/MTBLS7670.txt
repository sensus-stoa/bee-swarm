Study Identifier	MTBLS7670
Study Title	Comparative characterization of the infant gut microbiome and their maternal lineage by a multi-omics approach
Study Description	<p>The human gut microbiome establishes and matures during infancy, and dysregulation at this stage may lead to pathologies later in life. We conducted a multi-omics study comprising three generations of family members to investigate the early development of the gut microbiota. Fecal samples from 200 individuals, including infants (0-12 months old; 55% females, 45% males) and their respective mothers and grandmothers, were analyzed using two independent metabolomics platforms and metagenomics. For metabolomics, gas chromatography and capillary electrophoresis – using multisegment injection – coupled to mass spectrometry were applied. For metagenomics, both 16S rRNA gene and shotgun sequencing were performed. Here we show that infants greatly vary from their elders in fecal microbiota populations, function, and metabolome. Infants have a less diverse microbiota than adults and present differences in several metabolite classes, such as short- and branched-chain fatty acids, which are associated with shifts in bacterial populations. These findings provide innovative biochemical insights into the shaping of the gut microbiome within the same generational line that could be beneficial in improving childhood health outcomes.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Participants were recruited at Hospital Universitario Infantil Niño Jesús, Hospital General Universitario Gregorio Marañón and five Health Centers in Madrid (Spain). All participants provided informed consent. The final study population consisted of <strong>200 participants</strong>: <strong>69 Infants</strong> up to 12 mth of age, their <strong>67 Mothers</strong> and their <strong>64 maternal Grandmothers</strong>.</p><p>All participating centers applied the same recruitment protocols and questionnaires. Information on age, sex, mode of birth, antibiotics use at birth, cow’s milk allergy and feeding regime for Infants, and age and smoking habits of Mothers and Grandmothers were collected. This data is presented in <strong>Table S11</strong> of the paper associated with this study, along with the family number and the techniques and datasets in which each sample was measured and/or included.</p><p><br></p><p>Each participant received a sample collection kit developed by our group consisting of a small disposable cooler, a hermetic bag, a stool collection sterile container with a spoon for easier collection, a cold-pack, collection paper, a pair of gloves, labels, and an easy-to-read guideline that patients could follow at home. Subjects were asked to record the sample collection date, to not contaminate the sample with urine or toilet paper and to store the samples in the home freezer at -20 °C before taking them to the laboratory. A total of 200 fecal samples were collected at the laboratory and stored at -80 °C before being processed.</p><p><br></p><p>Samples were analyzed using Gas Chromatography-QTOF-Mass spectrometry (<strong>GC-QTOF-MS</strong>) and Multisegment injection (MSI) capillary electrophoresis coupled to TOF mass spectrometry analysis (<strong>MSI-CE-TOF-MS</strong>).</p>	<p>The protocol for sample preparation was adapted from a previously published method<strong>[1]</strong>, with some modifications. Briefly, feces samples were lyophilized overnight at -110 °C in a LyoQest -85 lyophilizer (Telstar) until completely dried. Then, the lyophilizate from each individual was homogenized mechanically. Samples were taken as quickly as possible from the -80 °C freezer to avoid any thawing that would interfere with the lyophilization.</p><p><strong>&nbsp;</strong></p><p><strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>Extraction of samples for GC-QTOF-MS analysis</strong></p><p>10 mg of lyophilized feces were mixed with <strong>300 μL of NaOH (1M)</strong> solution in Milli-Q water and <strong>10 μL of 1000 ppm 4-methyl valeric acid (4MV) solution</strong> <strong>(IS1)</strong>, in a 0.5 mL Eppendorf tube and vortexed vigorously for 2 min. Samples were then centrifuged at 16,000 rcf at 4 °C for 20 min. 200 μL of supernatant was transferred into an autosampler vial, and the residue was further extracted with <strong>200 μL of cold methanol</strong>. After repeating the homogenization and centrifugation as above, 167 μL of supernatant was combined with the first supernatant in the sample vial. Blank samples were prepared in the same way, omitting the 10 mg of lyophilizate. To derivatize the extracted metabolites, <strong>20 μL of MCF</strong> were added to each vial, as well as <strong>34 μL of pyridine</strong>. The samples were then vortexed vigorously for exactly 30 s. Another <strong>20 μL of MCF</strong> were added again, and samples were vortexed for another 30 s. Subsequently, <strong>400 μL of 20 ppm tricosane (TRI) solution in chloroform (IS2) </strong>were added. Samples were vortexed for 30 s, followed by an addition of<strong> 400 μL of sodium bicarbonate solution (NaHCO3, 50 mM in Milli-Q water)</strong> and additional vortexing for 30 s. Samples were then centrifuged at 2000 rcf for 20 min at 4 °C in order to clearly visualize the double meniscus. Finally, 350 μL of the bottom chloroformic phase were transferred to transparent GC vials containing <strong>∼100 mg of anhydrous sodium sulfate (Na2SO4)</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p>To be able to measure reliably all the samples from our cohort we divided them into three batches, according to the age group: batch 1) Mothers; batch 2) Grandmothers; and batch 3) Infants. The samples were randomly measured inside their batch. We decided to begin with the batch with the most sample quantity (Mothers), then Grandmothers, and finally the batch where sample quantity was limited (Infants), to assure that the analysts had enough experience and skill when analyzing the most valuable samples. To later be able to join these batches and compare the data between them, we devised a <strong>quality control (QC) strategy</strong>. To prepare the <strong>QC</strong> samples, 17 mg of 30 lyophilized samples were taken (10 random samples from each of the 3 age groups) and a <strong>pool of 510 mg</strong> was obtained. This pool was split in three parts, one for each batch (<strong>170 mg per batch</strong>). <strong>Inside each batch, 11 QC samples were prepared</strong>, by taking <strong>10 mg of the lyophilized faeces</strong> and following the same protocol as the samples. From these 11 QCs, 3 were used as equilibrium QCs at the start of the analysis (by injecting them 5 times), and the other 8 were placed in the worklist every 9-10 samples – whose order was randomized inside each batch. This assured that the batches could be joined later by using a normalization strategy.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Extraction of samples for MSI-CE-TOF-MS analysis</strong></p><p>Fecal extracts were prepared by taking 25 mg of feces lyophilizate and extracting with <strong>1 mL of H2O:ACN:IPA (10:6:5)</strong>. This extract was filtered through Micro centrifuge filters (0.45 μm Nylon, Costar) at 16,100 x g for 15 min at 4 °C. For this extract to be suitable for MSI-CE-TOF-MS analysis, 100 μl aliquots of each sample were dried in a N2 evaporator and reconstituted with <strong>50 μl of H2O:Methanol (70:30)</strong>, containing the corresponding internal standard (<strong>IS</strong>) – <strong>40 μM chloro-tyrosine (Cl-Tyr)</strong> as the <strong>IS for positive mode</strong> and <strong>1 mM 2-Naphthalene-sulfonic acid (NMS)</strong> as the<strong> IS for negative mode</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>MSI-CE-TOF-MS</strong> <strong>QC samples</strong> were prepared by pooling 5 μl of each sample in the study to a “Grand Pool”, from which 6 50 μl aliquots were prepared by the same protocol as the rest of the samples. These QC samples were measured throughout the run to monitor analytical variability and equipment performance.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Linjing Zhao, L., Ni, Y., Su, M., Li, H., Dong, F., Chen, W., Wei, R., Zhang, L., Guiraud, S. P., Martin, F-M., Rajani, C., Xie, G., Jia, W. 2017. High Throughput and Quantitative Measurement of Microbial Metabolome by Gas Chromatography/Mass Spectrometry Using Automated Alkyl Chloroformate Derivatization. Analytical Chemistry&nbsp;2017&nbsp;89&nbsp;(10), 5565-5577. doi:10.1021/acs.analchem.7b00660</p>	<p><strong>GC</strong></p><p>The GC system consisted of an<strong> Agilent 7693 Autosampler</strong> and an <strong>Agilent 7890B GC</strong>. GC-QTOF parameters were optimized from a previous published protocol<strong>[1],</strong> to increase the coverage of relevant SCFA by enabling the analysis of the most volatile SCFAs. 1 μL of each derivatized sample was injected using a splitless injection technique into a <strong>DB-5ms GC (0.25 µm, 0.25 mm x 30 m; 95% dimethyl-5% diphenyl-polysiloxane bonded and crosslinked with a 10 m integrated precolumn) capillary column (Agilent J&amp;W Scientific)</strong>. Helium as the carrier gas at a constant ﬂow rate of approx. 1 mL/min. The solvent delay time was set to 2.9 min. The optimized temperature gradient was the following: 30 °C held for 1 min, then increased at a rate of 10 °C/min up to 120 for 9 min, 20 °C/min to 320 °C for 10 min, and then held there for 2 min. The total time of analysis was 22 min.</p><p><br></p><p><strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>[1]</strong> Linjing Zhao, L., Ni, Y., Su, M., Li, H., Dong, F., Chen, W., Wei, R., Zhang, L., Guiraud, S. P., Martin, F-M., Rajani, C., Xie, G., Jia, W. 2017. High Throughput and Quantitative Measurement of Microbial Metabolome by Gas Chromatography/Mass Spectrometry Using Automated Alkyl Chloroformate Derivatization. Analytical Chemistry&nbsp;2017&nbsp;89&nbsp;(10), 5565-5577. doi: 10.1021/acs.analchem.7b00660</p>	<p><strong>GC-QTOF-MS</strong></p><p>Samples were analyzed using an<strong> Accurate-Mass Q-TOF (Agilent Technologies 7250)</strong> mass spectrometer. The temperatures of the injector, transfer interface, and ion source were set to 270 °C, 270 °C, and 220 °C, respectively. Electron impact ionization (70 eV) at the examined <strong>m/z range of 38−650</strong> was used. The acquisition rate was 20 spectra/s. To avoid signals from the solvents that could saturate the MS, the detector was turned off in the following retention time (RT) segments: 3.52-3.90 and 4.84-5.30.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>MSI-CE-TOF-MS</strong></p><p>A <strong>time-of-flight mass spectrometer 6230</strong> with a coaxial sheath liquid Jetstream electrospray ion source (<strong>Agilent Technologies</strong>) was used. The instrument was calibrated daily prior to starting any sample analysis and operated under positive and negative ion modes. Scanning mass range was&nbsp;<strong>m/z&nbsp;50-1700</strong> with a data acquisition rate of 500 ms/spectrum. Both profile/centroid raw data were stored in a ‘.d’ file format. The ESI settings were the same for both positive and negative ion mode: Capillary and nozzle voltage: 2000 V (ESI +/−), Nebulizer gas: 10 p.s.i, Drying gas flow: 8 L/min, Drying gas temperature: 300 °C, Sheath gas flow: 3.5 L/min, Sheath gas temperature: 195 °C, Voltage settings of fragmentor, skimmer and Oct1 RF are 120, 65 and 750 V, respectively. Instrument control and data acquisition were performed using the vendor software ‘<strong>Agilent’s MassHunter Workstation LC/MS Data Acquisition Software (B.06.01)</strong>’ according to the vendor guidelines.</p>	<p>The data treatment procedure was adapted from recently published protocols that have been rigorously tested and fine-tuned at <strong>CEMBIO[1]</strong>. <strong>GC-QTOF-MS</strong> data, peak detection and spectra processing algorithms were applied using <strong>MassHunter Software (Agilent Technologies)</strong>. The overall analytical performance was carefully examined by inspection of total ion chromatograms (TIC) of experimental samples, QC samples, blanks, and both internal standards (IS): 4MV and TRI. 4MV is the IS1 that is added before the derivatization and thus controls the reproducibility of the whole sample preparation process, while TRI is the IS2, added after the derivatization, and is used to control mostly the analytical performance of the <strong>GC-QTOF-MS</strong> equipment (drifts in response and performance of the injection process). The data files were then introduced into <strong>MassHunter Unknowns Analysis (B.10.0, Agilent)</strong> and the <strong>SureMass (Agilent Technologies)</strong> mass spectral deconvolution algorithm – specifically designed to work with accurate mass – was applied to identify co-eluted compounds. The algorithm reconstructs a pure mass spectrum for the components that the mass spectrometer observes according to their RTs. In a next step, the program matched these deconvoluted mass spectra together with the RTs to spectral libraries to assign an identification. We used a “Personal Compound Database and Library” (PCDL) built by analyzing chemical standards with the same method, and thus includes accurate mass in all detected m/z (mass-to-charge) fragments. Then, we performed a further search into the NIST20 library (National Institute of Standards and Technology, U.S. Department of Commerce), which includes only spectra of nominal mass. The details and parameters of these analyses are described in detail elsewhere<strong>[1]</strong>. After the deconvolution and library search, a <strong>CEF </strong>file<strong> </strong>was created for each sample with the hits with the highest match. Next, these <strong>CEF</strong> files were imported into <strong>Mass Profiler Professional (Agilent) </strong>to generate an alignment of all the different hits of all the samples. This alignment generated a <strong>CEF</strong> file for all samples that gathered all the compounds that were later quantified.</p><p><br></p><p>For obtaining the abundances of the metabolites, <strong>MassHunter Quantitative (B.10.0, Agilent) </strong>was used. First, the targeted ions selected from the corresponding spectrum of the library were carefully checked with the experimental data, avoiding saturated ions and discarding compounds derived from the solvents, derivatizing agents or column bleeding. This process allowed the better revision of compound annotation, which was performed by comparing mass fragmentation patterns with the <strong>NIST20 library</strong> using <strong>MassHunter Qualitative (B.08.00, Agilent) </strong>and the <strong>NIST MS Search v.2.4</strong> tool. To enhance annotation confidence, the RI of the features were calculated by interpolating their experimental RT into calibration files built with the n-alkanes mix measured at the beginning of every batch. The experimental RI were then matched with the values in the <strong>NIST20 library</strong>. Then, the integration method was applied, and peak areas were carefully checked and corrected manually if necessary for all compounds in samples, blanks and QCs. The result of these processes was an Excel file for each batch (Infants, Mothers, and Grandmothers) containing all the features integrated in the corresponding samples.</p><p><br></p><p>The following step was a blank subtraction, where the mean abundance of each metabolite in the blank samples was calculated and subtracted from the abundance of that metabolite in each sample. This process ensured that signals from the reagents, solvents, contaminants and derivatizing agents were removed. Then, a <strong>Quality Assurance procedure (QA)</strong> was performed<strong>[2][3]</strong>. First, we applied a filter by presence, where features that appeared in &lt;50% of QCs and &lt;70% of samples were removed. Then, we applied a filter by <strong>Relative Standard Deviation</strong> (RSD, also known as coefficient of variation, CV), where metabolites were kept if they had, in at least one batch, an RSD&lt;30% in the QC samples. All the above processes were performed independently for each batch. <strong>Normalization procedures</strong> were also carried out to ensure the comparison between the three batches. First, <strong>an intra-batch correction using “QC Sample – Support Vector Regression Correction” (QC-SVRC)[4]</strong>, and after the fusion of the three batches in a unique Excel, an <strong>inter-batch correction with QC-Norm[5][6]</strong> were applied. After these normalizations, the QA filters were carried out in the same way as before.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Rey-Stolle, F. et al. Low and high resolution gas chromatography-mass spectrometry for untargeted metabolomics: A tutorial. Anal. Chim. Acta 339043 (2021) doi:10.1016/j.aca.2021.339043.</p><p><strong>[2] </strong>Dudzik, D., Barbas-Bernardos, C., García, A. &amp; Barbas, C. Quality assurance procedures for mass spectrometry untargeted metabolomics. a review. J. Pharm. Biomed. Anal. 147, 149–173 (2018).</p><p><strong>[3] </strong>Godzien, J., Alonso-Herranz, V., Barbas, C. &amp; Armitage, E. G. Controlling the quality of metabolomics data: new strategies to get the best out of the QC sample. Metabolomics 11, 518–528 (2015).</p><p><strong>[4]</strong> Kuligowski, J., Sánchez-Illana, Á., Sanjuán-Herráez, D., Vento, M. &amp; Quintás, G. Intra-batch effect correction in liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry using quality control samples and support vector regression (QC-SVRC). The Analyst 140, 7810–7817 (2015).</p><p><strong>[5] </strong>Rodríguez-Coira, J. et al. Troubleshooting in Large-Scale LC-ToF-MS Metabolomics Analysis: Solving Complex Issues in Big Cohorts. Metabolites 9, (2019).</p><p><strong>[6] </strong>Broadhurst, D. et al. Guidelines and considerations for the use of system suitability and quality control samples in mass spectrometry assays applied in untargeted clinical metabolomic studies. Metabolomics 14, 72 (2018).</p>	<p><strong>GC-QTOF-MS</strong></p><p>The details and parameters of these analyses have been previously published[1]. As described before in detail, the identification of metabolites was performed using <strong>SureMass mass spectral deconvolution algorithm</strong> to identify co-eluted compounds and reconstruct a pure mass spectrum. These deconvoluted mass spectra were matched with the <strong>RTs</strong> of spectral libraries to assign an identification. A “<strong>Personal Compound Database and Library” (PCDL)</strong> which was built by analyzing chemical standards with the same method, and thus includes accurate mass in all detected m/z (mass-to-charge) fragments, was used. Then, we performed a further search into the <strong>NIST20 library (National Institute of Standards and Technology, U.S. Department of Commerce)</strong>, which includes only spectra of nominal mass. After this, manual revision of compound annotation was performed, by comparing mass fragmentation patterns with the <strong>NIST20 library</strong> using <strong>MassHunter Qualitative (B.08.00, Agilent)</strong> and the <strong>NIST MS Search v.2.4 tool</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>MSI-CE-TOF-MS - Targeted search of metabolites using in-house library </strong></p><p>Once the data were obtained, they were processed following the procedures detailed previously<strong>[2]</strong>. We selected 14 known metabolites and performed a targeted search to obtain their relative responses. Of these, 6 were in common with GC-QTOF-MS to check the accordance between both techniques, while the other 8 were crucial metabolites that had not been previously detected (see <span style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54); font-style: normal; font-weight: 700;'>Figure S4B</span> in the paper associated with this study). The metabolite selection was based on a literature search for relevant metabolites, the filtering of metabolites already detected by GC-MS and the quality (peak shape and dilution pattern) of the extracted ion electropherograms in the QCs. The QA procedures and statistical analyses were applied in the same way as for the GC-QTOF-MS data. Of note, GABA was not detected in all samples (total n= 160) due to isomeric interferences. Thus, a K-nearest neighbors (KNN) missing value imputation was carried out with a MATLAB script<strong style='font-style: inherit;'>[3]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong>Rey-Stolle, F., Dudzik, D., Gonzalez-Riano, C., Fernández-García, M., Alonso-Herranz, V., Rojo, D., Barbas, C., García, A. 2022. Missing value imputation strategies for metabolomics data: General. ELECTROPHORESIS 36, 3050–3060.<strong> </strong>PMID:&nbsp;26376450, doi:&nbsp;10.1002/elps.201500352</p>	<p><strong>MSI-CE</strong></p><p>Capillary electrophoresis-mass spectrometry (CE-MS) was applied, using a methodology developed by the Britz-McKibbin lab and published recently<strong>[1]</strong>. This methodology, called <strong>Multisegment Injection (MSI)-CE-MS</strong>, is especially designed for big cohorts of samples, as in the present case, and employs multiplexed injections in the CE system to allow for a significant reduction in analysis time. Samples were measured in a <strong>G7100A CE equipment</strong>, as detailed in a previous published method<strong>[1]</strong>. An LC isocratic pump is used to deliver sheath liquid (0.1% (vol/vol) formic acid in 60:40 (MeOH:H2O) for cationic analysis under positive ion mode, and 50:50 (MeOH:H2O) for anionic analysis under negative ion mode) using a rate of 10 µL/min to the CE-MS coaxial sheath liquid sprayer. 135 cm of a <strong>10 m of fused-silica capillary with an internal diameter of 50 µm and outer diameter of 363 µm (Polymicro Technologies, No. 1068150017)</strong>, was preconditioned and used for the analysis of each sample as previously detailed<strong>[1]</strong>. The samples were injected twice, following an asymmetric dilution pattern that allowed for the better identification of peaks. Since each run was able to accommodate 13 injections (12 samples + QC), the total number of runs was 31. The sample preparation and analysis in the equipment were performed in the <strong>Britz-McKibbin lab, at McMaster University (Hamilton, ON, Canada)</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Shanmuganathan, M., Kroezen, Z., Gill, B.&nbsp;&nbsp;Azab, S., de Souza R.J., Teo, K.K., Atkinson, S., Subbarao, P., Desai, D., Anand, S.S., Britz-McKibbin, P. 2021.

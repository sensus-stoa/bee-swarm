Study Identifier	MTBLS7676
Study Title	The timing of bacterial mesophyll infection shapes the leaf chemical landscape during the interaction
Study Description	<p>Chemistry in eukaryotic intercellular spaces is shaped by both hosts and symbiotic microorganisms such as bacteria. Pathogenic microorganisms like barley associated Xanthomonas translucens (Xt) swiftly overtake the inner leaf tissue becoming the dominant microbial community member during disease development. The dynamic metabolic changes due to Xt pathogenesis in the mesophyll spaces remain unknown. Genomic group I of Xt consists of 2 barley-infecting lineages: pathovar <em>translucens</em> (Xtt) and pathovar <em>undulosa</em> (Xtu). Xtu and Xtt, although genomically distinct, cause similar water-soaked lesions. To define the metabolic signals associated with inner leaf colonization, we used untargeted metabolomics to characterize Xtu and Xtt metabolism signatures associated with mesophyll growth. We found that mesophyll apoplast fluid from infected tissue yielded a distinct metabolic profile and shift from catabolic to anabolic processes over time compared to water-infiltrated control. The pathways with the most differentially expressed metabolites by time were glycolysis, tricarboxylic acid cycle, sucrose metabolism, pentoses interconversion, amino acids, galactose, and purine metabolism. Hierarchical clustering and principal components analysis showed that metabolic changes were more affected by the time point rather than by the individual colonization of the inner leaves by Xtt compared to Xtu. Overall, in this study, we identified metabolic pathways that explain carbon and nitrogen usage during host-bacterial interactions over time for mesophyll tissue colonization. This foundational research provides initial insights into shared metabolic strategies of inner leaf colonization niche occupation by related but phylogenetically distinct phyllosphere bacteria.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Barley plants</strong></p><p>The <strong>barley cultivar Morex</strong> was used for in-planta studies. All plants were sown at 20 °C and 60% humidity in Promix soil. Plants were grown for 3 wk before inoculation and had to have at least 2 fully developed leaves.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Bacterial cultures </strong></p><p><strong>﻿Xtu </strong>(<em>Xanthomonas translucens</em>&nbsp;pv.&nbsp;<em>undulosa</em>) and <strong>Xtt </strong>(<em>Xanthomonas translucens&nbsp;</em>pv. <em>translucens</em>) were taken out of -80 °C glycerol stocks and plated on Nutrient Agar media (3 g/L beef extract, 5 g/L peptone, 15 g/L agar) 1 wk before the experiment and incubated at 28 °C. 3 d before the experiment, a single bacteria colony was plated again on Nutrient Agar and incubated at 28 °C. On the day of inoculation, cultures taken and diluted in water to an absorbance of 0.1 at 600 nm (10^8 CFU/mL).</p><p><br></p><p><strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>Inoculation and </strong><strong>Apoplast collection</strong></p><p>The youngest fully expanded leaves of the 3-wk old plants were inoculated with either 1-2mL of <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>water</strong>, <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>Xtu</strong>, or <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>Xtt</strong><span style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>, using</span> a 1 mL needleless syringe. After <strong>6 h</strong>, <strong>12 h</strong>, and<strong> 24 h post-inoculation</strong>, leaves were collected<strong> </strong>and cleaned using 75% ethanol to eliminate any surface microorganisms, with 4 leaves being pooled per biological replicate. Leaves were cut at the base for fluid extractions and then weighed before and after vacuum infiltration with a 10 ppm ribitol solution, to determine the amount of water infiltrated (with water density assumed to be 1g/ml). A previously described<span style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54); font-weight: 700;'>[1] </span>protocol for mesophyll fluid extraction and cytoplasmic contamination test was used. At each time point, 2 µL of apoplastic fluid from each sample was plated in Nutrient Agar, to count the colony-forming units and ensure that all samples had the same number of bacteria. After that, fluids were flash-frozen to avoid metabolite degradation. For analysis, 4 pools with similar number of bacteria were selected from each time point.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Roman-Reyna, V., Rathjen, J.P. (2017). Apoplastic Sugar Extraction and Quantification from Wheat Leaves Infected with Biotrophic Fungi. In: Periyannan, S. (eds) Wheat Rust Diseases. Methods in Molecular Biology, vol 1659. Humana Press, New York, NY. DOI:10.1007/978-1-4939-7249-4_11.</p>	<p><strong>Polar metabolites extraction</strong>:</p><p>Around 500 µL of apoplast was mixed with 1mL of (5:2:2 MeOH:ClNa2:H2O).&nbsp;The sample was vortexed and incubated 5 min at 28 °C in a shaker (incubator). Samples were then centrifuged 15 min at 2200 g, the methanol phase was collected and 200 µL were sent to the <strong>West Coast Metabolomics Center</strong> (UC Davis) for analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Derivatization</strong></p><p>Samples were extracted using 1mL of 3:3:2 ACN:IPA:H2O (v/v/v) and derivatized as described previously<strong>[1]</strong>. Thus, after extraction, half of the sample was dried to completeness and then <strong>10 µL of 40 mg/mL of</strong> <strong>methoxyamine</strong> <strong>in pyridine </strong>(silylation grade) were added to protect aldehyde and ketone groups, and the mixture was shaken at 30°C for 90 min. Then 91 µL of N-methyl-N-trimethylsilyltrifluoroacetamide with 1% trimethylchlorosilane (<strong>MSTFA/1% TMCS</strong>) and reference FAMEs were added to each sample and shaken at 37°C for 30 min to finish derivatization. The reaction mixture was transferred to a 2 ml clear glass auto-sampler vial with micro-insert, capped, and injected into the instrument.</p><p><br></p><p>2 sets of <strong>QCs</strong> were prepared by pooling 1) equal volumes of all 6h and 12h samples (pool and pool1) and 2) equal volumes of all 24h samples (Pool_1 and Pool_2).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Fiehn, O., Wohlgemuth, G., Scholz, M., Kind, T., Lee, D.Y., Lu, Y., Moon, S. and Nikolau, B. 2008. Quality control for plant metabolomics: reporting MSI-compliant studies. The Plant Journal, 53: 691-704.&nbsp;DOI:10.1111/j.1365-313X.2007.03387.x</p>	<p>Samples were analyzed by the <strong>West Coast Metabolomics Center</strong>. Chromatographic separation was performed using an <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>Agilent 7890A GC </strong><span style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>and</span><strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'><em> </em></strong>a <strong>Rtx-5Sil MS</strong> column with a 10 m integrated guard column (95% dimethyl/5% diphenyl polysiloxane film; 0.25 µm, 0.25 mm x 30 m; Restek). 0.5 µL of the derivatized samples were injected in splitless mode. The GC oven temperature program is as follows: initial temperature was held constant at 45 °C for 2 min and then ramped to 300 °C at 20 °C/min. It was held constant at 300 °C for 2 min, then ramped to 330 °C at 20 °C/min, keeping it constant for 0.5 min at this temperature. The carrier gas (99.9999% He) flow is held constant at 1 mL/min.&nbsp;</p>	<p>Samples were sent to the <strong>West Coast Metabolomics Center (UC Davis)</strong> for GC-TOF MS analysis. Analysis were performed using a <strong>LECO Pegasus IV</strong> mass spectrometer with electron impact ionization at 70 V and ion source temperature of 250 °C. Mass spectra were acquired in a range of&nbsp;<strong><em>m</em>/<em>z</em>&nbsp;85-500</strong> with unit mass resolution at 17 spectra/s and 1800 V detector voltage. The temperature for the transfer line between gas chromatograph and mass spectrometer was set to 280 °C.</p><p><br></p>	<p>The data is given as peak heights for the quantification ion (mz value) at the specific retention index. <strong>West Coast Metabolomics Center</strong> provided a variant of a ‘vector normalization’ in which the sum of all peak heights for all identified metabolites for each sample is calculated (named <strong>mTIC</strong>), with only genuine metabolites (identified compounds) being used. Data is normalized to the total average <strong>mTIC, </strong> to normalize data to the ‘total metabolite content’, disregarding unknowns that might potentially comprise artifact peaks or chemical contaminants.</p>	<p>Metabolite identification was performed by <strong>West Coast Metabolomic Center</strong>. <strong>ChromaTOF version 2.32</strong> was used for data preprocessing without smoothing, 3 s peak width, baseline subtraction just above the noise level, and automatic mass spectral deconvolution and peak detection at signal/noise levels of 5:1 throughout the chromatogram. Apex masses are reported for use in the <strong>BinBase </strong>algorithm<strong>[1]</strong>. Result <strong>*.txt</strong> files are exported to a data server with absolute spectra intensities and further processed by a filtering algorithm implemented in the metabolomics <strong>BinBase</strong> database. Remaining pre-processing details are as previously described<strong>[2]</strong>. Peaks were annotated and identified based on retention times and mass spectra using the MassBank of North America (<a href='https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>MoNA</a>) repository, with <a href='https://www.genome.jp/kegg/kegg1.html' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>KEGG</a> IDs being provided.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Skogerson, K., Wohlgemuth, G., Barupal, D.K.&nbsp;<em>et al.</em>&nbsp;The volatile compound BinBase mass spectral database.&nbsp;<em>BMC Bioinformatics</em>&nbsp;12, 321 (2011). DOI:10.1186/1471-2105-12-321</p><p><strong>[2] </strong>Fiehn, O., Wohlgemuth, G., Scholz, M., Kind, T., Lee, D.Y., Lu, Y., Moon, S. and Nikolau, B. 2008. Quality control for plant metabolomics: reporting MSI-compliant studies. The Plant Journal, 53: 691-704.&nbsp;DOI:10.1111/j.1365-313X.2007.03387.x</p>

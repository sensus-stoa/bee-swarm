Study Identifier	MTBLS779
Study Title	Comparative analysis of proteomic and metabolomic profiles of different species of Paris
Study Description	<p>An extract prepared from species of Paris is the most widely consumed herbal product in China. The genus Paris includes a variety of genotypes with different medicinal component contents but only two are defined as official sources. Closely related species have different medicinal properties because of differential expression of proteins and metabolites. To better understand the molecular basis of these differences, we examined proteomic and metabolomic changes in rhizomes of P. polyphylla var. chinensis, P. polyphylla var. yunnanensis, and P. fargesii var. fargesii using a technique known as sequential window acquisition of all theoretical mass spectra as well as gas chromatography-time-of-flight mass spectrometry. In total, 419 proteins showed significant abundance changes, and 33 metabolites could be used to discriminate Paris species. A complex analysis of proteomic and metabolomic data revealed a higher efficiency of sucrose utilization and an elevated protein abundance in the sugar metabolic pathway of P. polyphylla var. chinensis. The pyruvate content and efficiency of acetyl-CoA-utilization in saponin biosynthesis were also higher in P. polyphylla var. chinensis than in the other two species. The results expand our understanding of the proteome and metabolome of Paris and offer new insights into the species-specific traits of these herbaceous plants.</p><p><strong>SIGNIFICANCE:</strong> The traditional Chinese medicine Paris is the most widely consumed herbal product for the treatment of joint pain, rheumatoid arthritis and antineoplastic. All Paris species have roughly the same morphological characteristics; however, different members have different medicinal compound contents. Efficient exploitation of genetic diversity is a key factor in the development of rare medicinal plants with improved agronomic traits and malleability to challenging environmental conditions. Nevertheless, only a partial understanding of physiological and molecular mechanisms of different plants of Paris can be achieved without proteomics. To better understand the molecular basis of these differences and facilitate the use of other Paris species, we examine proteomic metabolomic changes in rhizomes of Paris using the technique known as SWATH-MS and GC/TOF-MS. Our research has provided information that can be used in other studies to compare metabolic traits in different Paris species. Our findings can also serve as a theoretical basis for the selection and cultivation of other Paris species with a higher medicinal value.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>In this study, we used rhizome samples of PPC, PPY, and PFF collected from an experimental field with the average monthly temperature of approximately 18–28 °C in Enshi Prefecture, Hubei Province, China. The identity of all plant materials was confirmed by Professors Dingrong Wan and Hong Liu of the South-Central University for Nationalities. The 3 species were grown simultaneously in the experimental field in 3 plots under identical environmental conditions, with field management applied to reduce any environmental impacts. In particular, soil moisture was maintained between 30% and 40%, and a sunshade net with a coverage of 70% was used during plant growth. Plants were grown in two-row plots of 6.0 x 1.4 m, with 0.8-m spacing between beds and 0.3-m spacing between plants. After 4 years of growth, plants with no pests or diseases and a height of approximately 0.7 m were selected for study. The rhizomes of each species were harvested in September from 10 different plants in the 3 experimental plots to normalize growth and developmental effects. Relevant information on the morphology of different Paris species can be found in Fig. S1 of the paper associated with this study.</p>	<p>10 rhizome tissue samples of each species were separately ground with liquid nitrogen for analysis. The powdered plant material (200 mg) was first vortexed with 8 ml methanol for 3 min and then subjected to ultrasonication for 1 h at room temperature<strong>[1]</strong>. Methoxyamine hydrochloride (20 mg/ml) was prepared with pyridine, and 0.2 mg/ml ribitol was prepared by dissolving ribitol in water and used as an internal standard[2]. The derivatization protocol was similar to our previously published methods<strong>[2]</strong>. First, oximation reactions were performed to protect ketones and aldehydes of compounds using methoxyamine hydrochloride solution (40 ml; 20 mg/ml, in pyridine) at 130 rpm and 30 °C for 90 min. To increase compound volatilities, trimethylsilylation of acidic protons was then carried out using N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl)-trifluoroacetamide solution at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, the derivatized samples were stored at 4 °C before detection.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Lee J, Jung Y, Shin JH, Kim HK, Moon BC, Ryu DH, Hwang GS. Secondary metabolite profiling of Curcuma species grown at different locations using GC/TOF and UPLC/Q-TOF MS. Molecules. 2014 Jul 4;19(7):9535-51. doi:10.3390/molecules19079535. PMID:25000465</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Cheng J, Che N, Li H, Ma K, Wu S, Fang J, Gao R, Liu J, Yan X, Li C, Dong F. J Sep Sci. 2013 Apr;36(8):1418-28. doi:10.1002/jssc.201201158. PMID:23526673</p>	<p>GC–MS analyses were performed on an Agilent 7890 gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a LECO Pegasus 4D TOF/MS detector and coupled to a Chroma TOF 4.50 workstation and a NIST mass spectral database (LECO, Saint Joseph, MI, USA). A 1-μl aliquot of each sample was injected onto a DB-5MS column (0.25 µm, 0.25 mm x 30 m, Agilent). The oven temperature was initially set to 70 °C for 1 min and then increased to 280 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min for 10 min. Helium was the carrier gas with a flow rate of 1 ml/min. The temperature of the secondary column was kept 10 °C higher than that of the primary column, and the modulator temperature offset was 15 °C higher than that of the secondary column. Temperatures of the inlet, transfer interface, and ion source were 250, 250, and 220 °C, respectively.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometry measurements were made on a LECO Pegasus 4D TOF/MS instrument with electron impact ionization (70 eV) in full scan mode (m/z 50–800) at an acquisition rate of 10 spectra/s. Compounds were identified by searching against the NIST database (version 15.0). To obtain technical replicates, each sample was measured 3 times under the same conditions.</p>	<p>All GC/TOF-MS data were further processed using the statistical compare function of Chroma TOF 4.5 software for data pretreatment, including baseline correction, de-noising, smoothing and alignment, time-window splitting, and peak feature extraction (based on multivariate curve resolution algorithm). Peaks with a signal-to-noise ratio &gt;100 were included and normalized to the intensity of ribitol.</p>	<p>Metabolites were identified using the NIST library and the library constructed with various metabolite standards in our laboratory; a similarity &gt;800 and characteristic fragment ions were considered as identification factors. The resulting 3-D matrix, organized by peak retention time, sample information (observations), and peak intensity information (variables), was normalized and then introduced into MetaboAnalyst 3.0 software (www.metaboanalyst.ca) for principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA)<strong>[1]</strong>. In addition, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukeyʼs multiple comparison test was performed using PASW Statistics 17 (SPSS, Inc.) to reveal differences between relative contents of individual metabolites in the 3 species.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Xia J and Wishart DS. Using MetaboAnalyst 3.0 for Comprehensive Metabolomics Data Analysis. Curr Protoc Bioinformatics. 2016 Sep 7;55:14.10.1-14.10.91. doi:10.1002/cpbi.11. PMID:27603023</p>

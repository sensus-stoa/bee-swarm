Study Title	Delayed gut microbiota maturation in the first year of life is a hallmark of pediatric allergic disease
Study Description	Allergic diseases affect millions of people worldwide. An increase in their prevalence has been associated with alterations in the gut microbiome, i.e., the microorganisms and their genes within the gastrointestinal tract. Maturation of the infant immune system and gut microbiota occur in parallel; thus, the conformation of the microbiome may determine if tolerant immune programming arises within the infant. Here we show, using deeply phenotyped participants in the CHILD birth cohort (n = 1115), that there are early-life influences and microbiome features which are uniformly associated with four distinct allergic diagnoses at 5 years: atopic dermatitis (AD, n = 367), asthma (As, n = 165), food allergy (FA, n = 136), and allergic rhinitis (AR, n = 187). In a subset with shotgun metagenomic and metabolomic profiling (n = 589), we discover that impaired 1-year microbiota maturation may be universal to pediatric allergies (AD p = 0.000014; As p = 0.0073; FA p = 0.00083; and AR p = 0.0021). Extending this, we find a core set of functional and metabolic imbalances characterized by compromised mucous integrity, elevated oxidative activity, decreased secondary fermentation, and elevated trace amines, to be a significant mediator between microbiota maturation at age 1 year and allergic diagnoses at age 5 years (β<sub>indirect</sub> = -2.28; p = 0.0020). Microbiota maturation thus provides a focal point to identify deviations from normative development to predict and prevent allergic disease.

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong>Study cohort:</strong></p><p>The <strong>CHILD</strong> Study is a multi-center longitudinal, prospective, general population birth cohort study following infants from pregnancy to age 5 yr, and beyond. With enrollment beginning in 2008 and closing in 2012, a total of <strong>3621 pregnant women</strong> from 4 cities (Vancouver, Edmonton, Winnipeg, Toronto) across Canada enrolled along with <strong>eligible infants (n = 3455)</strong> that had no congenital abnormalities and were born at a minimum of 34 wk of gestation<strong>[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p>All infants enrolled in the <strong>CHILD</strong> protocol were administered a <strong>skin prick test (SPT)</strong> at their 1-, 3-, and 5-yr scheduled visits. Children were then diagnosed with IgE-mediated allergic sensitization (also referred to as atopy) based on <strong>SPT</strong> to multiple common foods and environmental inhalant allergens, using ≥2 mm average wheal size as indicating a positive test relative to the negative control. The allergens tested at all 1-, 3-, and 5-yr visits include <strong>cat hair</strong>, the <strong>German cockroach, <em>Alternaria tenuis</em></strong>, <strong>house dust mites (<em>Dermatophagoides psteronyssinus</em> and <em>Dermatophagoides farnae</em>)</strong>, <strong>dog epithelium</strong>, <strong>cow’s milk</strong>, <strong>peanut</strong>, <strong>egg white</strong>, and <strong>soybean</strong>. In addition to these, participants at 3- and 5-yr visits were tested with<strong><em> Cladosporium</em></strong>, <strong><em>Penicillium</em></strong>, <strong><em>Aspergillus fumigatus</em></strong>, <strong>trees</strong>, <strong>grass</strong>, <strong>weeds</strong>, and <strong>ragweed</strong>. Glycerin and histamine served as the negative and positive controls, respectively.</p><p><br></p><p>The primary outcomes of our study were <strong>atopic dermatitis</strong>, <strong>asthma</strong>, <strong>food allergy</strong>, and <strong>allergic rhinitis</strong> diagnosed as <strong>(Yes/Possible/No)</strong>, using history and physical examination in combination with <strong>SPT,</strong> by an expert study physician at the clinical assessment at the age of 5 yr based on our published approach<strong>[2].</strong> For this study, children were considered to have allergic diseases only if the response was ‘<strong>Yes</strong>’ to a clinical inquiry of whether they had <strong>atopic dermatitis</strong>, <strong>asthma</strong>, <strong>food allergy</strong>, and/or <strong>allergic rhinitis</strong>. <strong>Non-allergic controls</strong> were limited to children with ‘<strong>No</strong>’ responses for 5-yr diagnoses, negative allergen <strong>SPTs</strong> at 1, 3, and 5 yr, and no history of wheezing at 1, 3, and 5 yr. Within the current study, we analyzed the data in a subset of <strong>CHILD</strong> that contained data for parent and child questionnaires, <strong>SPT</strong> results, and physician diagnoses at 5 yr for <strong>cases</strong> and 1, 3, and 5 yr for <strong>controls</strong> (<strong>n = 1115</strong>, see <strong>Fig. 1b</strong> and <strong>Table 1S</strong> in the paper associated with the study).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Stool sample collection:</strong></p><p>Sample collection and sequencing were performed as previously described<strong>[1][3]</strong>. Specifically, stool samples from diapers were collected at a home visit at around 3 mth [mean (SD), 3.8 (1.1) mth] and a clinic visit at around 1 yr [mean (SD), 12.5 (1.6) mth]. Samples were briefly stored at 4 °C and then aliquoted into 4 x 2-ml cryovials using a stainless steel depyrogenated spatula and were frozen at −80 °C. <strong>CHILD</strong> recorded the time between stool collection and long-term storage, and this processing time was adjusted for in our statistical analysis of metabolic profiles.</p><p><br></p><p>Metabolic profiles were created from the same sequenced stool samples at <strong>The Metabolomics Innovation Center (TMIC)</strong> in Edmonton, Alberta using 2 separate assays. <strong>Targeted nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis</strong> of <strong>31 metabolites</strong> was performed across <strong>62 batches[4][5]</strong>. <strong>Targeted liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis</strong> of <strong>590 metabolites</strong> was performed using <strong>TMIC’s Microbiome Metabolism (MEGA)</strong> assay across <strong>27 batches[6][7]</strong>. <strong>NMR</strong> and <strong>LC-MS/MS</strong> precision were confirmed to be &lt;5 and &lt;10% <strong>coefficients of variability (CV)</strong>, respectively. Additionally, overlapping metabolites detected by both methods were cross-checked to confirm the accuracies of the reported concentration values.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Moraes TJ, Lefebvre DL, Chooniedass R, Becker AB, Brook JR, Denburg J, HayGlass KT, Hegele RG, Kollmann TR, Macri J, Mandhane PJ, Scott JA, Subbarao P, Takaro TK, Turvey SE, Duncan JD, Sears MR, Befus AD; CHILD Study Investigators. The fat-soluble extracts were used for the analysis of lipids, and all other metabolites were from the water-soluble extracts.</p>	<p><strong>LC-MS/MS analysis</strong> was performed on a Agilent 1290 series UHPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA) coupled to an ABSciex 5500 QTrap tandem mass spectrometry instrument (Applied Biosystems/MDS Analytical Technologies, Foster City, CA). Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent reversed-phase Zorbax Eclipse XDB C18 column (3.0 mm x 100 mm, 3.5 μm particle size, 80 Å pore size) with a Phenomenex (Torrance, CA) SecurityGuard C18 guard column (4.0 mm x 3.0 mm).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>UPLC-MS/MS POS:</strong></p><p>The UHPLC parameters used for the <strong>LC-MS/MS analysis</strong> of a<strong>mino acids and derivatives</strong> as well as <strong>biogenic amines and derivatives</strong> were as follows - solvent A: 0.2% (v/v) formic acid in water, and solvent B: 0.2% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient profile for this UHPLC solvent run was as follows: t = 0 min, 0% B; t = 0.5 min, 0% B; t = 5.5 min, 95% B; t = 6.5 min, 95% B; t = 7.0 min, 0% B; and t = 9.5 min, 0% B. The column oven was set at 50 °C. The flow rate was 500 μl/min, and the sample injection volume was 10 μl.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>UPLC-MS/MS NEG:</strong></p><p>For the <strong>analysis of organic acids</strong> by<strong> LC-MS/MS</strong>, the solvents used were (A) 0.01% (v/v) formic acid in water and (B) 0.01% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient profile was as follows: t = 0 min, 25% B; t = 6.0 min, 65% B; t = 6.3 min, 90% B; t = 6.5 min, 100% B; t = 7.0 min, 100% B; t = 7.5 min, 25% B; t = 12.0 min, 25% B. The column oven was set to 40 °C. The flow rate was 400 μl/min, and the sample injection volume was 10 μl.</p><p><br></p>	<p>Mass spectrometric analysis was performed on an ABSciex 5500 QTrap tandem mass spectrometry instrument (Applied Biosystems/MDS Analytical Technologies, Foster City, CA)</p><p><br></p><p><strong>UPLC-MS/MS POS:</strong></p><p>For the <strong>LC-MS/MS analysis</strong> of <strong>amino acids and derivatives</strong> as well as <strong>biogenic amines and derivatives</strong>, the mass spectrometer was set to a positive electrospray ionization mode with a scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) scan. The IonSpray voltage was set at 5500 V and the temperature at 500 °C. The curtain gas (CUR), ion source gas 1 (GAS1), ion source gas 2 (GAS2), and collision gas (CAD) were set at 20, 40, 50, and medium, respectively. The entrance potential (EP) was set to 15 V. The declustering potential (DP), collision energy (CE), collision cell exit potential (CXP), MRM precursor ion (Q1), and fragment ion (Q3) were optimized and set individually for each analyte and isotope-labeled ISTD.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>UPLC-MS/MS NEG:</strong></p><p>For the <strong>LC-MS/MS analysis</strong> of <strong>organic acids</strong>, the mass spectrometer was set to a negative electrospray ionization mode with scheduled MRM scanning. The IonSpray voltage was set at −4500 V and the temperature at 400 °C. The CUR, GAS1, GAS2, and CAD were set at 20, 30, 30 and medium, separately. The EP was set at −10 V, and the DP, CE, CXP, Q1, and Q3 were optimized and set individually for all the analytes and isotope-labeled ISTDs.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>UPLC-MS/MS ALL:</strong></p><p>For the <strong>LC-MS analysis</strong>, calibration curves using standards for each batch of analysis were used, looking for the specific known analytes and using their calibration curves to calculate concentrations. There were 7 calibration points and the calibrants were run as a mixture. Calibrants were prepared in matrix-free solution. Whether or not there was a significant carry-over was observed by comparing between the Cal7 injection and the following double blank injection. Individual standards were used for all reported metabolites. Identifications were done with respect to standards and MRM transitions.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>FIA-MS/MS:</strong></p><p><strong>DFI-MS/MS analysis</strong> of <strong>lipids</strong>, <strong>acylcarnitines</strong> and <strong>glucose</strong> was carried out with a the ABSciex 5500 QTrap tandem mass spectrometry instrument. The IonSpray voltage was set at 5500 V and the temperature was set at 200 °C. The CUR, GAS1, GAS2, and CAD were set at 20, 40, 50, and medium, respectively. The EP and CXP were set at 10 and 15 V, respectively, for positive mode and −10 and −15 V, respectively, for negative mode. Likewise, the DP, CE, Q1, and Q3 were optimized and set individually for each analyte and ISTD.</p><p><br></p>	<p><strong>UHPLC-MS/MS:</strong></p><p>The controlling software for the sample analysis was <strong>Analyst 1.7.2 (Applied Biosystems/MDS Analytical Technologies, Foster City, CA)</strong>. Data analysis was done using <strong>MultiQuant 3.0.3 (Applied Biosystems/MDS Analytical Technologies, Foster City, CA)</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NMR:</strong></p><p>All FID's (free induction decays) were zero-filled to 250 K data points. The singlet produced by the DSS methyl groups was used as an internal standard for chemical shift referencing (set to 0 ppm) and for quantification all 1H-NMR spectra were processed and analyzed using an <strong>in-house version of the MAGMET automated analysis software package using a custom metabolite library</strong>. <strong>MAGMET</strong> allows for qualitative and quantitative analysis of an NMR spectrum by automatically fitting spectral signatures from an internal database to the spectrum.</p>	<p>All metabolite concentrations for both the <strong>NMR</strong> and <strong>LC-MS/MS analysis</strong> were recorded by <strong>TMIC</strong> as well as their <strong>limit of detection (LOD)</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>UHPLC-MS/MS:</strong></p><p>Stool was weighed before and after lyophilization to quantify total water content prior to analysis. Analyte concentrations were determined using a standard approach to absolute quantification, using isotope-labeled internal standards to correct for technical variation and then assessing the result against a calibration curve of known concentrations of standard mixtures. Metabolite levels (μmol) were normalized to dry/lyophilizeed stool weight (g) and analyzed using the ratio (μmol/g).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>NMR:</strong></p><p>Each spectrum was further inspected by an NMR spectroscopist to minimize compound misidentification and misquantification. Typically, all of the visible peaks were assigned. The term “targeted” is used when spectra are profiled against a known metabolite library, which is the case for our NMR assay. Regarding the assignment of all visible peaks, this applies to when, within this targeted assay, very low abundant peaks are not visible for manual assignment and are therefore not reported (as those are hard to quantify via a targeted method). Most of the visible peaks are annotated with a compound name. Background subtraction methods were not used, as blanks were checked properly before analyzing the samples. It has been previously shown that this fitting procedure provides absolute concentration accuracy of 90% or better<strong>[1]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Ravanbakhsh S, Liu P, Bjorndahl TC, Mandal R, Grant JR, Wilson M, Eisner R, Sinelnikov I, Hu X, Luchinat C, Greiner R, Wishart DS.

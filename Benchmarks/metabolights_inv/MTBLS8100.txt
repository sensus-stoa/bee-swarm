Study Title	The Effect of Dietary BIoactives on Gut Microbiome DivErsity (DIME) - A pilot study (Untargeted metabolomics)
Study Description	<p>The gut microbiome refers to the complex community of microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, archaea and viruses) present within our gut. The composition and complexity of gut microbiota communities are crucial for maintaining human health. Humans depend on the gut microbiota to undertake essential metabolic functions and for effective immune system operations, such as protection against pathogens and maintaining gut integrity. Richer diversity in these microbiota communities has been associated with better health. A key modulator of intestinal microbial colonies is food. Dietary bioactives, also referred to as phytochemicals (non-nutrient food components) present in fruit and vegetables, can modulate metabolic processes, promoting better health; however, the interaction of bioactives with microbial communities is not yet fully understood. We have conducted a 2x2 randomised crossover study to estimate the effect of diets rich in dietary bioactives on gut microbial diversity and health markers in 20 healthy participants. Participants consumed a diet of high bioactive (HB) rich foods (containing polyphenols, Sulphur (S) metabolites and carotenoids) vs a low bioactive (LB) diet for 2 wk, with an in-between washout period of 4 wk. The primary aim was to examine the effect of a 2-wk diet high in bioactives rich foods on gut microbial diversity compared to a low bioactive diet. The secondary aim was to assess a high vs low bioactive diet on markers of cardiovascular health, inflammation and post-prandial glucose. We hypothesized that consuming a high bioactive diet would increase microbial diversity compared to a low bioactive diet.</p><p><strong>METHODS:</strong> Each volunteer completed 4 study visit days, 1 at the start and 1 at the end of each intervention phase. Dietary intake, including bioactives, was recorded through the Libro app linked to Nutritics. We recorded diets during a 7-d period (baseline habitual diet) followed by a 14-d intervention period per arm. On each study day, volunteers provided a 24 h urine sample collected in the 24 h preceding their visit and a stool sample. Urine and stool samples were utilized for both untargeted and targeted metabolomics. Microbial composition was assessed through whole-genome shotgun sequencing carried out by GeneWiz, performed using Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform with paired-end read length 2 x 125 bp. For vascular health, total HDL/LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, along with inflammatory markers such as hs-CRP, were analyzed with an at-home finger-prick blood sample collection kit (Medichecks). Participants also carried out an oral glucose tolerance test; glucose measurements were provided through the Freestyle Libre continuous glucose monitor (Abbott Laboratories) over a 2 h period following ingestion of 113 mL of polycal (75 g anhydrous glucose).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Untargeted metabolomics</strong> is reported in the current study <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS8100' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MTBLS8100</strong></a>.</p><p><strong>Targeted metabolomics</strong> is reported in <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS7290' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MTBLS7290</strong></a>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Linked cross omic data sets:</strong></p><p>Whole genome sequencing data associated with this study are available in the <strong>European Nucleotide Archive (ENA)</strong>: accession number<strong> </strong><a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/browser/view/PRJEB55607' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>PRJEB55607</strong></a>.</p><p>Metagenomic data associated with this study are available from <strong>MGnify</strong>: accession number <a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metagenomics/studies/MGYS00006076' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MGYS00006076</strong></a>.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>The primary aim of the <strong>DIME study</strong> was to test the hypothesis that a <strong>2-wk high bioactive diet</strong> compared to a <strong>2-wk low bioactive diet</strong> increases microbiome diversity. The secondary aims were: (1) to determine gut diversity and microbiome composition, (2) to determine levels of microbial and host metabolites (3) to determine the levels of inflammatory cardiovascular markers and glycaemic response. The study was a randomised <strong>2 x 2 cross-over study</strong>, where participants consumed a <strong>diet high in bioactive rich foods</strong> <strong>for 2 wk</strong> and, following a <strong>4 wk washout period</strong>, a <strong>diet low in bioactive rich foods for 2 wk</strong> (<strong>Figure 1</strong> in the paper associated with this study). Levels of dietary fibre were kept constant at 30 g. The high bioactive arm consumed polyphenols, Sulphur-S containing metabolites and carotenoids (<strong>Table 1</strong> in the paper associated with this study). The protocol was approved by the Human Research Governance Committee of the Quadram Institute Bioscience (<strong>HRGC QIB</strong>) in December 15th 2020. The North West – Greater Manchester West&nbsp;Research Ethics Committee (<strong>REC: 20/NW/0359</strong>) and the NHS Health Research Authority (<strong>HRA</strong>) gave full ethical approval. The trial was registered on a publicly accessible database (<strong>ClinicalTrials.gov, ISRCTN14510401</strong>).</p><p><br></p><p>In total, <strong>20 healthy adults aged 18-60</strong>, with a body mass index (BMI) between 18.5 and 35 kg/m^2 were recruited and randomised to the order in which they received the 2 dietary intervention phases (diet 'high' bioactive and diet 'low' bioactive). Block randomisation (1 block of 12 and 1 block of 13) was carried out to ensure that the diets were roughly balanced with respect to calendar dates, which otherwise could have confounded the effects of the diet. Written consent from participants for undertaking the study and tissue banking of samples was obtained. Before the dietary intervention, participants completed a 1-wk “run-in” period during which participants recorded their habitual diet and activity using the <strong>Libro app</strong> and fitness tracker. Each volunteer completed <strong>4 study visit days</strong>, 1 at the start and 1 at the end of each intervention phase.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p>On each study day (Study<strong> Visit 1, Visit 2, Mid-point sample, Visit 3 and Visit 4</strong>), volunteers provided a 24 h<strong> urine</strong> and <strong>stool samples</strong> collected in the 24 h preceding their visit, completed oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) by drinking 113 mL of Polycal and then collecting the CGM readings over a 2 h period, had anthropometric data recorded (these were self-reported using calibrated study equipment alongside data collected at the clinical research facility (CRF)) and provided fasted blood samples for full blood count and HbA1c. Urine samples were collected in a 3 L universal container containing 1 g ascorbic acid. Following collection and mixing, the sample was weighed, and <strong>urine</strong> was divided into <strong>1 mL aliquots</strong> and frozen at -80 °C until required for analysis. <strong>Stool samples</strong> were aliquoted into <strong>200 mg</strong> and <strong>diluted 1:1 in water</strong>, so 200 µL of MiliQ sterile water were added and the samples were vortexed to obtain a slurry consistency, before storing them at -80 °C until further processing.</p>	<p><strong>Urine</strong></p><p>The urine samples collected from each sample visit from the DIME study were processed separately at different times. Urine samples were thawed and centrifuged at 3000 x g for 2 min at 4 °C. Briefly, each filtered supernatant was then diluted 5x times by adding solvent (aqueous 5% 30:70 (v/v) acetonitrile (ACN)/methanol (MeOH)) and randomized into 2x 96-well microtiter plate (Waters, Hedehusene, Denmark), where samples from the same subject were kept on the same plate and each sample was measured once by profiling.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Feces</strong></p><p>Faecal samples were heated to 36.5 °C and homogenized by ultrasonic processing before sample preparation. Fecal samples global profiling was performed using a single-phase extraction protocol. From each sample, 50 mg fecal slurry (feces: Milli-Q water 1:1 v/v) was mixed with 1492.5 µL of 96% EtOH. The mixture was heated to 50 °C for 30 min under shaking in Eppendorf tubes with a closed lid, then centrifuged at 3000 x g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants from each sample were transferred to a well in a 96well plate and evaporated to dryness in a rotary evaporator. The residue was dissolved in 200uL 0.1% formic acid with 5% 30:70 (v/v) ACN/MeOH under vigorous shaking for 15 min, then the whole plate with samples was centrifuged at 3000 x g for 15 min, before placing it into the autosampler for injection in the LC-MS system.</p><p><br></p><p>An <strong>internal standard</strong> mixture was added to all samples, including isotopically-labelled standards of arginine, adenine, glycocholic acid, hippuric acid, tryptophan and uric acid. The solution of mixed metabolomics and internal standards were prepared in 20% aqueous ethanol and used in a final concentration of 10 µg/mL. Mixed standards, assay blanks and a pooled quality control (QC, formed by mixing 10 uL of all samples) were injected at the beginning of the batch and after every 10 samples. In addition, an in-house pool of stools or urine from ∼15 healthy subjects was used as a QC to allow comparison between studies (<strong>MetNexs pool</strong>).</p>	<p>Analysis was performed using a <strong>Waters ACQUITY UPLC system</strong> coupled to an <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 VanGuard Pre-column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm x 5 mm; Waters) </strong><span style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>and an</span><strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'> ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm x 100 mm, 100 Å pore size; Waters) </strong><span style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>column,</span> at a temperature of 50 °C for reversed-phase chromatographic separation. In total, 5 µL of each sample were injected from a 96-well microtiter plates (Waters) into the system, with a 10 min elution gradient composed of 4 mobile phases, mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in Milli-Q water), mobile phase B (methanol), mobile phase C (10% 1 M ammonium acetate in methanol (v/v)) and mobile phase D (isopropanol).</p>	<p>Data acquisition was conducted with a <strong>Waters SYNAPT G2 HDMS System</strong>, using electrospray ionization in both positive (+) and negative (-) mode, with a scan time of 0.08 s. Centroided data was generated in real-time with a <strong>mass range of 50 to 1000 Da</strong>. The temperatures of the ion source and desolvation gas (nitrogen) were 120 °C and 400 °C, respectively. The flow rates of cone gas and desolvation gas were set to 50 and 1000 L/h, respectively, with a capillary voltage of 3.5 kV for positive mode and 2.8 kV for negative mode. The sampling cone voltage was set as 30 kV for both polarities. Leucine encephalin was infused intermittently every 10 s as a lock mass for correcting the online mass calibration.</p>	<p>Both the urinary and faecal data from each study in each mode were preprocessed separately by Bioconductor package <strong>XCMS for R (v 3.6.3)</strong>. The preprocessing aimed at obtaining a table of features where the term 'feature' refers to an ion with a unique m/z and retention time (RT), measured by the UHPLC-MS instrument. The preprocessing in <strong>XCMS </strong>included the following steps: <strong>(1) </strong>peak picking using the <strong>centWave algorithm </strong>that is suitable for centroid acquisition data in case of partially overlapping features; <strong>(2)</strong> grouping using the <strong>PeakDensity </strong>method, based on the distribution of identified peaks along the RT within slices of overlapping m/z ranges;<strong> (3)</strong> peak alignment using the <strong>peak groups</strong> method, based on the alignment of features present in most of the samples; <strong>(4) </strong>repeat<strong> peak grouping</strong>, with a change of some default parameters; and<strong> (5)</strong> gap filling using the <strong>fillChromPeaks </strong>method to estimate the intensity of missing features. </p><p><br></p><p>Lists of different features were obtained after performing <strong>XCMS </strong>for each polarity acquisition mode. Before statistical data analysis, irrelevant features were removed from each feature list belonging to pilot studies, which <strong>(1)</strong> were present in blank samples,<strong> (2)</strong> eluted before 0.3 min and after 6.5 min, <strong>(3)</strong> were only present in 30% of samples in each subgroup based on a '70% rule', <strong>(4)</strong> were potential duplicates or isotopes annotated by <strong>CAMERA </strong>package, and <strong>(5)</strong> showed a coefficient of variance higher than 0.7 in the quality control samples. Intrabatch correction for reducing the effect of analytical drift was performed on each cleaned dataset using the<a href='https://github.com/tuhulab/libra' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong> 'libra' package</strong></a> in <strong>R</strong>. All of the features in the DIME study were kept, and an additional inter batch correction was performed on each sample list using the same package. List of statistically significant features were obtained by through an <strong>ANOVA</strong>, following multiple testing corrections.</p>	<p>Full-scan MS spectra were investigated to identify precursors and their relative isotopes, fragments, adducts and dimers. The precursors were first matched with an <strong>in-house standard database</strong>, then fragmented by DDA and targeted MS/MS acquisition using UHPLC-MS/MS (VION, Waters). Structure elucidation of discriminative biomarkers was performed by matching their precursors and their corresponding MS/MS fragmentation patterns with the help of online databases and informatics tools such as <a href='http://foodb.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>FooDB</strong></a>, <a href='https://hmdb.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>HMDB</strong></a>, <a href='https://metlin.scripps.edu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>Metlin</strong></a>, <a href='https://gnps.ucsd.edu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>GNPS </strong></a>and <a href='https:// www.mzcloud.org/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>mzCloud </strong></a>. A search for putative markers was then additionally performed in <a href='http://phenol-explorer.eu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>Phenol-explorer</strong></a> and <a href='https://www.genome.jp/kegg/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>KEGG</strong> </a>to clarify potential metabolic pathways.&nbsp;</p>

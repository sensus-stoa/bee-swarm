Study Identifier	MTBLS8122
Study Title	Suboxic DOM is bioavailable to surface prokaryotes in a simulated overturn of an oxygen minimum zone, Devil’s Hole, Bermuda
Study Description	Oxygen minimum zones (OMZs) are expanding due to increased sea surface temperatures, subsequent increased oxygen demand through respiration, reduced oxygen solubility, and thermal stratification driven in part by anthropogenic climate change. Devil's Hole, Bermuda is a model ecosystem to study OMZ microbial biogeochemistry because the formation and subsequent overturn of the suboxic zone occur annually. During thermally driven stratification, suboxic conditions develop, with organic matter and nutrients accumulating at depth. In this study, the bioavailability of the accumulated dissolved organic carbon (DOC) and the microbial community response to reoxygenation of suboxic waters was assessed using a simulated overturn experiment. The surface inoculated prokaryotic community responded to the deep (formerly suboxic) 0.2 μm filtrate with cell densities increasing 2.5-fold over 6 days while removing 5 μmol L<sup>-1</sup> of DOC. After 12 days, the surface community began to shift, and DOC quality became less diagenetically altered along with an increase in SAR202, a Chloroflexi that can degrade recalcitrant dissolved organic matter (DOM). Labile DOC production after 12 days coincided with an increase of <i>Nitrosopumilales,</i> a chemoautotrophic ammonia oxidizing archaea (AOA) that converts ammonia to nitrite based on the ammonia monooxygenase (<i>amoA</i>) gene copy number and nutrient data. In comparison, the inoculation of the deep anaerobic prokaryotic community into surface 0.2 μm filtrate demonstrated a die-off of 25.5% of the initial inoculum community followed by a 1.5-fold increase in cell densities over 6 days. Within 2 days, the prokaryotic community shifted from a <i>Chlorobiales</i> dominated assemblage to a surface-like heterotrophic community devoid of <i>Chlorobiales</i>. The DOM quality changed to less diagenetically altered material and coincided with an increase in the ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase form I (<i>cbbL</i>) gene number followed by an influx of labile DOM. Upon reoxygenation, the deep DOM that accumulated under suboxic conditions is bioavailable to surface prokaryotes that utilize the accumulated DOC initially before switching to a community that can both produce labile DOM via chemoautotrophy and degrade the more recalcitrant DOM.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Samples were gathered biweekly aboard the R/V Sea Dance at the Devil’s Hole site in Harrington Sound, Bermuda (32°19.399’ N 64°43.08’ W) between May and October 2018. Seawater was collected from the Devil’s Hole site at oxic surface (1 m) and suboxic deep (23-24 m) water on August 9th, 2018, using a 10 L Niskin bottle. 3 experimental treatments were prepared (see <strong>Table 1</strong> in the paper associated with this study) by diluting whole (unfiltered) seawater by 70% with 0.2 μm seawater filtrate<strong>[1]</strong> from different depths. The experiment was carried out over 21 d with samples for organic metabolies taken from the oxic surface and suboxic deep seawater collection on day 0 and collected using a positive pressure sampling system on day 6 and day 21. The experimental seawater treatment (1 L) was filtered through an Omnipore PTFE filter (pore size 0.2 µm, dia. 47 mm, Millipore, Burlington MA, USA) housed in a perfluoroalkoxy (PFA) filter holder for the analyses of organic metabolites via ultra-high resolution mass spectrometry at day 0, 6 and 21. The filtrate (1 L) collected into a PFA bottle for organic metaboilites was acidified with 1 ml ultrapure HCl (Optima, 35%, Thermofisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to pH ~3, stored at 4 °C and extracted within 24 h for mass spectrometry analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Carlson CA and Ducklow HW. (1996). Growth of bacterioplankton and consumption of dissolved organic carbon in the Sargasso Sea. Aquat. Microb. Ecol. 10, 69–85. doi:10.3354/ame010069.</p>	<p>Within 48 h at the Bermuda Institute of Ocean Sciences, metabolites were eluted from the thawed cartridges into combusted, borosilicate test tubes using 100% methanol (Optima grade). The eluents were transferred into combusted amber 8 ml vials and nearly dried using a vacuum centrifuge. Samples were then shipped to the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI) for further analysis. At WHOI, acidified 0.2 μm filtered seawater samples were passed through Bond Elut PPL cartridges (1 g/6 ml; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, United States) to extract extracellular DOM. Extraction followed the previously published protocol<strong>[1]</strong> with modifications described previously<strong>[2]</strong>. Extracted DOM samples were analyzed in negative ion mode using untargeted mass spectrometry methods as described previously<strong>[3]</strong>. Samples were reconstituted in 200 μl of 95:5 (v/v) Milli-Q (MQ, Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) water:acetonitrile with a deuterated standard mix added as an internal control[3] (see <strong>Table S2</strong> in the paper associated with this study), vortexed, and prepared for untargeted metabolomics analyses. Samples were further diluted (1:200) with the reconstitution solvent. Separate pooled samples (technical replicates) were made for each analysis to assess instrument drift over the course of the run and for downstream sample processing.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Dittmar T, Koch B, Hertkorn N, and Kattner G. (2008). A simple and efficient method for the solid-phase extraction of dissolved organic matter (SPE-DOM) from seawater. Limnol. Oceanogr. Methods 6, 230–235. doi:10.4319/lom.2008.6.230.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Longnecker K. (2015). Dissolved organic matter in newly formed sea ice and surface seawater. Geochim. Cosmochim. Acta 171, 39–49. doi: 10.1016/j.gca.2015.08.014.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Weber L, Armenteros M, Kido Soule M, Longnecker K, Kujawinski EB, Apprill A. Extracellular Reef Metabolites Across the Protected Jardines de la Reina, Cuba Reef System. Front Mar Sci 2020; 7: 582161. doi:10.3389/fmars.2020.582161.</p><p><br></p>	<p>MS data were collected in the Orbitrap analyzer with a mass resolution of 120,000 FWHM at m/z 200. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was 4e^5, the maximum injection time was 50 s, and the scan range was 100-1000 m/z. Data-dependent MS/MS data were acquired in the Orbitrap analyzer using higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy of 35%. The AGC target value for fragmentation spectra was 5e^4 and the intensity threshold was 2e^4. Cycle time was set at 0.6 s. Precursor selection was performed within the quadrupole with a 1 m/z isolation window. All data were collected in profile mode. Pooled samples were run after every 6 samples to assess instrument performance and drift over the sample run and samples were run in a random order.</p>	<p>For the untargeted metabolomics analyses, raw files containing <strong>MS1</strong> and <strong>MS/MS</strong> data were converted into <strong>mzML</strong> files using <strong>msconvert</strong> and processed using <strong>XCMS[1]</strong>. Ion modes were analyzed separately. Before processing with <strong>XCMS</strong>, the <strong>R</strong> package <strong>Autotuner[2]</strong> was used to find <strong>XCMS</strong> processing parameters appropriate for the data. In <strong>XCMS</strong>, the <strong>CentWave</strong> algorithm picked peaks using a gaussian fit. The specific parameters for peak picking for both ion modes were: noise = 10000, peak-width = 3-15, ppm = 15, prefilter = c(2,168.600), integrate = 2, mzdiff = -0.005, snthresh = 10. Obiwarp was used to adjust retention times and this step was followed by correspondence analysis. For statistical analyses, <strong>MS1</strong> features (defined as unique pairings of mass-to-charge (m/z) values with retention times) in both ion modes were culled following <strong>XCMS</strong> if they: <strong>1)</strong> had &gt;1 average fold change in the MQ blanks compared to the other samples, <strong>2)</strong> occurred in less than 20% of samples (excluding pooled controls), and/or <strong>3)</strong> were invariant (relative standard deviation of &lt;15%) across the samples (adapted from<strong>[3]</strong>). This feature-removal strategy retained 84% (4725) of features ionized in negative mode.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Smith CA, Want EJ, O'Maille G, Abagyan R, Siuzdak G. XCMS: processing mass spectrometry data for metabolite profiling using nonlinear peak alignment, matching, and identification. Anal Chem. 2006 Feb 1;78(3):779-87. doi:10.1021/ac051437y. PMID:16448051.</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> McLean C, Kujawinski EB. AutoTuner: High Fidelity and Robust Parameter Selection for Metabolomics Data Processing. Anal Chem. 2020 Apr 21;92(8):5724-5732. doi:10.1021/acs.analchem.9b04804. PMID:32212641.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Xia J, Broadhurst DI, Wilson M, Wishart DS. Translational biomarker discovery in clinical metabolomics: an introductory tutorial. Metabolomics. 2013 Apr;9(2):280-299. doi:10.1007/s11306-012-0482-9. PMID:23543913.</p>	<p>Samples prepared for untargeted metabolomics were analyzed with a ultra-high performance liquid chromatography system (Vanquish UHPLC, Thermo Scientific) coupled to an ultra-high resolution mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribid, Thermo Scientific). A Waters Acquity HSS T3 column (2.1 x 100 mm, 1.8 μm) equipped with a Vanguard pre-column was used for chromatographic separation at 40 °C for targeted and untargeted analyses. Sample order was randomized and the pooled sample was analyzed after every 6 samples. The column was eluted at 0.5 ml/min with a combination of solvents: <strong>A)</strong> 0.1% formic acid in water and <strong>B)</strong> 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The chromatographic gradient was as follows: 1% B (1 min), 15% B (1-3 min), 50% B (3-6 min), 95% B (6-9 min), 95% B (10 min). The column was washed and re-equilibrated with 1% B (2 min) between injections. Individual autosampler injections (5 μl each) were taken for negative ion mode analyses. The electrospray voltage was set to 2600 V for negative mode. Settings for source gases were 55 (sheath) and 20 (auxiliary) and these settings are presented in arbitrary units. The heated capillary temperature was 375 °C and the vaporizer temperature was 400 °C. Samples were run in negative ion mode.</p>	<p>The <strong>mzRT</strong> features were grouped into compound classes based on the elemental formulas and the calculated aromaticity index<strong>[1]</strong>. The following groups were defined: black carbon, carbohydrates, condensed hydrocarbons, highly unsaturated compounds, lignin, lipids, peptides, proteins, protein maya, polyphenols, unsaturated aliphatic compounds, sugars, saturated fatty acids, and carboxyl-rich alicyclic molecules (CRAM)<strong>[2]</strong>. From the molecular formula assignments, the magnitude-averaged O/C, H/C, and double bond equivalent (DBE) values for each sample were calculated for each assigned molecular formula based on the relative magnitude of each peak<strong>[3]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Koch BP and Dittmar T. (2016). From mass to structure: an aromaticity index for high-resolution mass data of natural organic matter. Rapid Commun. Mass Spectrom. 30:250. doi:10.1002/rcm.7433</p><p><strong>[2]</strong> Martínez-Pérez AM, Osterholz H, Nieto-Cid M, Álvarez M, Dittmar T, and Álvarez-Salgado XA. (2017), Molecular composition of dissolved organic matter in the Mediterranean Sea. Limnol. Oceanogr., 62: 2699-2712. doi:10.1002/lno.10600.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Sleighter RL, and Hatcher PG. (2008). Molecular characterization of dissolved organic matter (DOM) along a river to ocean transect of the lower Chesapeake Bay by ultrahigh resolution electrospray ionization Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry. Marine Chemistry 110, 140-152. doi:10.1016/j.marchem.2008.04.008.</p>

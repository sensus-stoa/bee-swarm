Study Identifier	MTBLS8263
Study Title	Dual transcriptomic & metabolomic analysis of elicited flax sheds light on the kinetics of immune defense activation against the biotrophic pathogen <i>Oidium lini</i>.
Study Description	Flax (<i>Linum usitatissimum</i>) grown under controlled conditions displayed genotype-dependent resistance to powdery mildew (<i>Oidium lini</i>) following COS-OGA (comprising chitosan- and pectin-derived oligomers) elicitor application. The present study reveals a two-step immune response in plants preventively challenged with the elicitor: an initial, rapid response characterized by the transcription of defense genes whose protein products act in contact with or within the cell wall, where biotrophic pathogens initially thrive, followed by a prolonged activation of cell wall peroxidases and accumulation of secondary metabolites. Thus, dozens of genes encoding membrane receptors, pathogenesis-related proteins, and wall peroxidases were initially overexpressed. Repeated COS-OGA treatments had a transient effect on the transcriptome response while cumulatively remodeling the metabolome over time, with a minimum of two applications required for maximal metabolomic shifts. Secondary metabolites, in particular terpenoids and phenylpropanoids, emerged as major components of this secondary defense response alongside pathogenesis-related proteins and wall peroxidases. The sustained accumulation of secondary metabolites, even after cessation of elicitation, contrasted with the short-lived transcriptomic response. Wall peroxidase enzyme activity also exhibited cumulative effects, increasing strongly for weeks after a third elicitor treatment. This underscores the plasticity of the plant immune response in the face of a potential infection, and the need for repeated preventive applications to achieve the full protective potential of the elicitor.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Flax seeds (L. usitatissimum) from the fiber varieties Elixir (Terre de Lin, France) were disinfected with bleach (3.5%, 15°) and rinsed three times with 0.0025% Tween 20. In total 9 seeds were sown per 10 cm square pot in perlite substrate and grown in a growth chamber at 24 °C with a 16/8 h (day/night) regime under cool white fluorescent lamps.</p><p><br></p><p>Plants, 2-week-old, were sprayed 3x times (7, 3 and 1 day before powdery mildew infection) with either 12.5 mL/pot water (control) or 62.5 ppm COS-OGA (0.5% v/v FytoSave, FytoFend, Belgium). The morning of each treatment, plants were transferred into a growth cabinet (MD-1400, Snijders, The Netherlands) and maintained at 24 °C and 85% relative humidity.</p><p><br></p><p>For the transcriptomic study, 2-week-old flax plants were sprayed once, twice or three times with either 12.5 mL water or 62.5 ppm COS-OGA (0.5% v/v FytoSave, FytoFend, Belgium) per pot. The morning of each treatment, plants were transferred into a growth cabinet (MD-1400, Snijders, The Netherlands) and maintained at 24 °C and 85% relative humidity.</p><p><br></p><p>For the study of the cumulative and persistence effect of COS-OGA sprayings, biological material was harvested 1 day after 1-3 sprayings, and 7 and 14 days after the third spraying, respectively. Material collection was made by cutting stems 1 cm above cotyledons, snap freezing the aerial parts (all stems and leaves from one pot pooled together), crushing in liquid nitrogen with pasteurized mortar and pestle, and storing at -80 °C until use.</p>	<p>Extraction of metabolites was performed as described[1] on 8 replicates for each experimental condition.</p><p><br></p><p>Briefly, homogenized plant material stored at -80 °C was accurately weighed to transfer 200-300 mg of frozen powder into 1.5 mL microtubes, to which 3 volumes of methanol:formic acid (99.875:0.125,&nbsp;v/v) were added. The samples were then vortexed thoroughly, sonicated at 45 kHz for 15 min at room temperature (ELMA TRANSONIC TI-H 10 MF2) and then sonicated a second time at 25 kHz for 15 min. The samples were centrifuged twice at 16,000 x&nbsp;g at 4 °C. Finally, 150 µL were transferred to HPLC vials and sealed.</p><p><br></p><p>[1] De Vos RC, Moco S, Lommen A, Keurentjes JJ, Bino RJ, Hall RD. Untargeted large-scale plant metabolomics using liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry. Nat Protoc. 2007;2(4):778-91. doi:10.1038/nprot.2007.95. PMID:17446877.</p>	<p>Samples (2 µL) were analyzed as described[1] on a high-performance liquid chromatography (1290 Infinity II, Agilent) equipped with a C18 column (Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 Rapid Resolution; 2.1 x 50 mm; 1.8 µm; Agilent). A QC sample (mix of all samples) was prepared and analyzed several times during the HPLC run to assess the technical reproducibility.</p><p><br></p><p>Samples were separated using acetronitrile with 0.1% formic acid (eluent A) and 0.1% formic acid in water (eluent B). The flow was set at 0.2 mL/min and the following gradient was used at 40 °C (min: [%&nbsp;B]): (0:95); (2:95); (27:65); (34:5); (35:5); (37:95); (42:95).</p><p><br></p><p>[1] De Vos RC, Moco S, Lommen A, Keurentjes JJ, Bino RJ, Hall RD. Untargeted large-scale plant metabolomics using liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry. Nat Protoc. 2007;2(4):778-91. doi:10.1038/nprot.2007.95. PMID:17446877.</p>	<p>The detection was carried out using a Quadrupole-Time-Of-Flight (G6545B Q-TOF, Agilent) operated in negative mode, acquiring spectra over a mass range from 100 to 3200 m/z. The nebulized eluates were dried at 190 °C under a flow of 12&nbsp;L/min N2. The capillary voltage was set at 3.5&nbsp;kV and the nozzle voltage at 1.5&nbsp;kV.&nbsp;The accuracy of the equipment was ensured by the continuous addition of standards throughout the runs, which allowed for constant recalibration of the mass spectrometer.</p>	<p>Profinder 10.0.2 (Agilent) and statistical analyses were made using Mass Profiler Professional 15.1 (Agilent). Data were transformed (log2), then successively filtered for occurrence through replicates, variability across QC samples, statistical relevance (Oneway ANOVA; p-value cut-off: 0.05; p-value computation: Asymptotic; Multiple Testing Correction: Benjamini-Hochberg) and fold change (cut-off: 2.0 against negative control). The PCA was determined based on the selected subset of features using the same software and the confidence of ellipses was 95%.</p>	<p>Metabolites were not identified. Instead, the Mummichog algorithm was used, for which raw LC-MS data were converted from the original Agilent (.d) format to an open-source format (.mzML) using MSConvert (ProteoWizard[1]), then analyzed using MZmine 3[2].</p><p><br></p><p>Extracted features were then analyzed using the Functional Analysis module of the MetaboAnalyst suite where the data were filtered (RSD &lt;25%; 20% IQR) and transformed (log10) before Mummichog analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>[1] Chambers MC, Maclean B, Burke R, Amodei D, Ruderman DL, Neumann S, Gatto L, Fischer B, Pratt B, Egertson J, Hoff K, Kessner D, Tasman N, Shulman N, Frewen B, Baker TA, Brusniak MY, Paulse C, Creasy D, Flashner L, Kani K, Moulding C, Seymour SL, Nuwaysir LM, Lefebvre B, Kuhlmann F, Roark J, Rainer P, Detlev S, Hemenway T, Huhmer A, Langridge J, Connolly B, Chadick T, Holly K, Eckels J, Deutsch EW, Moritz RL, Katz JE, Agus DB, MacCoss M, Tabb DL, Mallick P. A cross-platform toolkit for mass spectrometry and proteomics. Nat Biotechnol. 2012 Oct;30(10):918-20. doi:10.1038/nbt.2377. PMID:23051804.</p><p>[2] Schmid R, Heuckeroth S, Korf A, Smirnov A, Myers O, Dyrlund TS, Bushuiev R, Murray KJ, Hoffmann N, Lu M, Sarvepalli A, Zhang Z, Fleischauer M, Dührkop K, Wesner M, Hoogstra SJ, Rudt E, Mokshyna O, Brungs C, Ponomarov K, Mutabdzija L, Damiani T, Pudney CJ, Earll M, Helmer PO, Fallon TR, Schulze T, Rivas-Ubach A, Bilbao A, Richter H, Nothias LF, Wang M, Oresuc M, Weng JK, Böcker S, Jeibmann A, Hayen H, Karst U, Dorrestein PC, Petras D, Du X, Pluskal T.

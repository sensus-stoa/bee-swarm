Study Identifier	MTBLS8370
Study Title	Synergistic metabolism of salivary MUC5B in oral commensal bacteria during early biofilm formation
Study Description	<p>Bacterial metabolism in oral biofilms is comprised of complex networks of nutritional chains and biochemical regulations. These processes involve both intraspecies and interspecies networks as well as interactions with components from host saliva, gingival crevicular fluid, and dietary intake. In a previous paper, a large salivary glycoprotein, mucin MUC5B, was suggested to promote a dental health-related phenotype in the oral type strain of <em>Streptococcus gordonii</em> DL1, by regulating bacterial adhesion and protein expression. In this study, nuclear magnetic resonance-based metabolomics was used to examine the effects on the metabolic output of monospecies compared to dual species early biofilms of two clinical strains of oral commensal bacteria, <em>S. gordonii</em> and <em>Actinomyces naeslundii</em>, in the presence of MUC5B. The presence of <em>S. gordonii</em> increased colonization of <em>A. naeslundii</em> on salivary MUC5B, and both commensals were able to utilize MUC5B as a sole nutrient source during early biofilm formation. The metabolomes suggested that the bacteria were able to release mucin carbohydrates from oligosaccharide side chains as well as amino acids from the protein core. Synergistic effects were also seen in the dual species biofilm metabolome compared to the monospecies, indicating that <em>A. naeslundii</em> and <em>S. gordonii</em> cooperated in the degradation of salivary MUC5B. A better understanding of bacterial interactions and salivary-mediated regulation of early dental biofilm activity is meaningful for understanding oral biofilm physiology and may contribute to the development of future prevention strategies for biofilm-induced oral disease.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p><strong><em>S. gordonii </em>CW </strong>and <strong>A. <em>naeslundii </em>CW</strong> cultures from blood agar were inoculated in 25% Todd-Hewitt Yeast Extract (1/4 THYE, Becton Dickinson) and grown overnight in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The next day, the cultures were washed by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 5 °C, 10 min, 50 mL tubes, Beckman GS-6R centrifuge) and resuspended in sterile <strong>PBS</strong> pH 7.5 to an <strong>OD600nm of 0.5</strong> (corresponding to similar cell counts between the 2 species, 1.5 x 10^8 CFU/mL for <em>S. gordonii</em> CW and 1 x 10^8 CFU/mL for <em>A. naeslundii</em> CW). In total, 12 well-plates and Ibidi VI 0.1 μ-slides (Ibidi GmbH, Munich, Germany) were pre-coated with <strong>MUC5B-conditioning solution</strong> with 0.1% w/v CaCl2 (final concentration), diluted in PBS 1:5 and incubated at room temperature overnight. Biofilm formation was initiated by <strong>inoculation of the PBS OD600nm = 0.5 bacterial suspensions</strong> (separately for <strong>monospecies biofilms</strong> or <strong>1:1 v/v of each culture for dual species</strong>, to give a final volume of 2 mL in 12-well plates and 100 μL in Ibidi channels, per biofilm) to the mucin-coated wells and incubated in 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 2 h to allow the cells to adhere. After the <strong>adhesion phase</strong>, buffer supernatant and non-adherent cells were removed by gentle rinsing with PBS and replaced with <strong>25% MUC5B in PBS</strong>, <strong>25%</strong> low density salivary protein (<strong>LDP</strong>) <strong>in PBS</strong> or <strong>PBS</strong> and incubated for 2 h as described above.</p><p><br></p><p>After incubation, the supernatants were collected, centrifuged for 5 min at 5,000 rpm, 4°C (Heraeus Fresco 17 centrifuge, Thermo Scientific), and moved to fresh tubes to remove suspended cells. Samples were stored at −20°C until shipment to the <strong>Swedish NMR Centre</strong> in Gothenburg for NMR analysis.</p>	<p>No extraction was performed, with bacterial supernatants obtained after centrifugation being used directly for analysis.</p>	<p>Frozen samples were thawed on ice and a total volume of <strong>300 μL of each bacterial supernatant</strong> was transferred manually to a deepwell plate (Porvair cat nr 219030), prefilled with <strong>300 μL buffer</strong> (75 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.4, 0.08% w/v TSP-d4, 0.1% w/v sodium azide, 20% v/v deuterium oxide) in each well. After sealing with a silicone lid, the plate was shaken at 500 rpm, 12 °C for 5 min on a Thermomixer Comfort (Eppendorf). A SamplePro Tube L (Bruker Biospin) liquid handler was used to transfer 575 μL of each mixed sample to 5 mm SampleJet NMR tubes (Bruker Biospin).</p>	<p>NMR data was acquired from 5 biologically independent replicates on a <strong>Bruker Avance NEO 600 MHz</strong> spectrometer equipped with a <strong>5 mm QCI cryoprobe</strong> and a cooled SampleJet sample changer. Sample racks were kept at 6 °C during sample preparation as well as in the spectrometer sample changer up until measurement. </p>	<p>The standard pulse sequence '<strong>zgespe</strong>' was used to acquire <strong>1D 1H data</strong>. The experiment encompassed a perfect echo sequence with excitation sculpting for water suppression. A total of 64 scans were collected into 64K data points with a spectral width of 11904 Hz, using an acquisition time of 2.692 s, a relaxation delay of 4 s and 8 dummy scans. The receiver gain was set to a fixed value of 101 and acquisition was done at 25 °C.</p>	<p>The acquired data was zero-filled twice and an exponential line broadening of 0.3 Hz added before Fourier transform and subsequent automated phasing and baseline correction. Spectra were referenced to the TSP-d4 signal at 0 ppm. Data acquisition and processing were performed in <strong>TopSpin 4.1.4 (Bruker BioSpin)</strong>.</p>	<p>The NMR data was imported into <strong>Matlab (MATLAB version: 9.13.0 (R2022b), Natick, Massachusetts: The MathWorks Inc.; 2022) </strong>using the function <strong>rbnmr[1</strong>]. The <strong>TSP-d4</strong> peak was aligned between all spectra using the function<strong> ico_shift[2]</strong> and set to 0 ppm. The data was then imported to <strong>R</strong> using the package <strong>R.matlab</strong>. Corresponding metadata was imported using the package <strong>readx1</strong>. The data was processed with the package <strong>speaq[3]</strong>. Peaks were picked with the <strong>getWaveletPeaks</strong> function with <strong>baselineThresh = 0, SNR.Th = 10</strong> and <strong>include_nearbyPeaks = TRUE</strong>. Peaks were grouped with the<strong> PeakGrouper</strong> function with <strong>min.samp.grp = 10</strong> and <strong>grouping.window.width = 200</strong>. The <strong>SilhouetR </strong>function was run and groups with a value less than 0.5 were listed to be regrouped in a second run. The <strong>PeakFilling</strong> function was run filling in missing values with <strong>max.index.shift = 200</strong>. The output list was manually curated while it was annotated leaving 27 peaks for analysis of the <strong>MUC5B</strong> samples. Metabolite signal annotation was performed in <span style='font-style: normal; color: rgb(54, 54, 54); font-weight: 700;'>ChenomX 9.0 (ChenomX Inc.)</span>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Nyberg N. 2023.

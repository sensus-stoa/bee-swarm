Study Identifier	MTBLS8819
Study Title	The microbiome interacts with the circadian clock and dietary composition to regulate metabolite cycling in the gut
Study Description	Gut microbiomes play a key role in the maintenance of host metabolic homeostasis and health. An important aspect of metabolism is its circadian regulation, but how the microbiome regulates metabolite cycling under different conditions, including variations in dietary composition, remains undefined. In this study, we utilized high temporal resolution metabolite profiling of the Drosophila gut to investigate the role of the microbiome in metabolite cycling. We find that, in contrast to its effect on transcript cycling, the microbiome increases metabolite cycling in the gut. In the absence of a circadian clock, the microbiome has minimal effect on the number of metabolites cycling, but profoundly affects phase, suggesting that the clock stabilizes circadian phase in the face of microbial changes. Interestingly, the impact of the microbiome is also reduced in a timed feeding paradigm, and vice versa, suggesting that timed feeding and the microbiome have similar circadian effects. Finally, the microbiome increases metabolite cycling but does not alter phase in flies fed high protein diets, but suppresses metabolite cycling and changes cycling phase in flies fed high sugar diets. Enrichment analysis of cycling metabolites found that amino acid metabolism is particularly sensitive to changes in the microbiome, the circadian clock and timed feeding. Collectively, our observations highlight a key role of the microbiome in producing robust metabolite cycling, in conjunction with the circadian clock and dietary composition.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Diets preparation followed the protocols below. <strong>Normal chow diets</strong>:&nbsp;64.67g of corn meal, 27.1g of dry yeast, 8g of agar,&nbsp;and 61.6ml of molasses in 1L ddH2O. For <strong>high protein diets</strong>: 64.67g of corn meal, 150g of dry yeast, 8g of agar, and 61.6ml of molasses in 1L of ddH2O. For <strong>high sugar diets</strong>: 64.67g of corn meal, 27.1g of dry yeast, 8g of agar, 61.6ml of molasses, and 243g of sucrose in 1L of ddH2O. After autoclaving the diets at high temperature, temperature was allowed to drop to 40°C and then 10.17ml of 20% Tegosept was added to each 1L diet and mixed thoroughly. Sterile conditions were maintained throughout. Microbiome-containing and sterile female flies were separately fed on these diets for 10 days. Fly guts were dissected at <strong>ZT0, ZT6, ZT12, and ZT18</strong> time points in a 12:12 LD cycle, with 4 repeat samples collected for microbiome-containing and sterile female flies at each time point (&gt;200 guts per sample, which corresponds to at least 10 mg for each sample). All experimental processes were carried out on ice, and samples quickly transferred to dry ice for storage after each sample was collected.</p>	<p><strong>Primary metabolites:</strong></p><p>Samples were extracted using the Matyash extraction buffer which includes MTBE, MeOH, and H2O. The organic (upper) phase was dried down and submitted for resuspension and injection onto the LC while the aqueous (bottom) phase was dried down and submitted to derivatization for GC. 10 ul of Methoxamine in pyridine was added to the aqueous phase, after which it was shaken at 30&nbsp;C&nbsp;for 1.5 hours.&nbsp;Then 91 uL of MSTFA + FAMEs was added to each sample and shaking was continued at 37&nbsp;C&nbsp;for 0.5 hours to finish derivatization. Samples were then vialed, capped, and injected onto the instrument.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Lipids:</strong></p><p>Samples were extracted using the Matyash extraction procedure, which includes MTBE, MeOH, and H2O. The organic (upper) phase was dried down and submitted for resuspension and injection onto the LC while the aqueous (bottom) phase was dried down and submitted to derivatization for GC. It was resuspended with 110 uL of a solution of 9:1 methanol: toluene and 50 ng/mL CUDA. This was then shaken for 20 seconds, sonicated for 5 minutes at room temperature, and then centrifuged for 2 minutes at 16100 rcf. The samples were then aliquoted into three parts. 33 uL were aliquoted into each of two vials with a 50 uL glass insert for positive and negative mode lipidomics. The last part was aliquoted into an eppendorf tube to be used as a pool.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Biogenic Amines:</strong></p><p>Sample extraction for biogenic amines is a Liquid-Liquid extraction using the Matyash extraction containing MTBE, Methanol and Water and creating a biphasic partition. The polar phase is then dried down to completeness and run on a Waters Premier Acquity BEH Amide column.&nbsp;</p>	<p><strong>Primary metabolites:</strong></p><p>We used a 7890A GC. 0.5 uL of derivatized sample is injected using a splitless method onto a Rtx-5Sil MS (0.25 µm, 0.25 mm x 30 m; Restek) column&nbsp;with an Intergra-Guard at 275C with a helium flow of 1 mL/min. The GC oven is set to hold at 50 °C&nbsp;for 1 min then ramped at 20 °C/min to 330 °C and held for 5 min. The transferline is set to 280 °C.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Lipids:</strong></p><p>The samples are then loaded up on an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC, using a ACQUITY UPLC CSH C18 (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm x 100 mm; Waters) column, maintained at 65 °C. Mobile&nbsp;phase&nbsp;A was:&nbsp;60:40&nbsp;acetonitrile:water&nbsp;+&nbsp;10&nbsp;mM&nbsp;ammonium&nbsp;formate&nbsp;+&nbsp;0.1%&nbsp;formic&nbsp;acid,&nbsp;and mobile&nbsp;phase&nbsp;B was:&nbsp;90:10&nbsp;v/v&nbsp;isopropanol:acetonitrile&nbsp;+&nbsp;10&nbsp;mM&nbsp;ammonium&nbsp;formate&nbsp;+&nbsp;0.1%&nbsp;formic&nbsp;acid.&nbsp;The gradient was as follows:0&nbsp;min&nbsp;15%&nbsp;(B),&nbsp;0–2&nbsp;min&nbsp;30%&nbsp;(B),&nbsp;2–2.5&nbsp;min&nbsp;48%&nbsp;(B),&nbsp;2.5–11&nbsp;min&nbsp;82%&nbsp;(B),&nbsp;11–11.5&nbsp;min&nbsp;99%&nbsp;(B),&nbsp;11.5–12&nbsp;min&nbsp;99%&nbsp;(B),&nbsp;12–12.1&nbsp;min&nbsp;15%&nbsp;(B),&nbsp;12.1–15&nbsp;min&nbsp;15%&nbsp;(B), with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, injection&nbsp;volume of 3&nbsp;µL&nbsp;and injection&nbsp;temperature of&nbsp;4 °C.</p><p><br></p><p>The other sample aliquot was run in negative mode, which was also run on an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC and the same ACQUITY UPLC CSH C18 (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm x 100 mm; Waters) column, using the same gradient.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Biogenic Amines:</strong></p><p>An Agilent 1290 Infinity LC and an ACQUITY UPLC Premier BEH Amide (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm x 50 mm; Waters) column maintained at 45 °C were used. Mobile&nbsp;phase&nbsp;A was:&nbsp;Ultrapure&nbsp;water&nbsp;with&nbsp;10&nbsp;mM&nbsp;ammonium&nbsp;formate&nbsp;+&nbsp;0.125%&nbsp;formic&nbsp;acid,&nbsp;pH&nbsp;3, and mobile&nbsp;phase&nbsp;B was:&nbsp;95:5&nbsp;v/v&nbsp;acetonitrile:ultrapure&nbsp;water&nbsp;w/&nbsp;10&nbsp;mM&nbsp;ammonium&nbsp;formate&nbsp;+&nbsp;0.125%&nbsp;formic&nbsp;acid,&nbsp;pH&nbsp;3.&nbsp;The gradient was as follows:&nbsp;0&nbsp;min,&nbsp;100%&nbsp;B;&nbsp;0.5&nbsp;min,&nbsp;100%&nbsp;B;&nbsp;1.95&nbsp;min,&nbsp;70%&nbsp;B;&nbsp;2.55&nbsp;min,&nbsp;30%&nbsp;B;&nbsp;3.15&nbsp;min,&nbsp;100%&nbsp;B;&nbsp;3.8&nbsp;min,&nbsp;100%&nbsp;B, with a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min and an injection&nbsp;volume of 1-5&nbsp;µL.</p>	<p><strong>Primary metabolites:</strong></p><p>A <strong>LECO Pegasus IV</strong> TOF instrument was used, with the <strong>EI </strong>ion source set to 250 °C. The Mass spec parameters collect data from 85m/z to 500m/z at an acquisition rate of 17 spectra/sec.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p><strong>Lipids:</strong></p><p>The <strong>positive mode</strong> was run on an <strong>Agilent 6530</strong> with a scan range of <strong>m/z 120-1200 Da</strong> with an acquisition speed of 2 spectra/s. The sample aliquot run in negative mode, was analyzed using an <strong>Agilent 6546 QTOF&nbsp;mass spec</strong>. The acquisition rate was 2 spectra/s with a scan range of <strong>m/z 60-1200&nbsp;Da</strong>. The mass resolution for the Agilent 6530 is 10,000 for ESI (+) and 30,000 for ESI (-) for the Agilent 6546.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Biogenic Amines:</strong></p><p>A <strong>Sciex Triple-ToF&nbsp;</strong>scans from 50-1500 m/z&nbsp;with MS/MS collection from 40-1000 selecting from the top 5 ions per cycle.</p>	<p><strong>Primary metabolism:</strong></p><p>Raw data files are preprocessed directly after data acquisition and stored as ChromaTOF-specific *.peg&nbsp;files, as generic *.txt result files and additionally as generic ANDI MS *.cdf files.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Lipidomics:</strong></p><p>The raw data are processed in an untargeted (qualitative) manner by Agilent’s software MassHunter Qual to find peaks in up to 300 chromatograms.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Biogenic amines:</strong></p><p>Raw data files are processed using an updated version of MS-DIAL software which identifies and aligns peaks and then annotates peaks using both an in-house mzRT library and MS/MS spectral matching with NIST/MoNA libraries.</p>	<p><strong>Primary metabolism:</strong></p><p>Raw data files are preprocessed directly after data acquisition and stored as ChromaTOF-specific *.peg&nbsp;files, as generic *.txt result files and additionally as generic ANDI MS *.cdf files. ChromaTOF vs. 2.32 is&nbsp;used for data preprocessing without smoothing, 3 s peak width, baseline subtraction just above the&nbsp;noise level, and automatic mass spectral deconvolution and peak detection at signal/noise levels of 5:1&nbsp;throughout the chromatogram. Apex masses are reported for use in the BinBase algorithm. Result *.txt&nbsp;files are exported to a data server with absolute spectra&nbsp;intensities and further processed by a filtering&nbsp;algorithm implemented in the metabolomics BinBase database.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Lipidomics:</strong></p><p>Data are analyzed in a four-stage process. First, raw data are processed in an untargeted (qualitative) manner by Agilent’s software MassHunter Qual to find peaks in up to 300 chromatograms. Peak features are then imported into MassProfilerProfessional for peak alignments to seek which peaks are present in multiple chromatograms, using exclusion criteria by the minimum percentage of chromatograms in which these peaks are positively detected. We usually use 30% as minimum criterion. In a tedious manual process, these peaks are then collated and constrained into a MassHunter quantification method on the accurate mass precursor ion level, using the MS/MS information and the LipidBlast library to identify lipids with manual confirmation of adduct ions and spectral scoring accuracy. MassHunter enables back-filling of quantifications for peaks that were missed in the primary peak finding process, hence yielding data sets without missing values.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Biogenic amines:</strong></p><p>Chromatograms first undergo a quality control check in which internal standards are examined for consistency of peak height and retention time. Raw data files are then processed using an updated version of MS-DIAL software which identifies and aligns peaks and then annotates peaks using both an in-house mzRT library and MS/MS spectral matching with NIST/MoNA libraries. All MS/MS annotations are then manually curated by a lab member to ensure that only high-quality compound identifications are included in the final report.</p>

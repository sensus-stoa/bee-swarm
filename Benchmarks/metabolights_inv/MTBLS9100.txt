Study Title	Gut microbiota-derived butyric acid protected against secondary brain injury after SAH by inhibiting neuronal pyroptosis and regulating gut microbiota composition (Murine assays)
Study Description	<p><strong>BACKGROUND:</strong> Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a devastating cerebrovascular disease with a poor prognosis. Accumulating studies have reported that gut microbiota contributes to the pathophysiology of several central nervous system diseases. However, whether SAH can change gut microbiota composition and affected gut microbiota is involved in the development of secondary brain injury following SAH remains unclear.</p><p><strong>METHODS:</strong> A retrospective case-control study was performed in unruptured intracranial aneurysm (UIA, CTL) and ruptured intracranial aneurysm (SAH) patients. Patients' fecal and serum samples were collected and sequenced by metagenome and metabolome. The experimental SAH mice models were established, and their feces and serum were also analyzed by 16S rRNA and metabolic sequencing. Fecal microbiota from SAH patients and mice was transplanted to ABX mice to investigate the relationship between the gut microbiota and secondary brain injury following SAH.</p><p><strong>RESULTS:</strong> SAH seriously influenced gut microbiota composition and significantly increased the relative abundance of the genus Escherichia. SAH also considerably reduced the production of gut microbiota-derived butyric acid. Furthermore, SAH-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis aggravated brain injury and cognitive deficits in ABX mice by promoting inflammatory response. Sodium butyrate (NaB) administration protected against secondary brain injury following SAH by inhibiting pyroptosis-related neuroinflammation, which is mediated by NLRP1/Caspase-1/GSDMD and Caspase-3/GSDME signaling pathways. NaB drinking pretreatment also alleviated secondary brain injury following SAH by increasing the relative abundance of butyric acid-producing bacteria, such as Dubosiella and Faecalibaculum, and decreasing the Escherichia abundance.</p><p><strong>CONCLUSION:</strong> SAH contributed to gut microbiota dysbiosis and changed gut microbiota exacerbated secondary brain injury after SAH. Supplement with gut microbiota-derived butyric acid protected against brain injury following SAH by inhibiting NLRP1/Caspase-1/GSDMD and Caspase-3/GSDME-mediated neuronal pyroptosis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Linked studies:</strong></p><p><strong>UPLC-MS/MS&nbsp;assays</strong>&nbsp;of&nbsp;murine samples are reported in&nbsp;this study.</p><p><strong>UPLC-MS/MS</strong>&nbsp;<strong>assays</strong>&nbsp;of&nbsp;human samples are reported in&nbsp;<a href='https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS9087' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>MTBLS9087</strong></a>.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Male wild-type C57BL/6J mice (8-10 wk old) were purchased from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University. All mice were acclimated to a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle with rodent food and autoclaved water. The experimental <strong>SAH</strong> model was performed as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. Animals were fixed on the stereotaxic apparatus after the general anesthesia. Then, a burr hole was drilled at 4.5 mm anterior to the bregma in the midline. A 27-gauge needle was inserted at an angle of 40° in the sagittal plane into the suprachiasmatic cistern and injected 60 μL non-heparinized arterial blood from a donor male C57BL/6J mouse for 10 s by a syringe pump. After injection, the needle was kept for 5 min to prevent blood backflow or CSF leakage. The burr hole was then sealed immediately with bone wax. The sham <strong>controls </strong>performed the same procedure and injected 60 μL normal saline (NS).</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Colon fecal samples</strong> were collected from <strong>control</strong> and&nbsp;experimental <strong>SAH</strong> mice<strong> 24 h</strong> after the injection. Samples were&nbsp;immediately&nbsp;frozen at -80 °C for further analysis.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref: </strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Yuan, B., Zhou, X. M., You, Z. Q., Xu, W. D., Fan, J. M., Chen, S. J., Han, Y. L., Wu, Q., Zhang, X. 2020. Inhibition of AIM2 inflammasome activation alleviates GSDMD-induced pyroptosis in early brain injury after subarachnoid haemorrhage. Cell Death Dis. 2020 Jan 30;11(1):76. doi:10.1038/s41419-020-2248-z. PMID: 32001670. PMCID: PMC6992766.</p>	<p>Fecal samples were thawed on ice and the metabolites were extracted from 100 mg of each sample using <strong>120 μL </strong>of precooled <strong>50% methanol</strong> buffer. After vortexing for 1 min, the mixture of metabolites was incubated for 10 min at RT and then stored at -20 °C overnight. After this, samples were centrifugated at 4,000 x g for 20 min and the supernatants were transferred to 96-well plates. The samples were stored at -80 °C prior to the LC-MS analysis. In addition, pooled quality control samples (<strong>QC</strong>) were also prepared by combining <strong>10 μL</strong> of each extraction mixture.</p>	<p>Metabolomics analyses were performed using a<strong> Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class PLUS System</strong> and <strong>ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm x 100 mm; Waters)</strong> column, with 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution as mobile phase A and 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile as mobile phase B. Injection volume was 1 μL.</p>	<p>Analysis was performed with a <strong>Waters Xevo G2-XS QTof</strong> high resolution mass spectrometer, which can collect primary and secondary mass spectrometry data in MSe mode under the control of the acquisition software (<strong>MassLynx V4.2, Waters</strong>). Mass range was <strong>50-1200 m/z.</strong> In each data acquisition cycle, dual-channel data acquisition can be performed on both low collision energy and high collision energy at the same time. The low collision energy is 2V, the high collision energy range is 10~40V, and the scanning frequency is 0.2 s for a mass spectrum. The parameters of the ESI ion source are as follows: Capillary voltage: 2000V (positive ion mode) or -1500V (negative ion mode); cone voltage: 30 V; ion source temperature: 150 °C; desolvation&nbsp;gas temperature 500 °C; backflush gas flow rate: 50 L/ h; desolvation&nbsp;gas flow rate: 800 L/h.</p>	<p>After normalizing the original peak area information with the total peak area, the follow-up analysis was performed. Principal component analysis and Spearman correlation analysis were used to judge the repeatability of the samples within group and the quality control samples. </p>	<p>The raw data collected using <strong>MassLynx V4.2 </strong>was processed by <strong>Progenesis QI </strong>software for peak extraction, peak alignment and other data processing operations, based on the <strong>Progenesis QI </strong>software online <a href='https://metlin.scripps.edu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>METLIN</strong></a> database and Biomark’s self-built library for identification, and at the same time, theoretical fragment identification and mass deviation all within 100ppm. The identified compounds are searched for classification and pathway information in<strong> </strong><a href='https://www.genome.jp/kegg/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>KEGG</strong></a>, <a href='https://hmdb.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>HMDB</strong></a> and <a href='https://www.lipidmaps.org/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>Lipidmaps</strong></a> databases.</p>

Study Identifier	MTBLS9394
Study Title	Geographic variation of mutagenic exposures in kidney cancer genomes
Study Description	<p>International differences in the incidence of many cancer types indicate the existence of carcinogen exposures that have not been identified by conventional epidemiology yet potentially make a substantial contribution to cancer burden1. This pertains to clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), for which obesity, hypertension, and tobacco smoking are risk factors but do not explain its geographical variation in incidence2. Some carcinogens generate somatic mutations and a complementary strategy for detecting past exposures is to sequence the genomes of cancers from populations with different incidence rates and infer underlying causes from differences in patterns of somatic mutations. Here, we sequenced 962 ccRCC from 11 countries of varying incidence. Somatic mutation profiles differed between countries. In Romania, Serbia and Thailand, mutational signatures likely caused by extracts of Aristolochia plants were present in most cases and rare elsewhere. In Japan, a mutational signature of unknown cause was found in &gt;70% cases and &lt;2% elsewhere. A further mutational signature of unknown cause was ubiquitous but exhibited higher mutation loads in countries with higher kidney cancer incidence rates (p-value &lt;6 × 10−18). Known signatures of tobacco smoking correlated with tobacco consumption, but no signature was associated with obesity or hypertension suggesting non-mutagenic mechanisms of action underlying these risk factors. The results indicate the existence of multiple, geographically variable, mutagenic exposures potentially affecting 10s of millions of people and illustrate the opportunities for new insights into cancer causation through large-scale global cancer genomics.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Linked cross omic data sets:</strong></p><p>Geographic variation of mutagenic exposures in kidney cancer genomes – patient metadata files (<strong>Mutographs</strong>) associated with this study are available in the <strong>European Genome-Phenome Archive</strong>: https://ega-archive.org/datasets/EGAD00001012223.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Our study population arises from the <strong>Mutographs</strong> project (a Cancer Research UK ‘Grand Challenge’ project). The International Agency for Research on Cancer (<strong>IARC/WHO</strong>) coordinated case recruitment through an international network of over 40 collaborators from the 11 participating countries (see <strong>Table 1 </strong>and <strong>Table S17</strong> of the paper associated with the study). The inclusion criteria for patients were &gt; =18 yr of age (ranging from 23 to 87, with a mean of 60 and a standard deviation of 12), confirmed diagnosis of primary cell renal cell carcinoma (<strong>ccRCC</strong>) and no prior cancer treatment. Informed consent was obtained for all participants. Patients were excluded if they had any condition that could interfere with their ability to provide informed consent or if there were no means of obtaining adequate tissues or associated data as per the protocol requirements. Ethical approvals were first obtained from each Local Research Ethics Committee and Federal Ethics Committee when applicable, as well as from the IARC Ethics Committee.</p><p><br></p><p>Dedicated standard operating procedures, following guidelines from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (<strong>ICGC</strong>), were designed by <strong>IARC/WHO</strong> to select appropriate case series with complete biological samples and exposure information as previously described<strong>[1] (Table S17</strong> of the paper associated with the study). Of the 962 subjects included in the main analysis, <strong>901 subjects</strong> were included in this sub-study, with all Japanese samples (n=36) as well as few cases from Czech Republic (n=13), Romania (n=5) and Russia (n=3), not being included due to lack of available <strong>plasma samples</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p>Whole genome sequencing data and patient metadata are deposited in the European Genome-phenome Archive (<strong>EGA</strong>) associated with study <strong>EGAS00001003542</strong>. Aligned BAM files for all ccRCC cases included in the final analysis were deposited in dataset<strong> EGAD00001012102</strong>, variant calling files in dataset <strong>EGAD00001012222</strong> and patient metadata in dataset <strong>EGAD00001012223</strong>.&nbsp;</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Brennan P, Davey-Smith G. Identifying Novel Causes of Cancers to Enhance Cancer Prevention: New Strategies Are Needed. J Natl Cancer Inst. 2022 Mar 8;114(3):353-360. doi:10.1093/jnci/djab204. PMID:34743211.</p>	<p>Untargeted metabolomics of <strong>901 plasma samples </strong>was performed at the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC/WHO, Lyon, France), with details of the sample analysis and preprocessing of the data as previously described<strong>[1]</strong>. Briefly, samples were thawed at room temperature and prepared by mixing <strong>30 μL of plasma</strong> with <strong>200 μL of cold acetonitrile</strong> and filtering the precipitate with 0.2 μm Captiva ND plates (Agilent Technologies). A 100 μL aliquot of the filtrate was then mixed with equal volume of ultrapure water in a well plate that then sealed (Rapid EPS, BioChromato, Fujisawa, Japan), frozen at -80 °C, and thawed at room temperature before analysis.</p><p><br></p><p>Quality control (<strong>QC</strong>) samples were prepared from a plasma pool that was made by mixing small aliquots of 100 randomly selected study samples and processed together with the study samples. <strong>Blank samples</strong> were also prepared along the plasma samples in an identical manner, only leaving the plasma out of the process. Samples were analyzed as 2 independent analytical batches both consisting of 5 96-well plates. Samples were randomized across the batches. Each well plate included <strong>4 </strong>individually prepared <strong>QCs</strong> and <strong>2 blanks</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Ref:</strong></p><p><strong>[1] </strong>Loftfield E, Stepien M, Viallon V, Trijsburg L, Rothwell JA, Robinot N, Biessy C, Bergdahl IA, Bodén S, Schulze MB, Bergman M, Weiderpass E, Schmidt JA, Zamora-Ros R, Nøst TH, Sandanger TM, Sonestedt E, Ohlsson B, Katzke V, Kaaks R, Ricceri F, Tjønneland A, Dahm CC, Sánchez MJ, Trichopoulou A, Tumino R, Chirlaque MD, Masala G, Ardanaz E, Vermeulen R, Brennan P, Albanes D, Weinstein SJ, Scalbert A, Freedman ND, Gunter MJ, Jenab M, Sinha R, Keski-Rahkonen P, Ferrari P. Novel Biomarkers of Habitual Alcohol Intake and Associations With Risk of Pancreatic and Liver Cancers and Liver Disease Mortality. J Natl Cancer Inst. 2021 Nov 2;113(11):1542-1550. doi:10.1093/jnci/djab078. PMCID:&nbsp;PMC8562969. PMID:&nbsp;34010397.</p>	<p>Analysis was performed with an <strong>Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC</strong> and an <strong>ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm x 100 mm; Waters)</strong> column maintained at 45 °C. The mobile phase consisted of ultrapure water and LC-MS grade methanol (MeOH), both with 0.05 % (v/v) of formic acid. Following gradient profile was used: 0 min, 5% MeOH; 6 min, 100% MeOH; 6-10.5 min, 100% MeOH; 13 min, 5% MeOH. Mobile phase flow rate was 0.4 mL/min. Samples were kept at 4°C and 2 μL was injected.</p><p><br></p><p><br></p>	<p>An <strong>Agilent 6550 iFunnel Q-TOF</strong> mass spectrometer equipped with Jet Stream electrospray ionization source was used. Mass spectrometer drying gas temperature was 175 °C and flow 12 L/min, with capillary, nozzle and fragmentor voltages of 3500 V, 300 V, and 175 V, respectively. The sheath gas temperature was 350 °C and flow 11 L/min, and nebulizer pressure 45 psi. Continuous mass axis calibration was employed using lock mass ions m/z 121.0509 and 922.0098 from <strong>Agilent’s ESI-L Tune Mix </strong>solution, providing a typical mass error of &lt;5 ppm. Data was acquired in a<strong> full-scan centroid</strong> format using an extended dynamic range mode providing a resolution of approx. 22,000 at m/z 322, and acquisition rate of 1.67 Hz, over the mass range of <strong>50-1000 Da</strong> (<strong>MassHunter Acquisition 10.1, Agilent Technologies</strong>).</p><p><br></p><p><br></p>	<p>Pre-processing was performed using <strong>Profinder 10.0.2.162</strong> and <strong>Mass Profiler Professional B.14.9.1 software (Agilent Technologies)</strong>. A 'Batch recursive feature extraction (small molecules)' process was employed for samples and blanks to find<strong> [M+H]+</strong> ions. The 2 batches were processes separately and the resulting features were aligned in <strong>Mass Profiler Professional</strong>. Chromatographic peak areas were used as a measurement of intensity. No normalization or transformation of raw data was performed prior to the downstream data analysis.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><p>A total of 2392 features were detectable in at least one of the 901 samples. Features present in only 1 of the 2 batches were filtered out. Recursive filtering elimination was applied to decrease redundancy from highly correlated variables (r ≥ 0.85, Pearson's r calculated before any transformation/imputation) by selecting the features with least missing data within clusters of features. A total of 944 features were included in the statistical analysis. Features were pre-processed: missing values were replaced with 1/5 of the minimal value of the feature before applying mean centering and Pareto scaling. Each feature was regressed against both de novo and COSMIC signatures, adjusting for sex and age of diagnosis, as well as body mass index (BMI) and technical factors (batch, acquisition order) that could impact chromatographic peak area. Models for SBS22a and SBS22b were restricted to Romanian and Serbian samples to find potential pathways of Aristolochic acid exposure in the Balkan region. Logistic models were used for zero-inflated signatures (≥ 30% zeros) while quasi-Poisson regressions were used for the least zero-inflated signatures (SBS1, SBS40a, and SBS40b). To derive specific false detection rates, random variables were created from permutations of the initial features and regressed against signatures in the same fashion as true features. Maximum p-value thresholds from regressions with random features were compared to adjusted p-value thresholds according to Bonferroni’s procedure. The more conservative approach was used in selecting features of interest. Random forest models were also used as cross-checking multivariate models to assess the relative importance of each feature in explaining the signature attribution. As with univariate models, regression models were used for the least zero-inflated signatures (&lt; 30% of zeros) while classification models were used for all other signatures, with restriction to Romanian and Serbian samples for SBS22a and SBS22b. Importance was estimated from the total decrease in node impurities from splitting on the variable, averaged over all trees. Node impurity was measured by the Gini index for classification, and by residual sum of squares for regression. The significance of importance metrics for Random Forest models were estimated by permuting the response variable (<a href='https://github.com/EricArcher/rfPermute' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'>https://github.com/EricArcher/rfPermute</a>).</p>	<p>Features considered for identification, along with their highly correlated counterparts, were searched in <strong>Human Metabolome Database (</strong><a href='https://hmdb.ca/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>HMDB</strong></a><strong>)</strong>, <a href='https://www.lipidmaps.org/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>LipidMaps</strong></a>, <a href='https://metlin.scripps.edu/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>Metlin</strong></a> and <a href='https://www.genome.jp/kegg/' rel='noopener noreferrer' target='_blank'><strong>KEGG</strong></a>. Compound identity was confirmed by comparison of retention times and using targeted <strong style='color: rgb(54, 54, 54);'>MS/MS</strong> analysis, instead of full scan data acquisition, against chemical standards when available, or otherwise against reference <strong>MS/MS</strong> spectra. 3 collision energies were used (10, 20, 40 eV) and data was acquired in centroided format. Chromatograms and spectra were obtained from representative study samples, isotope patterns were retrieved via “<strong>Find Compounds by Formula</strong>” searches in <strong>Agilent MassHunter Qualitative Analysis B.06.00 SP1</strong> and are shown in <strong>Fig S24</strong> of the paper associated to this study.</p><p><br></p><p>Since the feature 240.1468@0.8929933 was strongly correlated with several features identified as TMAP (<strong>Table S11 </strong>of the paper associated to this study), the integration of these features was inspected and corrected manually, and regressed against SBS40b using the same model applied to features selected for analysis. Creatinine was identified among the features by matching its retention time and MS/MS spectra against a reference standard and also regressed against SBS40b in the same fashion as other metabolites. Estimation of correlation between metabolic features was done using linear regression adjusting for batch and acquisition order.</p>

Study Title	Chronic inorganic nitrate supplementation does not improve metabolic health and worsens disease progression in mice with diet-induced obesity.
Study Description	Inorganic nitrate (NO3-) has been proposed to be of therapeutic use as a dietary supplement in obesity and related conditions including the Metabolic Syndrome (MetS), type-II diabetes and metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Administration of NO3- to endothelial nitric oxide synthase-deficient mice reversed aspects of MetS, however the impact of NO3- supplementation in diet-induced obesity is not well understood. Here we investigated the whole-body metabolic phenotype and cardiac and hepatic metabolism in mice fed a high-fat high-sucrose (HFHS) diet for up to 12-months of age, supplemented with 1 mM NaNO3 (or NaCl) in their drinking water. HFHS-feeding was associated with a progressive obesogenic and diabetogenic phenotype, which was not ameliorated by NO3-. Furthermore, HFHS-fed mice supplemented with NO3- showed elevated levels of cardiac fibrosis, and accelerated progression of MASLD including development of hepatocellular carcinoma in comparison with NaCl-supplemented mice. NO3- did not enhance mitochondrial B-oxidation capacity in any tissue assayed and did not suppress hepatic lipid accumulation, suggesting it does not prevent lipotoxicity. We conclude that NO3- is ineffective in preventing the metabolic consequences of an obesogenic diet and may instead be detrimental to metabolic health against the background of HFHS-feeding. This is the first report of an unfavorable effect of long-term nitrate supplementation in the context of the metabolic challenges of overfeeding, warranting urgent further investigation into the mechanism of this interaction.

Study Protocol Description	<p>Following terminal anaesthetization of mice, the heart was rapidly excised, atria and extraneous tissue removed. The apex was removed and a transverse mid-section 1-2 mm above the apex was carefully sectioned for other analyses, before the remaining left ventricle was separated and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen for metabolomic analyses. The liver was removed from the abdominal cavity and the left lateral lobe (LLL) quickly divided into 3 equal sections with one third snap frozen for metabolomic analyses.</p>	<p>Aqueous and organic metabolites were extracted from ~25 mg liver LLL or cardiac left ventricle using a modified Bligh and Dyer method. Tissue was homogenised in 600 ul methanol/cholorform (2:1, 4C) using a tissue lyser and steel ball (QIAGEN; 2x 3 minutes at 20 Hz). Tissue homogenates were sonicated for 15 minutes before chloroform (200 ul) and water (HPLC-grade; 200 ul) was added to each sample. Samples were votexed thoroughly before centrifugation (13,300 rpm, 10 min). Aqueous and organic phases were carefully separated, and the extraction process repeated on the protein pellet to maximise the yield of recovered metabolites. Separated phases were dried under nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until analysis.</p><p><br></p>	<p>Analysis of acyl-carnitines in liver and heart was carried out using a mix of half of each of the aqueous and organic phases reconsitituted in methanol/water (4:1 v/v, 100 ul) containing an internal standard mix of eight deuterated carnitine species. Each sample (5 ul) was injected onto an ACE Excel-2 C18-PFP 5 um column (100 angstrom, 150 x 2.1 mm) conditioned at 40C using a Vanquish UHPLC+ series (Thermo Scientific). The mobile phase was comprised of solvent A: 0.1% formic acid solution, and solvent B: 0.1% formic acid/methanol solution. The mobile phase was pumpled at 450 ul/min with a total run time of 10 minutes. The gradient used was: </p><ul><li>99.5% A held for 1 min</li><li>Linear decrease to 0% A over 9 min</li><li>Held 0% A over 0 min</li><li>Return 99.5% A over 0.1 min</li></ul><p><br></p><p>Open-profiling lipidomics was carried out on the remaining organic phase. An internal standard mix containing 16 deuterated lipid standards was added to the organic phase metabolites before drying under nitrogen. The samples were then reconstituted in methanol/chlorofol (1:1 100 ul) and vortexed thoroughly. The sample (10 ul) was then diluted 1:20 in isopropanol/acetonitrin/water (2:1:1) and vortexed to ensure samples were thoroughly mixed. Each same (5 ul) was injected onto a C18 CSH column (2.1 x 100 mm, 1.7 um pore size; Waters) conditioned at 55C, with chromatography carried out using an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mobile phase was comprised of solvent A: acetonitrile/water (3:2), and solvent B: acetonitrile/isopropanol (1:9). Solvent additives were added depending on ionisation mode: 10 mM ammonium formate was added to mobile phases A and B in positive mode, and 10 mM ammonium acetate in negative mode. The mobile phase was pumpled at 400 ul/min with a total run time of 10 minutes. The gradient used was:</p><ul><li>Hold 60% A</li><li>Decrease to 57% A at 0.9 min</li><li>Decrease to 46% A at 4.8 min</li><li>Decrease to 30% A at 4.9 min</li><li>Decrease to 19% A at 5.8 min</li><li>Decrease to 1% A at 8 min</li><li>Hold 1% A for 0.5 min</li><li>Return to 60% A and hold for 1.5 min</li></ul><p><br></p>	<p>Analysis of acyl-carnitines was carried out using a TSQ Quantiva Triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Nitrogen at 48 mTorr, 420C, was used as a drying gas for solvent evaporation. An electrospray ionisation source was used, operated simultaneously in positive and negative ionisation modes, with electronspray voltages of 3.5 kV and 2.5 kV respectively. Detection of metabolites was carried out using XCalibr software (Thermo Scientific).</p><p><br></p><p>Open-profiling lipidomics was carried out using an LTQ Orbitrap Elite Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Mass spectrometry was carried out in both positive and negative modes, utilising an electrospray ionisation source. The source temperature was 420C and capillary temperature was 380C. Ionisation voltages were set to 3.5 kV in positive mode and 2.5 kV in negative mode. Lipid data was collected using the Fourier transform mass spectrometer analyzer set in profile mode with a resolution of 60,000. A full scan was performed across an m/z range of 110–2000.</p><p><br></p>	<p>All data were normalized to the intensity of appropriate internal standards and to sample protein concentration (Pierce BCA Assay, Thermo Scientific) to account for differences in sample size.</p>	<p>For acyl-carnitine species, data processing was carried out using Xcalibur software (v.2.2, Thermo Scientific) with metabolite identification determined using targeted analysis of parent/daughter ion <em>m/z</em> and retention times from chemical standards.</p><p><br></p><p>For lipid metabolites, raw spectra were converted to a .mxML file using MS Convert (Proteowizard) with peaks subsequently identified by XCMS software <strong>[1]</strong> based on an approximate chromatographic peak detection (FWHM) of 5 seconds and a signal to noise threshold ratio of 5. Peaks also had to be present in a inium of 25% of sample in each experimental run. Peaks were annotated by accurate mass, using an automated in–house R script and by comparison to the LipidMaps database <strong>[2]</strong>.</p><p><br></p><p><strong>Refs:</strong></p><p><strong>[1]</strong> Smith CA, Want EJ, O’Maille G, Abagyan R, Siuzdak G. XCMS: Processing Mass Spectrometry Data for Metabolite Profiling Using Nonlinear Peak Alignment, Matching, and Identification. Anal Chem 78: 779–787, 2006. doi: 10.1021/ac051437y.</p><p><strong>[3]</strong> Sud M, Fahy E, Cotter D, Brown A, Dennis EA, Glass CK, Merrill AH, Murphy RC, Raetz CRH, Russell DW, Subramaniam S. LMSD: LIPID MAPS structure database. Nucleic Acids Res 35: D527-532, 2007. doi: 10.1093/nar/gkl838.</p>

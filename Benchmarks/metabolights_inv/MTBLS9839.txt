Study Title	Assessment of phenotypic trait plasticity in the oilseed Camelina sativa using integrated early-stage abiotic stress and field studies
Study Description	<p>Connecting the characterisation of juvenile (pre-anthesis) plant stress responses undertaken in controlled environments to field agronomic performance is a challenge. The oilseed crop Camelina sativa (camelina), with its innate resilience and plasticity, presents an opportunity to understand both the underlying mechanisms of juvenile resilience and identify the implications for yield in diverse pedoclimates. A better understanding of camelina’s abiotic stress resilience is of exceptional importance in the context of climate change and the development of breeding programmes for climate-tolerant crops. In this study, 54 accessions representing the genetic diversity observed in the wider publicly available population, were utilised to investigate&nbsp;the plasticity of camelina’s early-stage response to drought and heat stress,&nbsp;combined with an evaluation of field performance in&nbsp;multi-location field trials.&nbsp;A combinatorial phenotyping approach (metabolic, biochemical, physiological and developmental) of early-stage drought and heat stress&nbsp;identified&nbsp;stress-responsive signatures within the diversity panel, with substantial variation in&nbsp;the morphophysiological&nbsp;line-specific responses to stress, indicating juvenile and mature camelina plants have significant plasticity and access different stress response strategies. In response to stress, we observed significant molecular metabolic adjustment (benzenoids, carbohydrates, phenylpropanoids and polyketides), alongside significant lipid remodelling and physiological compensation. Camelina was shown to be resilient to drought stress, and metabolites e.g., oleic and linolenic acids were identified as indicators of abiotic stress response.&nbsp;Applying an integrated approach, early-stage phenotyping and multi-location field trials, provided an integrated assessment of the camelina stress response and facilitated a connection to&nbsp;crop productivity.&nbsp;This approach&nbsp;facilitates improved&nbsp;breeding programmes, addresses the restrictions of limited genetic diversity in camelina and supports the development of local varieties optimised for climate resilience.</p>

Study Protocol Description	<p>Leaves were collected from camelina plants grown in glasshouse experiments under three environmental treatments: ambient temperature with well-watered conditions (20/18 °C day/night, 50% soil water content), ambient temperature with drought stress (20/18 °C day/night, drought imposed until 15–20% SWC), and high temperature with well-watered conditions (32/25 °C day/night, 50% SWC). Soil water content and BBCH developmental stage were monitored daily, while leaf length, width, and chlorophyll index (SPAD-502 Plus) were measured every two days. Stem width and leaf number were also recorded. Leaves were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground to a fine powder, and lyophilised for 72 hours. Lyophilised material was stored at −80 °C until extraction.</p><p><br></p><p>For untargeted metabolomics, 20 mg of lyophilised leaf powder per replicate were transferred into Micronic tubes, randomised on 96-well racks, capped using a robotised decapper, and stored at −80 °C.</p>	<p>Metabolites were extracted using a robotised high-throughput platform developed at the Bordeaux Metabolome Facility. Each rack of Micronic tubes was decapped and 300 µL of solvent A (80% ethanol, 0.1% formic acid, containing 250 µg/mL methyl vanillate as internal standard) was added. Racks were agitated on the robot for 30 seconds at 500 rpm, then sonicated for 15 minutes in ice-cold water. Samples were centrifuged for 5 minutes at 1,350 g, and 300 µL of the supernatant was transferred to clean tubes. A second extraction with 300 µL of solvent A was performed on the pellet, followed by a wash with 50% ethanol (solvent B). Supernatants were robotically filtered through 0.22 µm plates, and the filtrates were collected in fresh tubes. Quality Control (QC) were created by mixing 20 µL from each sample and standards. </p>	<p>Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a GEMINI C18 column (150 × 2 mm, 3 µm) and a SecurityGuard C18 pre-column (4 × 2 mm, 3 µm). The column was maintained at 35 °C, and the injection volume was 5 µL. The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (0.05% formic acid in water) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The gradient was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 3% B; 0.5–1 min, 3% B; 1–9 min, 50% B; 9–13 min, 100% B; 13–14 min, 100% B; 14–14.5 min, 3% B; 14.5–18 min, 3% B. The flow rate was set to 350 µL/min.</p>	<p>Mass spectrometric analysis was performed on an LTQ-Orbitrap Elite (Thermo Scientific) coupled to the UHPLC, using electrospray ionisation in negative mode. The instrument was controlled via XCalibur v3.0.63. The heater was set to 300 °C, sheath gas to 45, auxiliary gas to 15, sweep gas Flow Rate to 10, spray voltage to 2.5 kV, capillary temperature to 300 °C, and S-Lens RF level to 60%.MS2 data-dependent acquisition (DDA) was performed on all samples over a range of 50–1500 m/z at 30,000 resolution, with 2 Da isolation width, HCD activation, 60% normalized collision energy, and activation time of 0.1 ms. Besides, MS1 full scans were acquired on QC samples at a resolution of 240,000 over a range of 50–1500 m/z. Prior to the run, the mass spectrometer was calibrated using commercial Pierce ESI calibration solution.</p>	<p>Raw LCMS data were processed using MS-DIAL v 4.8 (Tsugawa et al. 2015), yielding 11 680 RT-m/z features. After data-curation (blank check, SN &gt; 10, CV QC &lt; 30%), we retained 3016 final features for further chemometrics, of which 220 matched with MS1 and MS2 (Level 2 ID), 1446 suggested features matched with MS1 only (Level 3 ID), and 1350 unknowns (no match). &nbsp;Retention time correction was also applied in MS-DIAL, and metabolomic signals were normalised by the QC samples injected every 12 samples. </p>	<p>MS-DIAL annotation of metabolic features was performed using the online library MSMS-Public-Neg-VS15.msp (36,848 records). Thus, putative annotation of differentially expressed metabolites resulted from MS-DIAL screening of the MS1 detected exact HR m/z and MS2 fragmentation&nbsp;patterns&nbsp;against&nbsp;multiple&nbsp;online&nbsp;databases (http://prime.psc.riken.jp/compms/msdial/main.html MSP). InChiKeys of annotated features were used in ClassyFire to provide an automated structural ontology of chemical entities.</p>
